## Fixed: unsupported time-to-effect, wait and dose figures

9 registry sentences for medicines without a Patient Guide state when something happens or how much to take, with no stored source statement. They are no longer shown as guidance; they stay in the "not shown as guidance (needs review)" list on the medicine's page. Source-backed label statements still show their figures, with the source beside them. A safety threshold inside a safety statement (for example a patch strength with a food warning) is not treated as an instruction.

- asenapine: The golden rule: nothing to eat or drink for 10 minutes after the tablet goes in, because it absorbs through the lining of your mouth, not your stomach.
- risperidone: Benefit builds over 1–3 weeks; do not stop because it hasn't worked immediately.
- aripiprazole: Expect benefit to build over 1–3 weeks.
- flumazenil: Flumazenil is the antidote to the benzodiazepine family of sedatives: given into a vein, it wakes a person from benzodiazepine sedation within about two minutes.
- zaleplon: Zaleplon is an ultra-short sleeping tablet: it helps you fall asleep again and is essentially gone from the body within about four hours, which makes it the one medicine of its kind that can occasionally be taken in the middle of the night without morning grogginess, provided at least four hours remain before you must drive.
- zopiclone: Zopiclone is a widely used sleeping tablet in India and the Commonwealth: it helps you fall asleep and stay asleep for about 5–6 hours.
- atomoxetine: Unlike stimulants it is not a controlled drug and cannot be misused, but it takes several weeks to show its full effect.
- pemoline: Benefit should be clear within 3 weeks of reaching the right dose, if not, the medicine is stopped.
- tacrine: Benefit may take up to 6 weeks to appear, and months for any stabilisation of the disease course.
