# Genetics in Psychiatry — From Mendel to the Molecular Maze

## 1. In one line + overview

**Psychiatric genetics runs on two engines: quantitative genetics (twin, family, adoption studies quantifying heritability, environment and their interplay) and molecular genetics (linkage, association and the whole-genome search for actual risk genes), and the field's central lesson is that psychiatric disorders are polygenic, multifactorial and entangled with gene-environment interplay.**

The Oxford chapters deliver the complete framework. **Quantitative genetics (2.4.1, Thapar & McGuffin)**: the patterns of inheritance, Mendel's laws with their vocabulary (gene, allele, genotype, phenotype); the single-gene disorder patterns (autosomal dominant: Huntington's and porphyria presenting psychiatrically; autosomal recessive with consanguinity: PKU; X-linked: fragile X with no father-to-son transmission); continuous traits and the Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium; the **polygenic/multifactorial threshold model** (liability normally distributed; affected beyond a threshold; relatives' liability distributions shifted right: the model explaining familial aggregation of common disorders); the variance partition (phenotypic = genetic + shared environmental + non-shared environmental; narrow- and broad-sense heritability); the critical interpretive cautions (population-not-individual; population-specific; heritability says nothing about modifiability: the PKU diet lesson); the non-additive layers (**gene-environment interaction**: genetic differences in response to environments; **gene-environment correlation**: passive, active, evocative, the sociable-parent example; epistasis); the research methods (family studies with ascertainment and age-correction; **twin studies** with the equal-environments assumption and its tests, zygosity by DNA typing, concordance rates and intraclass correlations; the four MZ/DZ model equations; **adoption studies** with their three designs: adoptee, adoptee's-family, cross-fostering; path analysis and model-fitting; the multivariate findings: same genes different environments for anxiety-depression; genetic influence on IQ increasing from childhood to adolescence); and the G×E demonstrations (Kendler's depression-risk sensitivity to life events; adoption-evidence for antisocial behaviour gene-environment interaction). **Molecular genetics (2.4.2, Flint)**: DNA-RNA structure and transcription; the genome's organisation (chromosomes, centromeres, telomeres; the "working copy written over countless drafts" metaphor); gene regulation (transcription factors and pleiotropy: the ATRX example; **DNA methylation and epigenetics**; **imprinting** with its neurodevelopment implications: maternal genes enhance and paternal genes reduce brain size; the imprinting disorders: Rett, Prader-Willi, Turner, Angelman; X-inactivation; the Skuse Turner-syndrome social-cognition work); **linkage** (co-segregation with markers; the recombination fraction; centimorgans; **lod scores**, the lod-of-3 convention and its 1000:1 odds; the model-specification and locus-heterogeneity problems: early-onset Alzheimer's with its three genes); **association studies** (allelic association more powerful for small effects; the candidate-gene versus whole-genome approaches; linkage disequilibrium and the HapMap); the molecular methods (polymerase chain reaction; microarrays; expression profiling) (and the honest modern conclusion: for common psychiatric disorders, linkage found few major loci, and the effect sizes of individual risk variants are small) the polygenic reality confirmed.

## 4. The core ideas

### 4.1 Quantitative genetics (2.4.1)

**Inheritance patterns.** Mendel's pea-plant experiments (published 1866, rediscovered ~1900): the particulate theory, heredity through discrete units (named **genes** in 1909; alternative forms **alleles**; the observed characteristic the **phenotype**; the genetic endowment the **genotype**). The laws: **dominance** (heterozygotes Aa display the dominant phenotype); **segregation** (intercrossing F1 heterozygotes produces recessive:dominant in 1:3, genotype proportions 1:2:1); **independent assortment** (traits transmitted independently when genes are on different chromosomes or far apart). The single-gene disorders of psychiatric relevance: **autosomal dominant** (one allele suffices, Huntington's disease and acute intermittent porphyria; every generation affected; heterozygote parent → 50% of offspring); **autosomal recessive** (two alleles needed: phenylketonuria; skips generations; carrier parents; commoner with **consanguinity**); **X-linked** (fragile X: carrier mother → half of sons affected, half of daughters carriers; affected father → all daughters carriers, no father-to-son transmission). **Continuous traits**: quantitative phenotypes (height, weight, blood pressure) also follow Mendelian principles; one locus with two alleles gives three phenotypic classes; adding loci multiplies classes (2n + 1), approaching the normal distribution: **polygenic inheritance**; with environmental contribution, **multifactorial**. The **Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium** (p²:2pq:q²) as the population genotype-frequency baseline.

**Complex disorders.** Most psychiatric disorders: neither Mendelian nor continuous, **quasi-continuous** (affected individuals gradeable along severity). The **polygenic/multifactorial threshold model**: a continuously distributed **liability**, with affection beyond a threshold; relatives of affected individuals carry liability distributions shifted right, so a greater proportion exceed the threshold, the elegant explanation of familial aggregation without Mendel. Alternative models: single-major-locus with **incomplete penetrance** (the probability of manifesting with a given genotype, between 0 and 1); **variable expression** (neurofibromatosis from café-au-lait spots to the full syndrome); **anticipation** (earlier onset, greater severity across generations, now molecularly explained by unstable nucleotide repeat expansions: Huntington's, fragile X); **imprinting** (parent-of-origin-dependent manifestation: Prader-Willi, Angelman); mixed models (major gene + polygenic) and **oligogenic** models (a few interacting genes).

**Variance partition.** Phenotypic variance vp = genetic vg + shared environmental vc + non-shared environmental ve (including error). Genetic variance subdivides into **additive (va)** and **dominance (vd)** effects. **Heritability**: narrow-sense h² = va/vp; broad-sense = vg/vp; similarly shared-environment (c²) and non-shared-environment (e²) proportions. The three interpretive cautions: (1) **population-not-individual**; a 50% population heritability of IQ does not mean 50% of any individual's IQ is genetic; (2) **population-specific**: estimates differ between populations; (3) **modifiability-agnostic**: heritability says nothing about environmental treatability: **PKU is fully genetic yet fully preventable by diet**. Non-additive layers: **epistasis** (gene-gene interaction across loci); **gene-environment interaction** (G×E: genetic differences in environmental response, those at genetic risk manifest only with exposure; the exposed don't all develop disorder); **gene-environment correlation** (genotype correlated with environment: **passive**, sociable parents provide both sociability genes and a socialising environment; **active**: the sociable child seeks social situations; **evocative**: the sociable child evokes friendly responses), with the critical implication that many "environmental" risk factors (life events) correlate with genetic risk, requiring genetically sensitive designs to test true environmental mediation.

**Research methods.** **Family studies** (aggregation; the family-history versus family-study methods; ascertainment and age-correction: the Slater-Stromgren adaptation of Weinberg's method; life-table analysis). **Twin studies**: MZ twins (100% shared genes) versus DZ (50%); intraclass correlations for continuous traits, concordance rates (pairwise and probandwise) for dichotomous; the twin-model equations rmz = h² + c²; rdz = ½h² + c²: decomposed by path analysis or model-fitting (Mx software; likelihood maximisation; χ² comparisons; the likelihood-ratio test); criticisms and answers: the **equal-environments assumption** (tested with measured environmental sharing; most studies supporting it), zygosity determination (DNA typing ideal; questionnaire 95%+ accurate), twin representativeness (psychiatric disorder rates not elevated), and twinness itself. **Adoption studies**, three designs: the adoptee study (adopted-away offspring of affected parents versus controls); the adoptee's-family study (biological versus adoptive relatives); the **cross-fostering study** (unaffected-biological/affected-adoptive versus affected-biological/unaffected-adoptive: the G×E experiment); the caveats (adoption itself increases some risk; non-random placement by agencies matching characteristics). **Multivariate model-fitting findings**: the **same genes, different non-shared environments** influencing anxiety and depressive disorders; the genetic contribution to depression symptoms and IQ **increasing from childhood to adolescence**; broader cognitive-social phenotypes in autism relatives. **G×E demonstrations**: the twin-study of maltreatment (environmentally mediated) versus corporal punishment (genetically mediated) effects on antisocial behaviour; **Kendler's finding** that those at higher genetic risk of major depression are more sensitive to the depressogenic effects of adverse life events; adoption-evidence that antisocial-behaviour risk multiplies in high-genetic-risk offspring exposed to adverse adoptive rearing.

### 4.2 Molecular genetics (2.4.2)

**The machinery.** DNA (double helix, A-T/C-G complementarity, 5'/3' polarity) and RNA (single-stranded, unstable, mediating expression); transcription (DNA → RNA; splicing out introns; capping and polyadenylation) and translation (the three-letter code to proteins). Genome organisation: chromosomes (replication origins, **centromeres**, the p (short) and q (long) arms; **telomeres**: TTAGGG repeats protecting the ends); the 3.3-gigabase, ~22,000-gene nuclear genome plus the small maternally-inherited mitochondrial genome; the metaphor: the genome as "a working copy written over countless rough drafts and discarded versions" rather than an efficient plan. **Gene regulation**: transcription factors (binding promoters and distant sites; controlling multiple genes: **pleiotropy**; the ATRX example: α-thalassaemia, facial appearance, developmental delay, hypotonia, genital abnormalities from one X-linked chromatin-remodelling gene); **DNA methylation** (CpG dinucleotides; gene repression; epigenetic: reversible but maintained through cell division); **imprinting** (expression from only one parental chromosome; ~60 documented genes; maternally-expressed genes enhancing and paternally-expressed genes reducing brain size; the imprinting disorders of intellectual disability: **Rett, Prader-Willi, Angelman, Turner**; X-inactivation; the Skuse Turner-syndrome findings: maternally-expressed X-linked genes influencing hippocampal, paternally-expressed caudate/thalamic development; social-cognition implications). **The epigenetic programming finding**: maternal care (rat licking-grooming) altering brain glucocorticoid receptor expression and stress sensitivity in offspring (observable with cross-fostered pups, mediated by **DNA methylation and histone acetylation**, persisting two generations and reversible) the biology of environmental programming.

**Gene mapping.** **Linkage**: the co-segregation of a disorder with genetic markers in families; the **recombination fraction** θ (the recombinants/total proportion; 0.5 for unlinked loci; smaller for closely linked: genetic distance in **centimorgans**, 1 cM ≈ 1% recombination; the sex-averaged human genome ~3,700 cM; whole-genome search with 200–300 evenly-spaced markers); **lod scores** (the log-of-odds for linkage at a given θ versus 0.5; the conventions, **lod ≥ 3 (1000:1 odds) accepting linkage, lod ≤ −2 excluding it**; genome-wide significance considerations); the model-specification problem (mis-specified penetrance → missed linkage); **locus heterogeneity** (different genes producing similar phenotypes: Usher's syndrome with six genes; early-onset Alzheimer's with presenilin 1, presenilin 2 and amyloid precursor protein); the multiplex-family strategy. **Association studies**: allelic association (a marker allele and the disease occurring together more often than by chance), more powerful than linkage for small effects; **candidate-gene association** (testing biologically plausible genes, the DRD2 and 5-HTT examples of candidate studies) versus **whole-genome association** (systematic scanning, the HapMap catalogue of common variation; **linkage disequilibrium** and haplotypes; tagging SNPs); the population stratification problem (spurious association from population ancestry differences); case-control and family-based (TDT) designs. The molecular tools: the polymerase chain reaction; microarrays (expression profiling; genotyping chips); the bioinformatics era: discovery through the internet (Ensembl, UCSC browsers).

**The psychiatric verdict** (the chapters' honest synthesis): for the major disorders (schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, depression) twin studies establish substantial heritability; linkage found occasional major-locus families but few replicable loci; association studies and the whole-genome era confirmed **polygenicity: many variants of small effect**, with gene-environment interaction and correlation entangling the risk architecture; the rare-variant and copy-number-variant stories (post-Oxford developments: DISC1, neuregulin, the 22q11 deletion and CNV discoveries; GWAS's schizophrenia hits) continue the theme; the clinical translation being risk-quantification and pharmacogenetics, not gene tests for diagnosis.

## 5. Why it matters clinically

- **Genetic counselling** for psychiatric disorders uses the threshold model: the population, familial and twin-derived risk figures (schizophrenia: general population ~1%, MZ co-twin ~50%, DZ/child of affected ~10-15%), framed probabilistically, never deterministically.
- **Heritability-misuse correction** ("it's genetic, so nothing can be done"); the PKU paradigm is the answer in one example; every psychiatric heritability estimate coexists with environmental modifiability.
- **The G×E reframing of prevention**: vulnerability genes (MAOA × maltreatment; 5-HTT short-variant × stress in the post-Oxford literature) mean prevention targets environments interacting with risk; child protection is genetic medicine.
- **Family history as the clinical genetic test**: the pedigree remains the most informative genetic assessment available for psychiatric disorders.
- **The gene-environment correlation correction**: apparently environmental risk factors (life events, parenting styles) partly reflect genetic propensities, cautioning against pure-environmental causal claims.
- **India**: consanguinity (cousin marriage in several communities) raises autosomal-recessive disease including metabolic psychiatric-mimics; the infrastructure reality (karyotyping and fragile-X testing in major centres; genetic counselling scarce) defines the practical genetics service.

## 6. India lens

- **Consanguinity's clinical weight**: cousin-marriage prevalence in South Indian communities raises homozygosity for recessive conditions, the PKU-like metabolic disorders presenting as intellectual disability and psychiatric symptoms; a three-generation pedigree with marriage-pattern enquiry is the Indian genetic assessment's opening move.
- **The fragile-X rule**: any male intellectual disability with macro-orchidism, or any family X-linked inheritance pattern (affected males through daughters) earns fragile-X testing, available in Indian metros.
- **Genetic counselling for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder**: the commonest Indian consultation question ("will my child get it?"): answered with the threshold model's figures (populations ~1%; one affected parent ~10-13%; both parents higher; MZ co-twin 40-50%) and the determinism-correction (PKU; heritability ≠ fixed destiny).
- **Marriage-alliance genetics**: the disclosure dilemmas of psychiatric family history in arranged marriage, confidentiality, stigma and counselling ethics converge (the clinical task: honest probabilistic answers without stigmatising labels).
- **The epigenetic teaching**: the maternal-care methylation findings give a biological dignity to the Indian paediatric emphasis on early nurturing, and a non-blaming framework for adversities' long reach.
- **The service reality**: genetic testing capacity (karyotype, FISH, microarray in few centres); the district psychiatrist's genetics is pedigree analysis, risk counselling and referral; the biochemical-screening layer (neonatal PKU screening, uneven across states) is the preventable-burden frontier.

**FAQ**
*"Is schizophrenia genetic?"*: Partially: heritability is high (identical-twin concordance ~50% against a 1% population rate), but no single gene causes it, many genes of small effect combine with environment through the threshold model. Genetic risk, not genetic destiny.
*"If it's genetic, can it be treated?"*: Yes: heritability measures the causes of *differences* in a population, not the modifiability of the condition; PKU is 100% genetic and 100% diet-preventable; every psychiatric disorder with high heritability responds to treatment.
*"Will my children get it?"*: Probabilistically: one affected parent with schizophrenia raises a child's risk from ~1% to ~10-13%; both parents higher; an unaffected sibling of an affected person ~5-8%, the threshold model's numbers, framed with the determinism-correction.
*"Why study twins?"*: Because comparing identical (100% shared genes) with fraternal (50% shared) twins decomposes observation into genes, shared environment and unique environment; the natural experiment genetics could never ethically design.
*"What did the genome project change?"*: The maps and databases (HapMap, Ensembl) that made whole-genome searches possible; the discovery that "junk" DNA regulates gene activity; and the confirmation of polygenicity: thousands of small effects, not disease genes.
*"Can trauma change genes?"*: It changes gene *expression*; methylation marks laid down by early caregiving and stress alter which genes are read, persist across generations in animals, and are potentially reversible: environment writing on genetics without changing the sequence.
*"My cousin married a cousin, does that matter?"*: Consanguinity raises the chance of recessive conditions (both parents carrying the same rare variant); it matters for disorders like PKU and the metabolic intellectual-disability mimics; pedigree-based counselling and newborn screening are the responses.

**Self-test MCQs**

**Q1.** The polygenic threshold model of psychiatric disorders proposes:
- (a) A single dominant gene in every family
- (b) A normally distributed liability, with affected individuals beyond a threshold, relatives' liability distributions shifted rightward
- (c) Pure environmental causation
- (d) Mitochondrial inheritance

**Answer: (b).** The elegant reconciliation: familial aggregation without Mendelian ratios, a continuum of inherited vulnerability with a manifest threshold.

**Q2.** A heritability of 50% for IQ in a population means:
- (a) Half of each individual's IQ is genetic
- (b) Half of the population's IQ variation is attributable to genetic variation, a population statistic with no individual-level meaning
- (c) IQ cannot be changed
- (d) Half the population has a genetic IQ

**Answer: (b).** The population-not-individual caution, with the companions: estimates are population-specific and say nothing about modifiability (PKU being the proof).

**Q3.** In gene-environment correlation, the sociable child who actively seeks social situations illustrates:
- (a) Passive correlation
- (b) Active correlation (genotype shaping the environment sought)
- (c) Evocative correlation
- (d) Epistasis

**Answer: (b).** Passive (sociable parents providing both genes and environment) and evocative (the child evoking friendly responses) being the other two, the trio complicating environmental-risk inference.

**Q4.** A lod score of 3 in linkage analysis corresponds to:
- (a) 3% probability of linkage
- (b) Odds on linkage of 1000:1 (nominal P ≈ 0.0001), the accepted linkage-detection convention
- (c) Three markers
- (d) 3 centimorgans

**Answer: (b).** Morton's convention: lod ≥ 3 accepts, lod ≤ −2 excludes linkage, with the genome-wide-significance debates confirming ≈3 as the approximate modern threshold.

**Q5.** Imprinting is relevant to intellectual disability through:
- (a) Maternal malnutrition
- (b) Parent-of-origin-dependent gene expression: Rett, Prader-Willi, Angelman and Turner syndromes
- (c) Twin studies
- (d) Population stratification

**Answer: (b).** The same deletion producing Prader-Willi (paternal) or Angelman (maternal) depending on the deleted parent's copy, imprinting's textbook demonstration.

**Q6.** Kendler's twin-study demonstration of gene-environment interaction in depression showed:
- (a) Life events affect only those without genetic risk
- (b) Individuals at higher genetic risk of major depression are more sensitive to the depressogenic effects of adverse life events
- (c) Genes and environment never interact
- (d) Identical twins never develop depression

**Answer: (b).** The interaction: genetic susceptibility amplifying environmental sensitivity; the prevention implication being that protection matters most precisely where genetic risk is highest.
