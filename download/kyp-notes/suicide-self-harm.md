# Suicide & Deliberate Self-Harm

> **If you or someone close to you is in danger right now: India's national tele-mental-health helpline is Tele-MANAS (14416, or 1-800-891-4416), free, 24 hours, in multiple Indian languages. The older KIRAN helpline (1800-599-0019) also continues to operate. In a life-threatening emergency, go to the nearest hospital emergency department.**

## 1. In one line + overview

**Suicidality is not a diagnosis; it is an emergency state where psychological pain exceeds the person's resources to bear it, and the response is always the same three moves: ask directly, remove the means, and connect to care fast.**

This note is written for three readers at once. For the worried family member: asking someone directly about suicide does NOT plant the idea; research is clear that it brings relief and opens a door. For the student: the exam question "the strongest predictor of suicide" has one answer, a previous attempt. For the clinician: chronic risk factors tell you WHO to worry about, but acute state (current ideation, plan, means, recent loss, intoxication, discharge from hospital within the last month) tells you WHEN to act today. Deliberate self-harm is a related but distinct behaviour (hurting the body to survive unbearable feelings, most often without wanting to die) and it is one of the strongest long-term risk markers we have, which is why "she was just doing it for attention" is among the most dangerous sentences in medicine. Most people who die by suicide were struggling with a treatable mental illness (depression, alcohol use disorder, psychosis, bipolar disorder), most had told someone something in the weeks before, and almost all passed through a health-care door in the month before death, which means every door is a chance to notice.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Distinguish suicidal ideation, plan, intent, preparatory acts, attempts, and non-suicidal self-injury
- Name the strongest static and the most important acute risk factors
- Conduct a direct suicide-risk interview without flinching or euphemism
- Build a safety plan (the evidence-based alternative to the paper "no-suicide contract")
- Apply means-restriction counselling: the single most population-effective prevention tool
- State India's epidemiology accurately (NCRB/ADSI data, SRS rates, high-risk groups)
- Manage post-attempt care: the high-risk first week after discharge, follow-up contact, justice-system rights
- Recognise and manage non-suicidal self-injury in adolescents without escalating or dismissing
- Counsel suicide-bereaved families with language that heals rather than wounds

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** WHO estimates just over 700,000 suicide deaths yearly (approximately 9–10 per 100,000), and it ranks among the leading causes of death for 15–29-year-olds; the second leading cause in several WHO analyses of this band. Men die more often than women in most countries (ratio near 3:1); women attempt more often. Peaks sit in two hills: youth (15–29) and elderly men. For every death, there are many more attempts; the widely quoted ratio is 10–20 attempts per death, higher in young people.

**India.** The National Crime Records Bureau's ADSI reports record around 170,000 registered suicide deaths in recent years (2022: about 170,900), a national rate of roughly 12 per 100,000, slightly above the global average, and WHO/SRS-based reanalyses suggest the true burden may be higher. India's picture is distinctive and examinable:
- **Age**: national burden concentrated under 45; student suicides have risen year-on-year (over 13,000 registered annually, roughly one student death per hour by NCRB counts); exam and results seasons produce detectable spikes.
- **Occupation profile**: daily-wage workers, the self-employed in agriculture, and homemakers are the largest groups; economic precarity and family conflict are dominant recorded stressors, more than "mental illness" as a recorded category.
- **Family problems and illness** are the leading recorded "causes" in NCRB data; reflecting how Indians report distress socially rather than diagnostically.
- The Dandekar/Patel pesticide-and-youth literature made southern and eastern states (Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Kerala, Andhra, West Bengal, Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh) high-rate zones in different eras; rates vary substantially by state and year.
- Means-restriction success story worth memorising: Sri Lanka's regulation of the most hazardous pesticides after the 1990s cut national suicide deaths dramatically without any change in "intent"; proof that the method, not just the mind, is a legitimate treatment target.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**The model to hold: stress-diathesis + state.** A person's lifetime vulnerabilities (diathesis) set the baseline; acute stressors and mental state determine whether today is the day. Prevention works on both layers, but the EMERGENCY layer is always the state.

**Static / chronic (WHO to worry about):**
- Previous suicide attempt: the strongest single predictor; attempt survivors carry a multi-fold elevated long-term risk
- Psychiatric illness: depression, bipolar disorder (mixed states), PTSD, psychosis and command hallucinations, alcohol and substance use disorders, personality disorder with impulsivity; comorbidity multiplies risk
- Family history of suicide (independent of diagnosis); twin and adoption studies show partial heritability of the tendency itself
- Male sex and older-male age; rural residence in India; marginalised identity (LGBTQ+ youth carry high risk)
- Chronic pain, disability, cancer, epilepsy, dialysis; severe tinnitus; head injury
- Access to lethal means at home (pesticides, firearms, stored medicines, high places, train lines)
- Bereavement by suicide of a close contact

**Acute / state (WHEN to act today):**
- Current ideation with plan and preparation; rehearsal; notes or giving away possessions
- Recent discharge from psychiatric inpatient care (first week highest)
- Recent attempt within days
- Intoxication right now (alcohol disinhibits reliably)
- Acute loss: exam results, relationship collapse, public humiliation, debt call, arrest
- Insomnia with agitation; command hallucinations; abrupt calm after severe agitation (resolution can mean the decision is made)
- Sudden "peace" or farewell gestures in a previously anguished patient

**Protective factors (real, but never sufficient alone):** responsibility to children, engaged treatment, religious/moral objections to suicide, restricted means, problem-solving capacity, one trusted person.

**Indian context.** Precarity drivers (crop failure and debt cycles, exam-result season, dowry and in-law conflict for young married women, unemployment spurts, and substance-driven family conflict) cluster in the recorded data. At household level: pesticides under the bed, farm-stored chemicals, and unsupervised medication strips are the recurrent access problem; means-restriction counselling must be delivered in the family's own language of "safe storage", not psychiatric jargon.

## 6. What happens in the mind/brain

**Story 1: the pain equation.** Think of suicidal drive as an equation: unbearable pain, felt as permanent, minus every resource the person believes they still have. Depression corrupts the "permanent" term (it will never improve); substance use corrupts the "resources" term (nobody would actually help me); shame corrupts both (they are better off without me). The clinician's job is not to argue the whole equation; it is to change one or two terms today (relieve the agony, restore one human connection, remove the immediate means) so that tomorrow becomes possible.

**Story 2: the narrowing tunnel.** People who survive attempts describe a collapse of the field of vision: options vanish one by one (talking to family, waiting a month, professional help, faith) until only one exit appears to remain. That is why "think of your family" so often fails: inside the tunnel, they have concluded the family is better off without them, an honest symptom of the illness, not a real belief to debate. The intervention that works is widening: "I can see how much pain you are in; you do not have to solve your whole life tonight; we start with tonight."

**Story 3: the volatility problem.** Suicidal crises are often SHORT. Many attempt survivors report the decisive window between decision and act as minutes to an hour. If the lethal means is ten minutes away instead of two minutes away, a meaningful share of people pass through the crisis alive; the entire logic behind means restriction and safe-storage counselling, and behind Sri Lanka's pesticide story. Time is the active ingredient of survival.

## 7. Symptoms: what warning signs look like

There is no "suicide symptom" in a textbook sense; there are communications. Watch for:
- **Speech**: "You all will be peaceful soon", "I'm a burden", "What's the point of it all", "I won't be in your way much longer", sudden apologies and thank-yous
- **Behaviour**: giving away valued things; settling accounts; searching behaviours online; acquiring or relocating means; saying goodbyes at odd times; abrupt calm; withdrawal from food and people
- **Mood state**: anguish, agitation with insomnia, humiliation, numbness; or a sudden lifting after weeks of turmoil (decision made)
- **Situations**: post-discharge days, post-results day, after arrest or public shame, after the death by suicide of a contact or a public figure (contagion windows; be watchful with class groups)

**In self-harm (NSSI):** recurrent cutting/burning/hitting done for relief, to feel something, or to communicate distress that has no words; typically WITHOUT intent to die, but with real long-term risk attached.

## 8. Assessment: the interview that saves lives

**Ask directly. Plain words. No euphemism.** "Have you had thoughts of ending your life?" then, if yes: "Have you thought about how?" "Do you have access to that?" "Have you taken any steps toward it?" "Have you tried before?" "What has stopped you until now?" Direct questioning lowers anxiety in the patient and improves the truth of the answer; this is evidence, not folklore.

**Then assess the state, layer by layer:**
1. **Ideation**: passive (wish to be dead) vs active (thoughts of acting); frequency; intensity; how close to action
2. **Plan**: specificity of method, time, place; specific and time-bound plans are far more dangerous
3. **Means**: what is actually accessible at home today, pesticides, medicine strips, ropes, wells, train lines, firearms if in a licensed home
4. **Preparatory acts and rehearsal**: the sharpest escalators
5. **Intent and reasons**: reasons for dying AND reasons for living (people hold both, write both on paper)
6. **Recent attempt**: lethality, planning discovered, rescue circumstances, current medical state
7. **State variables right now**: intoxication, agitation, insomnia, psychosis, command hallucinations
8. **Buffers**: who knows? who can supervise tonight? what does the person still live for?

**Instruments to know by name (not reproduced):** the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) as the standard structured screen; SAD PERSONS as a mnemonic-based checklist (know its letters for exams, but know its limits, it misses context); PHQ-9 item 9 as a depression-screening gateway question. Scales assist; they never replace the interview and the judgment.

**Decide the level of care:**
- **Imminent risk** (plan + means + intent, or fresh attempt, or intoxication with active intent): do not leave alone; remove means; emergency department or psychiatric admission; one-to-one observation if inpatient; treat medical consequences first.
- **High but not imminent**: daily contact, family supervision round-the-clock, means locked away, urgent psychiatric review within 24–48 hours; consider admission if buffers are thin.
- **Moderate/low**: safety plan, weekly reviews, treat the underlying disorder, involve the family, give crisis numbers in writing.

**The safety plan (write it WITH the patient):** warning signs → internal coping → people and places for distraction → people to ask for help → professionals and helplines (Tele-MANAS 14416) → making the environment safe (means locked/removed) → one reason to live at the top. Evidence supports safety planning; there is no evidence supporting "no-suicide contracts", they protect the clinician's anxiety, not the patient. Stop using them.

### Differential considerations

| Presentation | Read it as |
|---|---|
| Overdose after a breakup, regretted immediately, called for help | High-acuity attempt despite ambivalence: full risk assessment, never dismissive |
| Long-standing cutting without suicidal statements | NSSI: assess separately; do not assume safety |
| Psychotic patient with voices commanding self-harm | Emergency: command hallucinations override reassurance logic |
| "Peaceful" resolution after weeks of agitation | Red flag: decision may be made; raise supervision |
| Alcohol-intoxicated ideation | Assess again when sober; intoxication is itself an emergency amplifier |
| Post-results student panic with shame talk | Acute-on-chronic pattern: engage same day; involve family; means-restriction talk |

## 9. Management

**Emergency care (day 0):** medical stabilisation first; every attempt survivor gets a proper medical and toxicological assessment; then psychiatric evaluation before discharge whenever possible, with means-restriction counselling to the accompanying family BEFORE they leave (pesticides locked with a neighbour, medicine strips removed, keys managed) and a follow-up appointment inside 7 days.

**Treat the drivers:**
- Depression: SSRIs (fluoxetine approved down to age 8; escitalopram/sertraline common), remember the 1–2 week lag and the early activation window, schedule contact in week one, dispense limited quantities initially
- Psychosis and command hallucinations: antipsychotic, admission if uncontained
- Alcohol/substance use: specific treatment programme (see those notes); relapse prevention is suicide prevention
- Bipolar mixed states: mood stabiliser urgently
- Insomnia and agitation in the suicidal week: benzodiazepine cover short-term is legitimate and life-saving; hypnotic quantity dispensed must be small
- Lithium's anti-suicide effect in bipolar disorder and clozapine's in schizophrenia: the two psychiatric medicines with replicated anti-suicide evidence; choose them when otherwise appropriate

**Hospitalisation criteria:** imminent risk, fresh high-lethality attempt, no safe home, psychosis, need for ECT (severe psychotic depression with suicidality, among the strongest indications).

**Psychotherapy with direct suicide-focus:**
- **Cognitive-behavioural therapy for suicide prevention (CBT-SP)** and **dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT)**, the two best-evidenced approaches for recent attempters and for chronic suicidality in borderline-pattern patients respectively
- **Brief interventions that punch above their weight**: the "caring contact" literature (scheduled calls, texts, letters or postcards to people post-discharge) modest, cheap, replicated mortality/attempt-reduction signals; perfect for Indian district settings and Tele-MANAS follow-up
- Problem-solving therapy (6–8 sessions) for younger attempters with situational crises

*India note, the post-attempt reality: a medical ward bed, a police Form in some states, a family that has just paid for the stomach wash, and a discharge summary that says "counselling given". The upgrade path: (1) Psychiatry/tele-psychiatry review within 72 hours of medical discharge; (2) means counselling at the bedside, a two-minute conversation that costs nothing; (3) a written follow-up card with Tele-MANAS 14416; (4) treating the family as the safety net, with explicit instructions on supervision and safe storage, not vague advice. District hospitals under DMHP now increasingly have telepsychiatry links; use them for the post-attempt week.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **The legal landscape you must know cold.** Attempted suicide was historically an offence under Section 309 IPC. The Mental Healthcare Act 2017 (Section 115) creates a presumption of severe stress and decriminalises the attempt in effect; a person who attempts suicide is to be treated as suffering mental illness and stress, not prosecuted. The BNS 2023 (Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita) retains a Section 226 on attempted suicide, but with an explicit mercy-clause framing (to be pressed only in coercive/frustration-with-duty circumstances, e.g., attempts to compel public officials), and MHA 2017's protective presumption governs healthcare practice. Translate for families: "coming to the hospital will not get you arrested", because fear of police exactly what stops Indian families from bringing attempt survivors back for follow-up.
- **NCRB framing.** NCRB "causes" (family problems, illness, financial) are recorded categories, not clinical explanations; use them to talk about precarity, never to conclude "no mental illness was involved".
- **The means conversation, Indian edition.** Not "do you have access to lethal means?" but: "Where are the farm chemicals kept? Can they move to a locked box with a neighbour for these few weeks? How are the medicine strips stored? Who in the house can be in charge of giving them out?" Deliver to the senior decision-maker of the household, in front of the patient only when collaborative.
- **Student season protocol.** Around board and entrance results: school counsellor briefings, the "results are one door, not the last door" messaging, and open access to counsellors for the 72 hours around publication. The Rajasthani 2012–13 cluster literature and subsequent student-cluster events make "contagion-awareness" a duty: after a student death, the school needs a response plan, not silence.
- **Homemaker and dowry-conflict presentations.** Young married women's attempts are frequently framed by families as "family drama". The duty is to the person: private interview away from in-laws, documentation of disclosed coercion, safety planning, and linkage with women's-helpline services (181) and protection officers under PWDVA 2005 where applicable.
- **Bereavement support.** After a suicide, families carry guilt, stigma and (in some communities) exclusion from rituals. Connect them with suicide-bereavement support; avoid both blame-mapping and myth-mouthing; the phrase that helps most is "you could not have known everything, and you do not have to carry this alone."
- **Helplines to print on every card:** Tele-MANAS 14416 / 1-800-891-4416 (all-India, 24×7); KIRAN 1800-599-0019; iCall and other city services; 112 for emergencies; 181 for women in distress.

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: the two-minute bedside conversation.** A 19-year-old first-year B.Tech student in Indore ingested a pesticide after her semester results; her roommate found her vomiting and raised the alarm. After stomach wash and 48 hours of medical observation, the intern was asked to "give counselling". Instead, a proper assessment found a year of depressive symptoms, a specific plan made days earlier, and a roommate who had been handed a farewell note. The intern did three things before discharge: a direct risk assessment (active ideation, still present but softer), a two-minute means conversation with her father (the farm chemicals would move to a cousin's locked store, her medicine would be given by her mother daily, the hostel room would be changed from the seventh floor) and an appointment card for psychiatric review in 5 days with Tele-MANAS printed on it. Fluoxetine was started; the review happened; ideation faded over six weeks with CBT. Teaching points: (1) the post-medical-discharge moment IS the intervention; (2) means restriction takes minutes and outlasts any pep talk; (3) the follow-up appointment must exist as a dated fact, not advice.

**Case 2: the "attention-seeker" who wasn't.** A 16-year-old in Kozhikode with two years of forearm cutting was brought by exasperated parents after the third ED visit; the note in the file said "attention-seeking behaviour". Assessment revealed nightly cutting to "switch off" intrusive shame after years of classroom bullying, no wish to die, but a growing voice saying "everyone would be relieved". Management: DBT-informed skills (distress tolerance, emotion regulation), NSSI wound care without lectures, family sessions (supervision without surveillance; phone limits negotiated, not imposed as punishment), school engagement on the bullying pattern. Cutting frequency fell from near-daily to twice in four months; the "relief" voice was directly treated as a risk symptom. Teaching points: (1) NSSI is a communication and regulation behaviour with real long-term risk, never triage it as theatre; (2) treat the function (affect relief), not the behaviour as moral failure; (3) document and follow the emerging suicidal ideation separately.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way patients and families ask it

**"If I ask my son about suicide, will it put the idea in his head?"** No. Studies repeatedly show that asking directly neither plants nor strengthens the thoughts, most people describe the question as a relief, because someone finally named what they carry. Ask plainly, listen fully, and help him to safety. The danger is silence, not the question.

**"She tried it just to punish us / for attention."** Whatever the mix of motives, the attempt is a medical emergency, and the aftermath carries the highest short-term suicide risk of any group. "Attention" was at least partially a request for help that found no other door. Treat every attempt as serious and every attempt survivor as high-risk for the next year; that is what the data says, whatever the kitchen says.

**"He became so calm and peaceful: we thought he was improving."** Sudden calm after weeks of anguish is a warning, not a cure: it can mean the person has resolved the internal struggle by deciding. Any abrupt peace, farewell gestures, or giving away possessions should raise, not lower, your vigilance.

**"What do we actually do tonight, before the doctor's appointment?"** Three things: stay with them (do not leave them alone, including in the bathroom), remove what could be used (medicines, chemicals, ropes, sharp tools, to a locked place outside the house if possible), and call Tele-MANAS 14416 together. If they have taken anything or are about to, go straight to the emergency department.

**"Won't the police come? That's what neighbours said."** The Mental Healthcare Act 2017 protects people who attempt suicide; you are presumed to be under severe stress and entitled to care, not prosecution. Hospitals are for healing tonight; the law is on your side. Bring them in without fear.

**"He's drinking every day and talking about ending it only when drunk."** Intoxication removes the brakes, most acts happen under alcohol. The plan: assess when sober too, but never wait for "just drunk" talk to pass on its own; alcohol treatment IS suicide treatment here; tonight, no alcohol in the house and no being alone.

**"How many tablets / which method is dangerous?" (asked by a worried relative for 'safety planning')** Families sometimes ask this to "know what to hide". The right frame: we do not need an inventory of methods; we need an inventory of access. Everything that can harm gets locked away or removed, medicines are given out one day at a time, and farm/store chemicals leave the home for now. That is the version that protects.

**"She cut herself again but says she doesn't want to die. Is that even a problem?"** It is a real problem; self-injury is how she survives unbearable feelings, and it both signals deep distress and raises future risk. She needs help aimed at what the cutting does FOR her (relief, control, communication), not punishment aimed at the cutting itself. And she still gets asked directly about suicidal thoughts; both are true.

**"Is it true people who talk about it never do it?"** The reverse is closer to the truth: most people who die by suicide gave some signal in the weeks before, words, behaviour, goodbyes. Talk is the invitation to intervene; ignoring it because "talkers don't do it" is the myth that costs the most lives.

**"We lost him. Was it something we missed? Something we did?"** The kindest honest answer: you could not see everything, and nothing you did makes you guilty of this. Suicide is usually an illness of pain that hides its tracks. The way forward now is support for the living (grief counselling, other children's needs, and rituals without blame) and we can help you find that. (Say this slowly. Do not lecture.)

## 14. Exam & student lens

**Mnemonics and anchors:**
- **SAD PERSONS** (Sex, Age, Depression, Previous attempt, Ethanol, Rational thinking loss, Social supports, Organised plan, No spouse/Sickness); know it for exams; know it lacks context and youth adjustments in real use
- **The "1 month" fact**: highest-risk days after discharge, the first 1–4 weeks; structure follow-up accordingly
- **The "previous attempt" fact**: strongest single clinical predictor
- **Means restriction**: the Sri Lanka pesticide-regulation story; population-level mortality fall; the classic "public-health answer" in viva

**High-yield numbers:**
- India (NCRB 2022): ~170,900 registered suicides; rate ~12.4/100,000; >13,000 students; family problems and illness = top recorded causes
- Global (WHO): >700,000 deaths/year; second leading cause in 15–29-year-olds in WHO analyses; male:female death ratio ~3:1, attempts female-heavy
- Attempt:death ratio ~10–20:1 overall (higher in youth)

**Classic traps:**
1. Writing "no-suicide contract" as the answer to any exam question about management; the accepted answers are safety planning + means restriction + level-of-care decision
2. Assuming expressed "attention-seeking" attempts are low-risk; attempt survivors are the highest-risk group
3. Missing the intoxication state ("assess again when sober" is right; "wait and see" alone is wrong)
4. Forgetting to treat the underlying illness aggressively (depression, psychosis, alcohol); the ideation follows the disorder
5. In a post-results student scenario: forgetting contagion-awareness and school-level response
6. Counselling families to "watch the patient" without specifying supervision and means; vague advice is no advice

**Indian exam corner:** "evaluate and manage a recent suicide attempt" (station/viva), " Section 115 MHA 2017 vs Section 309 IPC/BNS 226 (legal position on attempted suicide in India", " Tele-MANAS and the national mental-health programme infrastructure", "NCRB trends) occupational and age profile", "preventive strategies at population level, the pesticide ban evidence". All are recurring question-bank favourites across universities.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. The strongest single clinical predictor of eventual death by suicide is:**
A. Male sex
B. A previous suicide attempt
C. Depression severity score
D. Recent job loss
**Answer: B.** Attempt history outweighs every other listed variable over the long run.

**2. The first 4 weeks after discharge from psychiatric inpatient care:**
A. Are a low-risk period because the patient is stabilised
B. Carry among the highest suicide risks in the whole course
C. Carry risk only in schizophrenia
D. Require follow-up only if symptomatic
**Answer: B.** Post-discharge weeks are the classic high-risk window, schedule contact inside the first week.

**3. A family asks what to change at home after an attempt. The best-evidenced single advice:**
A. Install CCTV
B. Remove/lock means (pesticides, medicines, ropes) and supervise
C. Keep the person indoors for a month
D. Arrange daily temple visits
**Answer: B.** Means restriction plus close supervision; access, not surveillance technology, is the modifiable lethal variable.

**4. "No-suicide contracts" in current evidence-based practice are:**
A. First-line
B. Protective in adolescents
C. Not supported by evidence; replaced by collaborative safety planning
D. Required for medico-legal safety
**Answer: C.** Safety plans (warning signs → coping → contacts → means restriction) are the supported standard.

**5. Under the Mental Healthcare Act 2017, a person who attempts suicide is:**
A. To be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours
B. Presumed to be under severe stress and entitled to care and protection
C. Liable to prosecution unless medically ill
D. Required to register with the district police
**Answer: B.** Section 115 creates the presumption of severe stress, effectively decriminalising the act for healthcare purposes.

**6. A patient with schizophrenia says voices command him nightly to jump. Management level:**
A. Weekly outpatient follow-up
B. Self-help leaflet with helpline numbers
C. Urgent containment: admission-level care, treat hallucinations, one-to-one supervision while risk persists
D. Discharge with benzodiazepine PRN
**Answer: C.** Command hallucinations are an emergency-state amplifier; containment and treatment of the psychosis come before outpatient logic.

**7. Sri Lanka's national suicide-mortality decline after pesticide regulation is cited as proof that:**
A. Reducing intent through counselling is the only effective prevention
B. Method availability is itself a modifiable cause at population level
C. Suicide is purely economic
D. Media reporting has no effect
**Answer: B.** The Sri Lankan natural experiment is the flagship evidence for means restriction as population-level prevention.
