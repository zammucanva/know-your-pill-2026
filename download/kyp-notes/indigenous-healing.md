# Indigenous & Folk Healing Practices — The Culturally Embedded Therapies

## 1. In one line + overview

**Indigenous and folk healing practices (shamanism, zar ceremonies, sacrificial rituals, divination, temple fortune-telling, meditation) are non-orthodox, culturally embedded therapies that patients use alongside or instead of us, work through identifiable common therapeutic factors, and deserve the clinician's knowledgeable respect, selective support and protection-from-harm.**

The Oxford chapter (Tseng) defines the field: non-orthodox therapeutic practices based on indigenous cultural traditions, **operating outside official healthcare systems**, validated by experience rather than scientific principles, found in pre-industrial and modern societies alike, and "culturally embedded" (intensely rooted in the cultural system that invented them, hence difficult to transplant). Crucially: neither healer nor client usually considers these *psychological therapy*, they are religious ceremonies or supernatural exercises, yet from the mental-health point of view they often deliver **psychotherapeutic effects: folk psychotherapy**. The chapter's clinical claim is that modern psychiatrists must know them, because our patients use them, they offer therapeutic mechanisms we can recognise (and share), and they carry harms we must protect against. The catalogue: the four orientations (supernatural, natural, medical-physiological, socio-psychological); **spirit mediumship** with its crucial healer-dissociates (shamanism) versus client-dissociates (zar) distinction; **religious healing ceremonies** (Salish spirit dancing with its brainwashing-like mechanisms; Yoruba sacrificial ritual; Spiritual Baptist mourning; snake-handling; Christian healing across the spectrum); **divination** (Afa and Ifa; Chinese temple chien-drawing, Japanese kujibiki: the naming effect and clear-cut answers); **fortune-telling** (astrology, physiognomy, the Yi-Jing: fate modifiable, harmony with nature). The synthesis: **the common therapeutic factors** (hope through dependency; naming: the Rumpelstiltskin principle; admired personal qualities; expectation and emotional arousal; learning and mastery; technique) shared with modern psychotherapy: Torrey's point that witchdoctors and psychiatrists have common roots; the traditional sector's advantages; and the regulatory demand that folk healing be surveyed, evaluated and its malpractice prevented.

## 4. The core ideas

### 4.1 The definition and the four orientations

Indigenous, folk healing practices: non-orthodox therapeutic practices based on indigenous cultural traditions, operating outside official healthcare systems, experience-validated, not science-founded; present in both "primitive" and developed societies. All therapies, including orthodox psychotherapy, are culturally influenced, but indigenous practices are **culturally embedded**: intensely tied to the cultural system that invented them, and therefore difficult to transplant where they lack meaning and legitimacy. The healers and clients do not consider them psychological therapy for psychological problems, they are religious ceremonies or supernatural exercises, yet they often provide psychotherapeutic effects: **folk psychotherapy**. The loose term covers: religious healing ceremonies; shamanism (spirit-medium); **divination and fortune-telling** (astrology, physiognomy: folk counselling); even meditation, if prevention and quality of mental life count as therapy. The orientation spectrum: **supernatural** (spirit mediumship, religious ceremony, divination); **nature** (fortune-telling, astrology, meditation); **medical-physiological** (mesmerism, acupuncture, herbal medicine); **socio-psychological** (Zen training, Alcoholics Anonymous, est, most modern psychotherapy), arbitrary, overlapping, but clarifying.

### 4.2 Spirit mediumship: who dissociates matters

**Shamanism** (heartland: Central and North Eurasia, diffused to Southeast Asia and the Americas): the healer enters trance, via rhythmic singing, dancing, praying or psychedelics (e.g., cactus substances among native American healers), becoming possessed by a god or supernatural power; the **client consults the supernatural through the shaman**. Causal interpretations follow folk concepts: soul loss, sorcery, spirit intrusion, taboo violation, disharmony with nature; the coping is magical (prayer, charms, extraction or exorcism rituals). The mechanisms: utilising supernatural power as an **authority figure**, making **suggestions**, providing **hope**. **Zar ceremonies** (Muslim societies: Ethiopia, Egypt, Iraq, Kuwait, Sudan, Somaliland): a ceremony and a class of spirits; crucially the **client also experiences the dissociated or possessed state**. A female event: participants in clean clothing, the patient in white with gold and perfume; the ceremony master sings and drums; the called spirit makes a woman shake, dance, tremble until she falls exhausted; the spirit demands favours (jewellery, clothing, expensive foods) which relatives and friends gather to provide, propitiation and persuasion, never coercion; ending with animal sacrifice and feast. The zar's sociology: sex-segregated societies, low female status, restricted religious participation, marital insecurity, the ceremony relieves persistent anxieties of those life conditions; the demanded goods are things **husbands should provide**. Mechanisms: **emotional catharsis, fulfilment of unsatisfied desire, compensation for the suppressed female role**; the implicit goal being restored balance in real life. (One clinical caveat from the wider KYP library: possession-trance presentations can be dissociative disorder, culturally sanctioned trance, or psychosis, the differential lives in the psychiatric notes, not the ceremony.)

### 4.3 Religious healing ceremonies

**Spirit dancing** (Salish-speaking Indians, Pacific North America): an initiation with brainwashing-like mechanisms, depatterning through shock (restraint, blindfolding, kinetic and intensive acoustic stimulation, then stillness, silence, starvation); physical training (running, ice-water immersion, dancing); indoctrination. **Yoruba sacrificial ritual**: problems identified by the diviner's tossed palm nuts; the lineage deity offended; an animal sacrificed: the supplicant's bad luck passes to the animal, killed in his stead; healing power in **reassurance and the generation of conviction**; proper curative steps are visibly being taken. **Spiritual Baptist mourning** (West Indies): after washing and anointing, members isolate in a back chamber for 7 days of prayer, fasting, dreams and visions, claimed benefits: mood relief, danger-foresight, better decision-making, physical cures, communication with God. **Snake-handling** (southern US): trance-state handling of poisonous snakes as a sign of blessing, occasional deaths, government prohibition, persistence; emotional excitement sought **at the risk of life**. **Christian religious healing**: common in industrialised societies, across the spectrum from Christian Science to fundamentalist healers (Oral Roberts) to the Roman Catholic anointing of the sick, hope of supernatural resources increasing security and well-being. Across all forms: prayer, testimony, sacrifice, reliving experience, spirit possession as the therapeutic operations; **assurance, suggestion and conviction** as mechanisms; healing the problem and giving life perspective as the aims.

### 4.4 Divination and fortune-telling

**Divination**: foretelling the unknown by occult means, the interpretation usually by the diviner, making the diviner-client interaction the key variable. Methods: the Nsukka Ibo's Afa (four strings of bush-mango half-shells); the Yoruba Ifa (palm nuts); the diviner's own shaking hand guided by supernatural power; ancient China's burned turtle shells read through their cracks; the **chien** system (Chinese temples; Japanese kujibiki): a sincere prayer, then a fortune-stick drawn, its number matched to a fortune paper bearing the answer. The therapeutic operation: **providing a clear-cut answer**, psychologically helpful as a definite way to address the problems; **naming effects** (the Rumpelstiltskin principle: anxiety falls when the trouble is named); the goal: finding the proper way to comply with the universe through divine instruction. **Fortune-telling**: the reference shifts from supernatural to **natural**: microcosm-macrocosm, human life as part of the universe; problems explained as imbalance of vital forces or disharmony with natural principles; the goal: living compatibly with nature. Varieties: astrology (stars as information source); the Chinese Oracle of Change (Yi-Jing) and birth-date/name-stroke calculations; physiognomy (character and fortune read from physical features, help the client use talents, compensate shortcomings). The subtle liberty: **fate is modifiable**, fortune-telling is not passive acceptance but adjustment, offering folk-natured interpretation and concrete guidance for choices.

### 4.5 The common factors and the clinical stance

**Shared therapeutic factors** (Frank; Torrey's *Witchdoctors and Psychiatrists*; Kirmayer on symbols): the core of religious and magical healing is the ability to **arouse hope by capitalising on dependency**; both witchdoctor and psychiatrist **decrease anxiety by naming what is wrong** (the Rumpelstiltskin principle); the therapist presents **personal qualities admired by the culture**; the client's **expectations** and the emotional arousal enhanced by setting, the therapist's self-belief and reputation; the **emerging sense of learning and mastery**; and technique. Folk healing and modern psychotherapy share these nonspecific mechanisms, and both use **symbols and metaphors** for interpretation and suggestion, folk healers sometimes more deliberately. The traditional sector's advantages over cosmopolitan medicine: **cultural congeniality, maximal use of the healer's personality, holistic approach, accessibility and availability (especially developing areas), effective use of affect and altered states, collective therapy management, cost-effectiveness**. **The three attitudes**, prohibition (modern clinicians dismissing superstition), academic study (anthropologists and cultural psychiatrists), support (community health workers facing personnel shortages), resolve into the chapter's position: *any folk healing practice that is proven or at least considered helpful to the client and useful to the community deserves support and encouragement*. **The universal factors named**: cultivation of hope, activation of surrounding support, enhancement of culturally sanctioned coping, with the supernatural dimension of healing power less intentionally utilised by modern therapy.

**The harms and the regulation demand**: the ill effects are under-studied but clinically observed, fraud and financial exploitation, sexual involvement with clients, dangerous prescribed substances, physical injury and even death during exorcisms. The quality range among folk healers is wide, benign servants alongside the untrained and malign; and in most societies **no formal regulation exists** as it does for modern therapy. The public-health demand: folk therapy should be subject to periodic surveys and reevaluation by the health administration, so benefits are protected and malpractice prevented; the healer who refuses examination and regulation should be discouraged or prevented from practising.

## 5. Why it matters clinically

- **Your patients are already there**: in most of the world, and for many patients in the West, folk healing precedes, accompanies or follows psychiatric contact; the consultation that never asks about it is working blind (the traditional sector is often the first help-seeking step, and the delay it causes is a clinical variable).
- **The common factors are our common factors**: hope, naming, expectation, the admired healer, mastery, seeing them in the shaman's tent clarifies what our own consulting rooms run on (and what to strengthen).
- **Selective support beats contempt**: dismissing the healer dismisses the family's world; the chapter's position (support what helps, study what puzzles, prevent what harms) is the workable clinical stance.
- **The harms are real and medical**: delayed treatment, financial ruin, sexual exploitation, dangerous substances, exorcism injuries; the psychiatric duty to protect does not pause for cultural respect.
- **Regulation is a psychiatric advocacy issue**: the demand for periodic survey and evaluation of folk practice is the system-level application of the clinical stance.
- **India**: the note's entire architecture applies to the temple-healing, dargah-circuit, faith-healer reality of Indian help-seeking, from the entry-question ("which healer has he already seen?") to the Erwadi-regulation imperative.

## 6. India lens

- **The Indian landscape is the chapter's best illustration**: temple healing (Tirupati, Shirdi, the goddess-circuit pilgrimages), dargah shrines (the Sufi healing tradition), exorcism practices (bhuta-vidya's classical descendant in contemporary ojha/bhagat practice), astrological consultation (jyotisha, the fate-modifiable guidance system), numerology, physiognomy-adjacent traditions, yoga and meditation (the nature-orientation practices now globally exported as therapy), and the Ayurvedic medical-physiological orientation. The chien-drawing description maps directly onto temple prasna and fortune-stick practices.
- **Possession presentations**: Indian OPDs meet the full spectrum, culturally sanctioned possession trance (normal in context), dissociative disorder presenting as possession, and psychosis wearing possession's clothing; the clinical discipline is the same triad the zar discussion implies, context-norm, distress/dysfunction, and the mental-state examination behind the trance.
- **The entry question**: "which healer has he seen, what was done, what did it cost?": the answers shape prognosis (treatment delay), drug history (herbal interactions), and the family alliance (contempt for the healer loses the family).
- **Collaboration over competition**: where healers refer onwards (the classic "he needs a doctor's medicine for this"), support and liaison; where they chain and beat (the Erwadi-type institutions), protect and report, the chapter's tri-partite stance operationalised in India.
- **Costs (approx 2026)**: temple and dargah healing is largely free-to-cheap (offerings), one of the sector's documented advantages; the exploitative end (commercial exorcists, "guaranteed-cure" clinics) extracts life savings, the regulatory demand's Indian target.
- **The shared-factors teaching gift**: Indian medical students understand therapeutic hope, expectation and the healer's authority fastest through the shaman-to-psychiatrist comparison; the chapter is a teaching instrument as much as a clinical guide.

**FAQ**
*"Are faith healers just frauds?"*: Some are; most are not. The field spans benign culturally-embedded healers delivering real therapeutic mechanisms (hope, naming, catharsis, support) alongside exploiters and the untrained, which is exactly why the chapter asks for evaluation and regulation rather than blanket judgement.
*"Should I tell my patient to stop going to the temple healer?"*: Ask first what the healing does for her; support what helps (hope, community, meaning); monitor what harms (delay, expense, dangerous rituals); and keep the psychiatric door open, collaboration protects better than prohibition.
*"Is a zar or possession ceremony a dissociative disorder?"*: Not by itself: culturally sanctioned trance is normal in its context; disorder requires distress, dysfunction and clinical features; the differential is psychiatric, the respect cultural.
*"Do these practices actually work?"*: They deliver the nonspecific therapeutic factors reliably (hope, naming, expectation, catharsis, support); the same engine our psychotherapies run on; what they lack is the specific, tested ingredients for defined disorders, which is where medicine enters.
*"Can meditation count as folk healing?"*: Under the broad definition, yes: a self-training practice for tranquillity, growth and prevention; its global export into modern therapy is the clearest case of the folk sector feeding the orthodox.
*"What harms should I actually watch for?"*: Treatment delay, financial exploitation, sexual involvement, dangerous substances, and physical injury during exorcism, the five the chapter names; your protective duty runs through all of them.
*"Who regulates these practices in India?"*: Largely nobody: the chapter's demand for periodic survey, evaluation and malpractice prevention is unmet Indian regulatory space; clinical advocacy belongs at the institutional and district level.

**Self-test MCQs**

**Q1.** Indigenous, folk healing practices are best defined as:
- (a) Any treatment without a prescription
- (b) Non-orthodox therapeutic practices based on indigenous cultural traditions, operating outside official healthcare systems, experience-validated and culturally embedded
- (c) All religious activity
- (d) Emergency first aid in rural areas

**Answer: (b).** The four defining features (non-orthodox, traditional-culturally-based, outside official systems, experience-validated) with cultural embeddedness explaining the transplant difficulty.

**Q2.** The critical distinction between shamanism and the zar ceremony is:
- (a) The continent of origin only
- (b) Who experiences the altered state: the healer in shamanism; both healer and (crucially) the client in zar
- (c) The music used
- (d) The duration of treatment

**Answer: (b).** The dissociating person determines the mechanism: the shaman's authority-suggestion-hope versus the zar's client catharsis, desire fulfilment and role compensation.

**Q3.** The zar ceremony's therapeutic mechanisms include all EXCEPT:
- (a) Emotional catharsis
- (b) Fulfilment of unsatisfied desires (the spirit's demanded gifts)
- (c) Compensation for the suppressed female role
- (d) Genetic counselling

**Answer: (d).** The three real mechanisms are inseparable from the ceremony's sociology, sex-segregated low-status women's lives, making zar "an ideal situation for relief of persistent and regular anxieties".

**Q4.** The "Rumpelstiltskin principle" in healing refers to:
- (a) Spinning straw into gold
- (b) The anxiety-reducing power of naming the problem; knowing what is wrong
- (c) Fairy-tale therapy
- (d) Keeping the client's name secret

**Answer: (b).** Torrey's point: witchdoctors and psychiatrists alike decrease anxiety by identifying (naming) what is wrong, divination's core therapeutic operation.

**Q5.** According to the chapter, the core of religious and magical healing's effectiveness lies in:
- (a) Pharmacological activity of rituals
- (b) The ability to arouse hope by capitalising on the patient's dependency on others
- (c) Insurance coverage
- (d) Dietary rules

**Answer: (b).** Frank's formulation: the first of the common therapeutic factors shared by folk and modern therapy.

**Q6.** The chapter's position on professional attitudes toward folk healing is:
- (a) Universal prohibition
- (b) Any practice proven or at least considered helpful to the client and useful to the community deserves support and encouragement
- (c) Complete indifference
- (d) Exclusive reliance on folk sector

**Answer: (b).** Between the prohibitionists, the academics and the pragmatists, with the twin duties of benefit-protection and malpractice-prevention through periodic survey and regulation.
