# Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)

## 1. In one line + overview

**GAD is a lifelong-style disorder of the worry engine: the mind treats every problem as a five-alarm emergency, generating uncontrollable worry across every domain of life, with a body that stays braced (tense, restless, sleepless, exhausted) for months and years on end.**

The difference between ordinary worry and GAD is the difference between a security guard and a smoke alarm with no off-switch. Ordinary worry targets real problems, resolves, and releases the body. In GAD the worry is general (money, health, children, work, the future, one topic handed off to the next), uncontrollable (starting it is easy; stopping is impossible), and chronic (six months or more by definition). The body pays the bill: muscle tension in the neck and jaw, a stomach that churns, sleep that will not come, fatigue that no weekend cures, irritability that the family bears. GAD is one of the most common mental disorders in the world and, in India, a substantial share of what presents as "tension", "gas", headache and burning hands. It runs in families, starts early, sits under depression like soil under grass, and responds well to structured psychotherapy and SSRIs; the tragedy being that most Indian GAD patients receive, instead, months of benzodiazepines that manage the evenings while the engine keeps running.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- State the DSM-5 architecture: excessive worry + uncontrollability + 6 months + 3+ physical symptoms + impairment
- Distinguish GAD from normal worry, depression's rumination, and adjustment disorders
- Explain the worry-function model (worry as avoidance of deeper emotional processing) to a patient
- Build the CBT package for GAD: worry-time, intolerance-of-uncertainty work, imaginal exposure, relaxation
- Use SSRI/SNRI first-line pharmacotherapy with proper duration (8–12 week trials, 12-month maintenance)
- Explain why benzodiazepines long-term are a trap in this specific disorder
- Recognise GAD's Indian presentations: somatic front-door complaints and the "tension" label
- Screen for the comorbidities that change the plan: depression, panic, alcohol, thyroid disease

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Lifetime prevalence ~4–7% (annual ~2–3%); women roughly twice as often as men; onset typically gradual in the teens through thirties, often a lifelong "worrier" biography rather than a new illness. The comorbidity rule: the majority of people presenting for treatment have or develop major depression; panic, social anxiety and alcohol use ride along frequently. GAD is among the top causes of work-day loss among mental disorders and is heavily over-represented in primary care, most GAD patients never reach a psychiatrist, circulating instead through general medicine, cardiology and gastroenterology for the body's complaints.

**India.** NMHS 2015–16 found neurotic/ stress-related disorders (a pooled bucket containing GAD) affecting roughly 3.5–3.6% of adults, with GAD itself at a substantial share of OPD "tension" presentations; treatment gaps in the 70–80% range. Indian epidemiology carries two systematic distortions to keep in mind: somatic presentation (patients report the body's bill ("gas", tremor, "heat in the head", palpitations) rather than worry) and prescription-pattern distortion (readily dispensed benzodiazepines and "nerve tonics" suppress the recorded prevalence of treated GAD while the engine runs on).

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**Biological.**
- Heritability ~30%: moderate, real; family aggregation of "worrier" temperaments
- Amygdala hyper-reactivity with weak prefrontal "braking"; serotonin and noradrenaline systems implicated (hence SSRI/SNRI response)
- Medical mimics to exclude: thyroid overactivity, anaemia, arrhythmia, caffeine excess, salbutamol/steroid/theophylline effects, hypoglycaemia in diabetics

**Psychological.**
- Intolerance of uncertainty: the mind treats ambiguity as danger ("a lump COULD be cancer" = "IS cancer, act now")
- Cognitive avoidance model: worry in WORDS is a way of dodging the sharper IMAGES and emotions beneath ("if I keep worrying in sentences, I never have to feel the picture of losing my job"), which is precisely why reassurance gives minutes, not months
- Worry as superstition: "if I worry about the flight, it will not crash"; worry gets the credit for survival

**Social / environmental.**
- Childhood adversity, overprotective or anxious parenting, financial precarity and job insecurity
- Caregiver roles (the family health-watcher who becomes the family GAD patient)

**Indian context.**
- Multi-domain load: loans, eldercare, school-admissions machinery, job insecurity, a genuinely high-uncertainty environment for the middle and working classes
- "Tension" as the socially permitted complaint that de-medicalises GAD ("everyone has tension"), delay to proper care averages years

## 6. What happens in the mind/brain

**Story 1: the alarm with no reset.** The amygdala screens the world for "could this go wrong?" and the prefrontal cortex normally answers "checked; proceed". In GAD the screening threshold drops to near-zero (everything registers as threat) and the braking cortex arrives late and weak. The result is a mind in a permanent airport-security state: every bag opened, every passenger patted down, all day, every day. Exhaustion is not a symptom added to this; it is the inevitable payroll.

**Story 2: the worry shield.** Worry feels like preparation, but in GAD it secretly functions as an anaesthetic. Worrying in words about "what if the biopsy is positive" keeps the mind at a safe, chattering distance from actually SEEING the image and feeling the dread of the moment itself. That is why the worry cannot be argued away: it is load-bearing. Remove it without replacing the processing beneath (which is what imaginal exposure in CBT does), and the mind reinstalls it within days, the explanation for the failed reassurances and the recurring 2 a.m. queries.

**Story 3: the body pays the bill.** A mind that never lowers the alert keeps adrenaline and muscle tension on retainer: jaws clenched overnight (waking with a heavy head and "sinus pain"), shoulders up (the classic neck bands), gut monitored (each bowel movement interpreted), sleep architecture broken (tired body, racing engine). Patients circle cardiologists (for palpitations), gastroenterologists (for "gas"), and neurologists (for "burning hands and feet"), sometimes for years. Each normal report produces a good evening, then the engine re-runs: "but what did they MISS?"

## 7. Symptoms

**The core psychological picture:**
- Excessive worry about multiple domains: health, family safety, finances, work, small decisions
- Worry that is hard to control once started; the "engine ignition is cheap, the brakes are broken"
- Restlessness, feeling keyed up or on edge ("I cannot sit through a film")
- Fatigue: the tiredness that rest does not clear (worry is shift-work without relief)
- Concentration failure: the mind is fully staffed by the worry department
- Irritability: short fuse at home; the family walks carefully
- Sleep disturbance: difficulty falling asleep (the engine will not idle), unrefreshing sleep, worried early waking

**The somatic translation (how it presents in Indian clinics):**
- Muscle tension: neck, jaw, shoulders, tension-type headaches
- Gastrointestinal: "gas", bloating, acidity, loose stools alternating with constipation (the irritable-gut alliance)
- Cardiovascular-flavoured: palpitations, "my heart is sinking"
- Sensory: "heat in the head", burning hands/feet, tingling, tremor-flavoured sensations
- The patient's opening line is more often "I have gas trouble" than "I worry too much"; the somatic history and the worry history must BOTH be taken

**The worry timeline signature:** present across contexts for years; worsens with transitions (transfers, results, illness in the family); never fully absent even in objectively calm periods ("worrying that I have nothing to worry about").

## 8. Diagnosis

**The logic (DSM-5, paraphrased):** worry that is excessive, hard to control, and spans multiple activities/events, more days than not for at least six months, plus at least three of the six physical symptoms (restlessness, fatigue, concentration problems, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance), causing real impairment, and not better explained by substances, medical illness, or another mental disorder doing the worrying (psychosis, mania, depression, OCD all "worry" differently; see the differential table).

**The interview craft:**
- Use the control question rather than the volume question: "when you start worrying, can you put it down and choose to think about something else?": GAD's signature is the NO
- Map domains on paper: health, money, children, work, future, four-plus domains active clinches the "generalised" gate
- Take the body's history fully (the patient trusts the body line) before translating it into the worry line
- Screen the comorbidity set: PHQ-9 (depression), panic screen ("sudden surges of fear out of the blue?"), alcohol audit ("do you take a drink or a tablet to calm the evenings?")
- Physical workup where indicated: TSH, CBC, fasting glucose; ECG if palpitations are new or the patient is older; caffeine and tea intake quantified honestly (8 cups of chai is its own anxiety disorder)

**Instruments by name:** GAD-7 (the seven-item screen and severity tracker, public-domain scale), HAM-A for structured severity in trials; PSWQ (Penn State Worry Questionnaire) as the worry-specific research tool.

### Differential table

| Looks like GAD | The distinguishing feature |
|---|---|
| Normal everyday worry | Proportional, controllable, resolves with the problem; body recovers |
| Major depression | Low mood and anhedonia lead; worry is gloom-flavoured ("everything is ruined") rather than threat-flavoured ("everything might go wrong"); morning-worse pattern |
| Adjustment disorder | Worry anchored to one identifiable recent stressor, sub-6-month clock |
| Panic disorder | Sudden discrete surges out of the blue vs continuous engine hum |
| Social anxiety | Worry confined to scrutiny/judgment situations |
| OCD | Intrusions are unwanted thoughts/images/urges with rituals; worry themes are stereotyped (contamination, harm, doubt), not free-range |
| Health anxiety | Worry locked to disease themes + reassurance-checking loop |
| Thyrotoxicosis / anaemia / caffeine excess | Physical findings, temporal lock to the medical cause |
| Bipolar anxious presentation | Episodic architecture; screen for highs |
| "White-coat" situational anxiety | Situational only, no six-month engine |

## 9. Management

**The architecture: therapy first or therapy-plus-medicine; benzodiazepines only as short bridges.**

**Step 1: diagnosis shared as physiology.** The most therapeutic first session in GAD explains the alarm-with-no-reset story: "this is not weakness or madness; it is a nervous system stuck in surveillance mode, and that state is trainable and treatable." Patients who understand the machinery stop half the self-blame that maintains the disorder.

**Step 2, CBT (the first-line, durable option):**
- **Worry time**: a scheduled 30-minute daily appointment with worry (the rest of the day's worries get written down and deferred to the slot), starves the engine of its all-day licence
- **Intolerance-of-uncertainty work**: behavioural experiments in tolerating "maybe" (not calling to check, letting the son travel untracked, ordering without researching), rebuilding the prefrontal brake
- **Imaginal exposure**: deliberately facing the feared image (the job-loss scene, the biopsy scene) in session until its charge drops; this is the piece that replaces the worry-shield and makes gains permanent
- **Relaxation training**: progressive muscle relaxation, breathing retraining (slow exhale-weighted), applied relaxation
- **Worry records and the "worry outcome diary"**: most predicted catastrophes never occur; the data collected on paper beats the argument
- 10–14 sessions typical; effects persist after therapy ends (unlike medicines), the honest selling point

**Step 3, pharmacotherapy (first-line drugs):**
- **SSRIs**: sertraline 50–150 mg, escitalopram 10–20 mg, paroxetine 20–50 mg (with paroxetine's approval status in GAD in various markets), full trials 8–12 weeks; early jitter handled with reassurance and dose-splitting, not abandonment
- **SNRI**: venlafaxine XR 75–225 mg, the best-studied GAD antidepressant; pregabalin 150–600 mg/day an alternative with faster anxiolysis (and its own misuse considerations)
- **Buspirone**: 15–60 mg, an old, honest second-line; no dependence, no sedation, slow onset, useful where substance risk looms
- **Maintenance**: 12 months minimum after response; taper slowly; restart early-warning signs (sleep first)
- **Benzodiazepines, the trap clause**: clonazepam/alprazolam calm the evening beautifully, which is exactly the problem: the drug "works" while the engine keeps running, tolerance builds, and the patient now has two problems. Legitimate use: the first 1–3 weeks of SSRI initiation, dose falling as the antidepressant takes hold; detox plans for the dependent (see Benzodiazepine Misuse note)

**Step 4, the comorbidities treated in parallel:** depression (SSRI dose handled for both), panic (CBT interoceptive module), alcohol (the evening-quiet plan; see Alcohol note), insomnia (sleep hygiene as a separate prescription, not as an alprazolam chit).

*India note (approx 2026): sertraline 100 mg ≈ ₹80–160/month; escitalopram 10 mg ≈ ₹70–150/month; venlafaxine XR 75 mg ≈ ₹200–400/month; pregabalin ≈ ₹150–300/month; buspirone ≈ ₹100–200/month; CBT private ≈ ₹600–1,500/session, district-psychologist and Tele-MANAS (14416) options at nominal cost. The Indian reality check: alprazolam and clonazepam cost less than a cup of tea and are dispensed like it; the country's GAD problem is significantly a benzo-prescription problem; writing the SSRI + bridge + review plan (three minutes more than a benzo chit) is the single highest-yield GAD practice upgrade in Indian primary care.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **The somatic front door protocol.** Every "gas/headache/palpitations/burning" circuit through medicine OPDs should carry a two-question turnstile: "how is your sleep?" and "when you start worrying, can you stop at will?" Positive answers redirect the file toward the anxiety pathway before the tenth normal report.
- **The tension label.** "Tension hai" is the Indian folk-diagnosis of GAD; accurate in spirit, lethal for help-seeking, because "everyone has tension" normalises what is actually a treatable engine disorder. Clinician's counter-frame: "everyone has tension; not everyone has tension THAT DOES NOT SWITCH OFF, that eats sleep and burns the body; that second kind is what we treat."
- **Families as worry-responders.** Indian joint families organise around the patient's worry: sons call twice daily from other cities, a daughter-in-law's phone is tracked, the household plans around "what she will think". Without briefing, families become the reassurance-machine that GAD requires and discards. The family module: reassurance REDUCED deliberately ("we have answered this exact question; we will answer it again tomorrow in worry time"), worry-deflection to the written record, calm presence over information delivery.
- **Benzodiazepine culture.** The "nerve tablet" is a household object in much of India, borrowed across neighbours, escalated by pharmacists, and continued for decades. The GAD consult is the moment to name the trade (the evening for the decade), offer the bridge plan, and where dependence already exists, route to the taper pathway with dignity.
- **Workplace and exam machinery.** High-uncertainty Indian stressors (transfers, results, appraisals) are unavoidable triggers; teach the worry-time and uncertainty practices BEFORE the season, in the same way vaccination precedes the epidemic. EAP-style counselling in IT/PSU sectors is a delivery vehicle for CBT-capable referral; know your city's list.
- **Yoga and breathing practices.** Slow-exhale-weighted pranayama and yoga modules have supportive trial evidence in Indian anxiety populations as adjuncts; prescribe them as adjuncts with the same specificity ("15 minutes, this sequence, this time daily") as tablets; vague "do yoga" advice is not prescription.

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: the engineer who visited four specialists.** A 34-year-old IT engineer in Hyderabad presented to gastroenterology for two years of "gas and acidity" with normal endoscopies, then cardiology for palpitations (normal ECG and echo), then neurology for "burning feet" (normal nerve studies). Each visit bought a week of relief. The somatic-turnstile questions turned positive at the fourth door: sleep 4 hours, "the moment I lie down the thoughts start", worry across health-money-parents-appraisal domains, uncontrollable by his own report ("I can start, doctor; I cannot stop"). Diagnosis: GAD. Management: the physiology explanation first (his relief was visible: "so my stomach is a victim, not the criminal"), sertraline 50→100 mg with a 10-day clonazepam bridge falling off as the SSRI engaged, CBT package (worry time, the outcome diary, imaginal exposure to the appraisal scene he had never let himself picture), and his wife briefed on the reduced-reassurance protocol. At 12 weeks: sleep 6.5 hours, one specialist retained (none). Teaching points: (1) the somatic circuit is the GAD referral pathway, interrupt it with two questions; (2) "I can start but cannot stop" is the diagnostic sentence worth eliciting verbatim; (3) the family's reassurance is a symptom-feeder, and reducing it is a treatment.

**Case 2: the mother of two on alprazolam for eleven years.** A 49-year-old Ludhiana homemaker had taken alprazolam 0.5 mg three times daily for eleven years, originally prescribed after her husband's cardiac scare, refilled by a family pharmacy without a fresh prescription. Evenings were manageable; mornings were dread; she had stopped driving ("what if I faint with the children in the car"), screens her daughters' phones, and described herself as "a useless frightened woman". Diagnosis: GAD with benzodiazepine dependence. Management: staged; the honest conversation (the tablet was treating evenings, not the engine, and had quietly become a second illness), transfer to a slow diazepam-equivalent taper over 8 weeks (see the Benzodiazepine note's architecture), escitalopram 10 mg built up during the taper, CBT's uncertainty-exposure experiments sequenced to the taper (driving returned at week 6, daughters' phones untracked at week 10), husband taught the non-reassurance response. At 6 months: off benzodiazepines, one relapse-week managed without restarting, driving restored, and her line at review: "I have not been this person since my thirties." Teaching points: (1) long-benzo GAD is the Indian default presentation, and the taper-plus-SSRI-plus-CBT sequence reverses a decade; (2) graded behavioural experiments (driving, phones) are the visible currency of recovery; (3) spouse-training converts the household's reassurance economy into the recovery economy.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way patients and families ask it

**"Everyone has tension. Why is mine an illness?"** Everyone has tension; not everyone has an alarm system that never switches off. When worry spreads to every domain, cannot be put down even for a film, eats your sleep and runs your body hot for months; that is a distinct, named, treatable condition. Calling it "just tension" is like calling a fever "just warmth".

**"Is this a mental illness, am I going mad?"** No madness is involved. GAD is a calibration problem of the threat-detection system, closer to a smoke alarm set too sensitively than to any loss of mind. People with GAD are typically too sane: they see every possible danger, all day.

**"Why can't I just stop worrying? I try."** Because in this condition the STOP circuit is the part that is weak, not the START. Willpower applied to a broken brake fails predictably, which is why the treatment trains the brake (structured uncertainty practice, exposure) rather than shouting at the engine.

**"All my reports are normal. So why do I still feel something is wrong?"** The reports are normal because GAD is not a disease of the organs it alarms; it is the alarm itself. The symptoms you feel are real (the body pays the worry's bill in muscle tension, gut and sleep currency); their SOURCE is the nervous system's set-point, and that is exactly what treatment adjusts.

**"The doctor gave an antidepressant. I am not depressed."** Antidepressant medicines work in anxiety disorders at their own doses, they quiet the over-set alarm. Being prescribed one in GAD does not mean you are depressed; it means the same chemistry that calms depression also calms the threat system. Expect 2–4 weeks, and tell us about early jitter.

**"The alprazolam worked immediately. Why not just continue it?"** It works immediately because it sedates the alarm; but it does not recalibrate it. Over months the body adjusts, the tablet wears off sooner, the dose creeps, and the engine still runs underneath. There is a proper role for it in the first weeks, as a bridge while the real treatment builds. As the destination, it is a trap with a soft carpet.

**"Will I need medicines lifelong?"** Most people do not. A full treatment season, 12 months of medicine combined with the therapy, then a planned slow taper, with a written early-warning list (sleep shrinking is the first), handles most cases. Some with strong family loading relapse and choose long-term maintenance; that is a decision, not a defeat.

**"My wife keeps asking the same worries and I keep answering. Is that right?"** Natural, loving, and unfortunately feeding the loop. We will teach you the counterintuitive version: answer fully ONCE, log the question, redirect repeats to the next day's worry time, and hold her through the discomfort instead of rescuing her from it. Rescuing soothes the minute and strengthens the machine.

**"Can children get this?"** Yes: over-worrying children (stomach-aches on school mornings, "what if" marathons, sleep fears) are often little GAD engines, and early CBT-style work can re-set the trajectory before adulthood. It is temperament plus learning, and both are trainable.

**"Is it hereditary? My mother was the same."** Partly. About a third of the tendency is inherited; the sensitive alarm is a family trait, but the maintenance habits (checking, reassurance-seeking, avoidance) are learned in such households and are exactly what therapy unlearns.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**Gates to memorise:** 6 months, more days than not; 3+ of the 6 physical symptoms (restlessness, fatigue, concentration, irritability, muscle tension, sleep); uncontrollability is the core discriminator from ordinary worry.

**Mnemonic for the six physical symptoms, "R FCI MS":** Restlessness, Fatigue, Concentration, Irritability, Muscle tension, Sleep (read as "Restless, Fatigued, Cant-concentrate, Irritable, Muscle-bound, Sleepless").

**High-yield facts:**
- Female: male ~2:1; onset before 30 typically; chronic waxing-waning course
- Comorbidity rule: depression eventually in the majority of treated patients; screen every visit
- Venlafaxine XR = best-studied SNRI in GAD; pregabalin alternative; buspirone for dependence-risk
- CBT effects persist post-treatment; drug effects fade off-drug: the trial-comparison answer
- GAD's medical mimics: thyrotoxicosis, caffeine, anaemia, hypoglycaemia
- GAD-7 = the public-domain screen; know its use, not its items
- Dementia in the elderly can present as "new late-life GAD", a geriatric trap

**Classic traps:**
1. Diagnosing GAD in depression whose rumination is gloom-flavoured; check mood, anhedonia, diurnal variation
2. Missing the panic question: "surges out of the blue" flips the plan
3. Accepting the patient's somatic frame and repeating the specialist carousel
4. Lifelong benzodiazepines as "management": the highest-frequency Indian prescribing error
5. Stopping the SSRI at week 4 because "no effect"; GAD responds slower than depression; commit to 8–12 week trials
6. Forgetting the alcohol audit in the evening-calmer

**Indian exam corner:** "GAD, diagnostic criteria and management" (evergreen), " differentiate GAD from panic disorder and adjustment disorder", "pharmacotherapy of anxiety disorders, first line and why", "yoga as adjunct in anxiety, evidence" (the Indian-context bonus). NMHS 2015–16's neurotic-disorders bucket and the treatment-gap figure are quotable marks.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. The single best discriminator of GAD worry from normal worry at the bedside:**
A. Worry about health
B. Difficulty stopping the worry once started
C. Worry during the night

**Answer: B.** Uncontrollability is the core gate; domain and timing overlap with normal worry.

A. 1 month, 1 symptom
B. 3 months, 2 symptoms
C. 6 months, 3 symptoms
D. 12 months, 4 symptoms
**Answer: C.** Six months, more days than not, plus 3 of the 6 physical symptoms.

**3. First-line pharmacological options for GAD include all EXCEPT:**
A. Sertraline
B. Venlafaxine XR
C. Escitalopram
D. Alprazolam as long-term monotherapy
**Answer: D.** Benzodiazepines are bridges, not destinations, in GAD, long-term monotherapy deepens the problem.

**4. The CBT component that most directly replaces the worry-shield function is:**
A. Worry time
B. Imaginal exposure to the feared core image
C. Relaxation training

**Answer: B.** Exposure processes the emotional material worry keeps at word-level distance, the durable-gain component.

**5. A patient with heat intolerance, weight loss and anxiety. Before diagnosing GAD:**
A. Start an SSRI
B. Check thyroid function
C. Begin CBT
D. Add a benzodiazepine
**Answer: B.** Thyrotoxicosis is the classic medical mimic; exclude before labelling.

**6. The most common comorbidity that eventually develops in GAD:**
A. Schizophrenia
B. Major depression
C. OCD
D. Dementia
**Answer: B.** The depression-comorbidity rule: screen every GAD visit.

**7. The Indian primary-care pattern most likely to maintain GAD for a decade:**
A. Referral to CBT within 6 months
B. Standing benzodiazepine refills from pharmacies without review
C. GAD-7 screening at OPD turnstiles

**Answer: B.** The over-the-counter "nerve tablet" economy suppresses symptoms while the engine and the dependence grow.

**8. In CBT's worry-time prescription, worries arising outside the slot are:**
A. Argued away immediately
B. Written down and deferred to the next scheduled worry period
C. Suppressed by relaxation

**Answer: B.** Written and deferred: the containment that starves the engine of its all-day licence.
