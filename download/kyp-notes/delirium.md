# Delirium

## 1. In one line + overview

**Tagline:** Delirium is a sudden clouding of the brain caused by physical illness, drugs or surgery; a **medical emergency** that is frequently mistaken for dementia, depression or "madness", and that often clears when the underlying cause is found and treated.

**Overview, in plain words:** A person who was orientated yesterday becomes confused tonight, asking the same question again and again, not recognising family at 3 a.m., pulling out IV lines, or sometimes just going quiet and drowsy. That is delirium: an **acute organ failure of the brain**, as real and as urgent as kidney failure. It affects roughly **one in five general hospital inpatients**, and up to half of intensive-care and post-operative patients. It is **not a psychiatric illness in origin**, but psychiatrists are called because the symptoms look psychiatric. The single most important clinical message: **the confusion is a signal, not the disease**. Behind it there is almost always something treatable, an infection, a metabolic upset, a drug, a retained urine, or withdrawal from alcohol. Families should know three things: it is common, it is not insanity, and the best "medicine" is often a familiar face, daylight, hearing aids and spectacles, not restraints.

## 2. Learning objectives

After working through this note, you should be able to:

1. Define delirium in one sentence and explain why it is a medical emergency, not a psychiatric curiosity.
2. Distinguish **hyperactive, hypoactive and mixed** delirium, and state why hypoactive delirium is the dangerous, missed one.
3. Apply a bedside screening approach (CAM / 4AT, named, not reproduced) in under two minutes.
4. Separate delirium from dementia and from depression using onset, course and attention.
5. Build a cause checklist using the **predisposing-vulnerability plus precipitating-insult** model.
6. Outline the investigations every delirium workup must include, and when to image, do an EEG, or a lumbar puncture.
7. Manage delirium: treat the cause, manage the environment, and use antipsychotic drugs only when you must, with the doses.
8. Explain the prognosis honestly: what usually happens at 1 week, 1 month and 6 months.

## 4. Epidemiology

### Global

- **10–30% of general medical inpatients** develop delirium at some point in their admission; the widely quoted figure is roughly one in five.
- Intensive care: **30–75%**, especially ventilated patients and the elderly.
- Post-operative: after hip fracture surgery, up to **50% of older patients**; after major cardiac surgery, 30–50%.
- Terminal illness: up to **80% of people in the last days of life**; "terminal agitation" is usually delirium.
- Mortality is substantially raised: in-hospital mortality is high, and 6–12-month mortality of hospitalised delirium approaches that of acute myocardial infarction. This is why it must never be dismissed as "just confusion".

### India

- No national prevalence figure exists; hospital-based studies from Indian medical colleges consistently find that **the majority of cases are missed** until severe, because a quiet, drowsy patient is labelled "tired" and a restless one is labelled "agitated", not delirious.
- Common Indian precipitants reported in hospital series: **sepsis (urinary, chest, abdominal), electrolyte imbalance (low sodium especially), hepatic encephalopathy, tuberculosis of the brain, malaria, stroke, uncontrolled diabetes, and post-operative states**.
- Indian family practice paradox: a family member usually stays with the patient 24 hours a day, which is a *protective* factor most Western guidelines try to engineer with volunteers. We already have it; we just need to use it deliberately (reorientation, familiar voice, day-night structure).
- Alcohol withdrawal delirium (delirium tremens) is common where alcohol use is high and admission is late; it remains a frequent reason for psychiatry referral in district hospitals.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

Think of a **two-hit model**: a vulnerable brain (predisposing) + an insult (precipitating). The more vulnerable the brain, the smaller the insult needed; an 85-year-old with dementia can become delirious from a change of room or a dose of cough syrup.

**Predisposing (the vulnerability):**

- Biological: **old age** (the biggest single risk), pre-existing **dementia or mild cognitive impairment**, prior stroke or Parkinson's disease, frailty, malnutrition, dehydration.
- Sensory: poor vision (cataract), poor hearing (wax, presbyacusis); each doubles the risk; both together are worse.
- Genetic/environmental: male sex in some series; long ward stays; intensive care; bladder catheter in situ.
- Indian context: chronic malnutrition and anaemia in the elderly, untreated cataracts and hearing loss, and late presentation of infections all raise baseline vulnerability.

**Precipitating (the insult):**

- **Drugs** (the most reversible cause): opioids, benzodiazepines, anticholinergics (bladder drugs, tricyclics, some antiemetics and cold remedies), steroids, antiparkinsonian drugs, sudden withdrawal of long-term benzodiazepines or alcohol.
- **Infection:** urinary tract (commonest in the elderly, sometimes without pain or fever), chest, skin, abdomen, malaria, tuberculosis, and any sepsis.
- **Metabolic:** low or high sodium, high or low glucose, calcium upset, kidney failure, liver failure (hepatic encephalopathy), thyroid disorders, vitamin B1 (thiamine) and B12 deficiency.
- **Brain events:** stroke (especially right parietal), seizure or post-ictal state, head injury, encephalitis, brain tumours with raised pressure.
- **Surgery and anaesthesia:** especially hip, cardiac and prolonged procedures.
- **Local discomfort:** urinary retention, severe constipation/impaction, pain, a full bladder with a blocked catheter; the classic "the patient is confused because nobody asked when he last passed urine".
- **Environment:** unfamiliar ward, no daylight, no clock, no glasses, no hearing aid, sleep deprivation, restraint use.

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: the seesaw of acetylcholine and dopamine.** Deep in the brainstem, two chemical systems keep each other in balance: acetylcholine keeps us alert, focused and orientated; dopamine pushes arousal. Most causes of delirium (low oxygen, low glucose, toxins, inflammation) **tip the seesaw towards acetylcholine failure and relative dopamine excess**. That is why the delirious brain misperceives (acetylcholine failing) and then builds vivid hallucinations and paranoia (dopamine unchecked), and why dopamine-blocking drugs (haloperidol, quetiapine) calm the storm while the real cause is being fixed.

**Story 2: inflammation at the gate.** The brain is guarded by the blood–brain barrier. In severe infection, surgery or trauma, the body's inflammatory signals (cytokines) cross or signal through this gate and switch on the brain's immune cells, the **microglia**. Activated microglia disturb synapses, the connections between neurons, and effectively "brown-out" the highest, newest functions first: attention, orientation, and the filter that tells dream from waking. This is why a chest infection in the toes can produce hallucinations in the cortex.

**Story 3: the sleep–wake collapse.** Delirium always attacks the brain's clock and the switch between sleeping and waking. As the switch fails, dream imagery leaks into waking hours (worse at dusk; "sundowning"), attention fades in waves, and nights become wild while days become sleepy. Families often notice day–night reversal *before* any doctor notices the confusion.

## 7. Symptoms

Group the picture into four clusters: **attention, cognition, arousal/behaviour, and the biological clock.**

**The core, attention collapses:**

- Cannot hold attention: drifts off mid-sentence, must repeat questions, follows a finger for a few seconds and loses it.
- Easily distracted by irrelevant sounds; cannot filter the ward noise.
- Awareness of surroundings waxing and waning, the same question asked thirty times in one hour.

**Cognition:**

- Disorientation, usually to **time first**, then place, later to person.
- Immediate and recent memory fail (he cannot remember being examined a minute ago); old memories stay relatively intact, which is why he talks about 1975 as if it were yesterday.
- Rambling, incoherent speech; mistaking the nurse for a dead relative; ideas of reference (the TV is about him); fleeting, poorly-formed delusions; **visual hallucinations** (small animals, children, people at the window), classically at dusk or night.
- Misinterpretations: the drip becomes a snake; the ward becomes a bus station he is trying to leave.

**Arousal and behaviour, the three faces:**

- **Hyperactive** (15–45%): restless, shouting, plucking at sheets, pulling catheters, trying to climb out of bed, paranoid, not sleeping. Everyone notices; usually gets treated (sometimes overtreated).
- **Hypoactive** (roughly half): quiet, drowsy, withdrawn, slow to answer, staring, off food, "depressed". Nobody notices; carries the **worst prognosis** because it is missed and the underlying cause festers.
- **Mixed**: swings between the two, hyperactive at 2 a.m., flat all day. Most common of all.

**The clock:**

- Day–night reversal, fragmented sleep, worse symptoms at dusk ("sundowning").
- Autonomic signs in severe cases: sweating, tremor, fast pulse, flushing, typical of withdrawal states.

**Course:** onset is **hours to days**, symptoms **fluctuate through the day** and are almost always worse at night. This fluctuation is the signature; a dementia does not swing from "oriented" to "seeing cats" within eight hours.

## 8. Diagnosis

### The logic of DSM-5 / ICD-11 (paraphrased)

DSM-5 (and ICD-11, in similar wording) builds delirium from four ideas: **(1)** attention and awareness are disturbed: the person cannot focus, sustain or shift attention; **(2)** the disturbance begins abruptly (hours–days), represents a change from baseline, and **fluctuates** through the day; **(3)** at least one more domain of cognition is affected, memory, orientation, language, perception; **(4)** there is direct evidence from history, examination or tests of a medical cause (illness, drug, withdrawal). It explicitly rules out explaining everything by a pre-existing dementia, though dementia and delirium very often coexist.

### Bedside screening

- **CAM (Confusion Assessment Method):** trained staff use it in about 2 minutes; it asks about acute onset-fluctuation, inattention, disorganised thinking and altered arousal. (Named here; items are not reproduced.)
- **4AT:** four quick checks including an attention test and arousal assessment, usable by any junior doctor or nurse without special training.
- **DRS-R-98:** a severity instrument used in research and audit.
- Informal but honest test: ask the person to **say the months backwards or days of the week backwards**. A person who can do that in hospital at night is almost certainly not delirious.

### Workup: every delirium deserves this minimum

- History from family: baseline function, exact onset, drug list (including over-the-counter and eye drops), alcohol and benzodiazepine history, recent falls.
- Examinations: vitals and oxygen saturation, hydration, chest, abdomen (bladder, bowel), skin (pressure sores, cellulitis), neurological including fundoscopy.
- Bloods: complete blood count, sugar, urea/creatinine, **electrolytes (sodium first among equals)**, calcium, liver function, thyroid, B12, and blood cultures if febrile.
- Urine: dipstick and culture, but remember, pyuria alone in an elderly patient can mislead; treat the patient, not the dipstick.
- ECG; consider troponin.
- Imaging: CT/MRI brain if head injury, focal neurological signs, fever with neck stiffness, first seizure, anticoagulated patient, or no cause found.
- EEG (rarely needed but decisive in doubt): **diffuse slowing** confirms delirium and helps exclude non-convulsive status epilepticus.
- Lumbar puncture when encephalitis or meningitis is on the table (fever, confusion out of proportion, meningism, immunosuppression).

### Differential table

| Feature | Delirium | Dementia | Depression |
|---------|----------|----------|------------|
| Onset | Hours–days, often exact clock time | Months–years | Weeks–months |
| Course | Fluctuates through the day, worse at night | Slowly progressive, steady | Persistently low, may vary with the day |
| Attention | **Globally impaired early** | Intact until later stages | Mildly reduced ("can't concentrate") |
| Consciousness | Clouded; drowsy or hyperalert | Clear until very late | Clear |
| Hallucinations | Common, visual | Late, if any | Usually absent; may hear critical voices |
| Speech | Rambling, incoherent | Word-finding difficulty | Slowed but coherent |
| Reversibility | Often largely reversible | Not reversible | Usually very treatable |

**Memory aid:** *"Sudden confusion = delirium until proven otherwise; and delirium = something physical until proven otherwise."*

## 9. Management

### Step 1: find and treat the cause (the only definitive treatment)

Recheck the drug chart and **stop every non-essential, especially anticholinergic, drug**. Treat infection. Correct sodium, glucose, oxygen, hydration. Relieve urinary retention and impacted stool. Manage pain (paracetamol first; minimise opioids). In withdrawal states, give benzodiazepines properly (this is the exception to the "avoid benzodiazepines" rule). Thiamine before any glucose in malnourished or alcoholic patients.

### Step 2: environmental and supportive management (works for everyone)

- One **familiar family member** present, day and night if possible; in India this is our greatest structural advantage.
- Restore the senses: **spectacles on, hearing aids in, dentures in**. Remove earwax.
- Reorientate repeatedly: introduce yourself each time, a clock and calendar visible, daylight through the window by day, dim light at night.
- Protect sleep: no routine night observations unless essential; cluster night care; minimise noise; warm milk beats sedatives.
- Mobilise by day; avoid bed rest orders unless mandatory.
- **Avoid catheters, restraints and "cage beds".** Restraint deepens the fear and the confusion.
- Treat the fear: a calm voice, sitting at eye level, short sentences, one instruction at a time. Never argue with the hallucination; orient gently instead.

*India note: none of the above costs money beyond the family's time. In district hospitals this bundle is often more effective than any prescription.*

### Step 3, drugs: only when distress or danger demands

Use the **lowest dose for the shortest time**:

- **Haloperidol** 0.25–0.5 mg orally (repeat after 30–60 min if needed; max ~2–3 mg/day in the elderly): cheap, available everywhere; watch for rigidity, dystonia, QT prolongation.
- **Olanzapine** 2.5 mg or **risperidone** 0.25–0.5 mg, alternatives with similar caution.
- **Quetiapine** 12.5–25 mg at night: preferred where parkinsonism or Lewy body dementia is suspected (see the DLB note; haloperidol can be catastrophic there).
- **Avoid benzodiazepines** except for withdrawal states (alcohol, benzodiazepine withdrawal; catatonia). Lorazepam 0.5–1 mg is the usual dose there.
- In the last days of life, titrate comfort over clarity; palliative sedation is sometimes the kindest treatment.

*India note: haloperidol tablets cost only a few rupees per tablet, so the real cost of delirium care is the investigation for the cause and the family's time, not the medication. Where quetiapine is needed, generic 25 mg tablets keep the total under ₹100–150 per course (approx, 2026, varies by brand and scheme).*

### Prevention (better than cure)

Multicomponent prevention programmes (nurse-led protocols: reorientation, sleep protection, early mobilisation, hydration, sensory aids, removing deliriogenic drugs) cut delirium rates by about a third in trials and cost almost nothing to adapt to Indian wards. For high-risk elective surgery patients, that list should start in the pre-operative clinic, not post-operatively.

## 10. Indian practice

- **Where it is seen:** general medical, surgical and ICUs of district hospitals; post-operative orthopaedic wards (hip fracture, prostate surgery); medical wards managing decompensated liver disease; and palliative care. Psychiatry is usually consulted only when the patient is agitated, the hypoactive cases almost never reach us.
- **The referral problem:** many patients are first labelled by families as having "gone mad", or given sedatives by the family doctor at night. The antidote is simple teaching: any abrupt change in thinking or behaviour is a **medical** event.
- **Government pathway:** there is no specific national delirium programme, but delirium care piggybacks on whatever programme owns the precipitating illness (TB, malaria, diabetes, stroke). The elderly patient with delirium in a government facility is managed under general geriatric services where they exist, otherwise general medicine.
- **Private practice reality:** families of elderly patients at home (post-discharge) frequently ask for night sedation. The safer counselling is structured sleep hygiene and a review of every evening tablet (many "sleep" syrups are anticholinergic).
- **Primary care role:** the MBBS doctor in a clinic can make this diagnosis with a calendar, a good collateral history and the months-backwards test, then arrange the workup, no psychiatrist needed for the diagnosis itself.
- **Family education in one minute:** "The brain has caught a fever of thinking, the same way the body catches a fever. It is common after serious illness and operations. It is not insanity. We are treating the cause; he needs your voice, his glasses, daylight, and his sleep protected. It usually improves over days to weeks; tell us if it does not."

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: the night the family thought he was possessed.** A 74-year-old retired schoolteacher, three days after prostate surgery, began at 11 p.m. to insist he was at a bus stand in his home town, tried to climb over the cot rails, and asked the same question ("when does the bus come?") every two minutes. The family considered spiritual remedies; the night nurse gave diazepam; he became more confused. Morning review found a **distended bladder with a blocked catheter**; released, he slept, and by evening was orientated except for mild patchiness that cleared over three days.

*Teaching points: (1) post-operative evening confusion is delirium until proven otherwise; (2) a blocked catheter is a five-minute cure; (3) benzodiazepines made it worse, as they usually do outside withdrawal states.*

**Case 2: the quiet one who was labelled depressed.** A 68-year-old widow with mild diabetes, admitted with a chest infection, became "cooperative and sleepy", ate little, and was noted to be "calm and withdrawn, probably depression". The resident who asked her to name the days of the week backwards found she could not get past Tuesday. Sodium was 118 mmol/L. With slow sodium correction the quietness resolved; the "depression" disappeared with the electrolyte.

*Teaching points: (1) hypoactive delirium hides inside "poor appetite" and "withdrawal"; (2) any new psychiatric label in a medical inpatient requires an attention test first; (3) in the elderly, hyponatraemia and silent urinary infection are the two commonest reversible causes in Indian series.*

## 13. FAQ

**"Doctor, has he gone mad?"**
No. His brain is temporarily failing to work clearly because the body is ill. It is as physical as a fever. When the underlying illness is treated, the confusion usually improves.

**"Why is he worse at night?"**
The brain's sleep–wake switch is part of what is failing. Dream imagery starts leaking into the evening hours; this is common and it is temporary, not permanent insanity.

**"He keeps seeing people who are not there. Is that schizophrenia?"**
Visual hallucinations in a medically ill older person are almost always delirium (or a dementia). Schizophrenia begins young and is dominated by voices and fixed beliefs, not sudden visual misperceptions with fever or infection.

**"Will it come back?"**
An episode of delirium makes future episodes more likely, especially with another illness or surgery. Tell every surgeon who operates on this patient, prevention protocols exist.

**"Should we tie him down so he does not pull the drip?"**
Restraints usually increase the struggle and the fear, and they worsen outcomes. A person sitting with him, plus treating the cause, works better. Restraint is a last resort under medical orders, loosened as early as possible.

**"Can you give something so he sleeps at night?"**
A quiet, dark, warm night with a familiar face works better than sleeping tablets in delirium. Sedatives can deepen the confusion. If a drug is truly needed, it is chosen carefully and stopped as early as possible.

**"He was fine in two days. Is he completely back to normal now?"**
Often the person is much better but not fully himself for weeks; subtle problems with memory and concentration can persist. Older patients, and those with dementia, recover slowest.

**"Is delirium the same as dementia?"**
No. Dementia comes on slowly over years and is not caused by a treatable infection. Delirium comes on in hours to days and is usually reversible when its cause is found. They can coexist, and delirium is often the first alarm bell that a dementia has begun.

**"What do we tell the relatives at home after discharge?"**
Give one line to repeat: "Any sudden confusion means a physical problem; take him to a doctor the same day, do not start sedatives on your own."

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **Mnemonics for causes:** *I WATCH DEATH*, **I**nfection, **W**ithdrawal, **A**cute metabolic, **T**rauma, **C**NS pathology, **H**ypoxia, **D**eficiencies (B1, B12), **E**ndocrine, **A**cute vascular, **T**oxins/drugs, **H**eavy metals. (Or build your own: the habit of *forcing a full checklist* is the exam point.)
- **Highest-yield single fact:** fluctuating attention. Fluctuation + acute onset = delirium, whatever the hallucinations look like.
- **Classic traps:** (1) the quiet elderly patient labelled "depressed"; test attention; (2) an elderly patient's new agitation blamed on "psychosis" and treated with high-dose antipsychotics; treat the cause; (3) giving benzodiazepines for night-time agitation in a non-withdrawal patient, worsening confusion; (4) forgetting the urinary catheter, the blocked bowel, the pain.
- **Classic vivas:** differentiate delirium/dementia/depression (know the table cold); causes of post-operative delirium; why haloperidol is used and its risks; EEG finding in delirium (diffuse slow activity); why the elderly are prone (cholinergic reserve is thin).
- **Indian exam corner:** hepatic encephalopathy as a common psychiatric consultation; delirium tremens management (parenteral thiamine before glucose, benzodiazepines, and watch autonomic storms); cerebral malaria and TB meningitis as causes of acute confusion; and the ethical point that "tying down" confused elderly patients is restraint, not care.
- **One-line definitions to rehearse:** delirium = acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness due to a medical condition or substance; hypoactive delirium = the quiet subtype with worse outcomes and highest miss rate; sundowning = worsening of confusion at dusk and night.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. The single feature that most reliably separates delirium from dementia is:**
A. Visual hallucinations
B. Acute onset and fluctuation of attention
C. Age above 70
D. Night-time worsening

**Answer: B.** Hallucinations and night-time worsening occur in both; the defining difference is acutely disturbed, fluctuating attention.

**2. A 78-year-old woman, admitted with pneumonia, is quiet, drowsy and eating little. The most useful next bedside step is:**
A. Start an antidepressant
B. Ask her to recite the days of the week backwards
C. Order an MRI brain
D. Refer for psychotherapy

**Answer: B.** The picture is classic hypoactive delirium; a 30-second attention task flags it so the search for the cause can begin.

**3. Which drug class can precipitate delirium even when taken as prescribed?**
A. Paracetamol
B. Anticholinergics (bladder drugs, some antiemetics, tricyclics)
C. Plain insulin
D. Calcium tablets

**Answer: B.** Anticholinergic burden is among the commonest and most reversible causes, especially in the elderly.

**4. In delirium due to alcohol withdrawal, the correct first-line drug is:**
A. Haloperidol alone
B. A benzodiazepine (plus thiamine)
C. Quetiapine
D. Amitriptyline

**Answer: B.** Withdrawal states are the exception where benzodiazepines treat the mechanism; thiamine must precede any glucose load.

**5. An 80-year-old man with known dementia of Lewy body type develops post-operative agitation. Which should be avoided?**
A. Quetiapine in low dose
B. Environmental reorientation
C. Haloperidol intramuscularly
D. Treating urinary retention

**Answer: C.** Patients with Lewy body disease can have severe, even life-threatening reactions to dopamine-blocking drugs like haloperidol (see the DLB note).

**6. The typical EEG finding in delirium is:**
A. Normal record
B. Generalised slow activity
C. Triphasic waves only
D. Epileptiform spikes in the temporal region

**Answer: B.** Diffuse slowing reflects widespread cortical dysfunction; triphasic waves are a special subset (classically metabolic), and a normal EEG argues strongly against delirium.
