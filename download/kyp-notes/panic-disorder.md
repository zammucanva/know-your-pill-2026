# Panic Disorder & Agoraphobia

## 1. In one line + overview

**A panic attack is a false alarm: a sudden surge of raw physical fear (pounding heart, breathlessness, dizziness, doom) peaking within minutes; panic disorder is what develops when the mind starts fearing the alarm itself, scanning for the next one and restructuring life around avoiding it; agoraphobia is the advanced stage; fear of places from which escape would be hard if an attack came.**

The panic attack itself is not an illness, half the population experiences a few in a lifetime, and they are harmless even when terrifying. The disorder begins with the interpretation: "my heart is racing; this must be a heart attack / I am losing my mind / I will die here". That catastrophic reading converts a bodily event into a threat, which fires more adrenaline, which produces stronger sensations, the vicious circle that psychiatry calls the panic cycle. Fear of the next attack produces hypervigilant body-scanning (which finds normal sensations to misread), anticipatory dread, and escape behaviour. Left untreated, the world shrinks: first the bus ("what if it happens where I cannot get out"), then queues, then malls, then leaving home alone; agoraphobia, an old Greek word that India has filled with new content: autos avoided, temple queues abandoned, the wife who has not crossed the gate alone in five years. The good news is the best in this whole book: panic disorder is among the most treatable conditions in psychiatry; brief structured therapy cures a large share outright, SSRIs work well, and the treatment is literally the practice of feeling the alarm ring until it bores the brain.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:

- Explain agoraphobia's five situation categories and its decoupling from panic in DSM-5

- Use SSRI first-line pharmacotherapy, dose titration, and the jitter window honestly
- Exclude the mimics: cardiac, thyroid, caffeine, asthma, vestibular, substance withdrawal

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Lifetime prevalence of panic disorder ~2–4%; panic attacks without the disorder far commoner; agoraphobia ~1–2% (and, since DSM-5, diagnosable without panic history). Women roughly twice as often as men; onset typically late teens through thirties (childhood and late-life onset occur and are commonly missed). Course without treatment is chronic and relapsing, with major secondary depression and benzodiazepine/alcohol self-treatment; with modern treatment, the majority improve substantially and many remit fully. Comorbidity set: depression, other anxiety disorders, substance misuse, and, an Indian clinical constant, repeated cardiology workups.

**India.** NMHS 2015–16 pooled anxiety disorders (~3% of adults, treatment gap ~70–80%). The Indian panic patient's route to a psychiatrist passes through: the ECG in the emergency room (often multiple), the cardiologist's echo/TMT, the gastroenterologist for "gas", sometimes the neurologist for "vertigo", and the temple, before anyone asks the diagnostic question. Two Indian distortions to teach every trainee: (1) the cardiology circuit, palpitations send the patient to cardiac doors first, and each normal ECG produces relief for exactly one evening before the next attack re-ignites the fear; (2) the housebound homemaker presentation: agoraphobic women in joint families are absorbed as "weak-nerved" household fixtures, invisible to clinical systems for decades.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**Biological.**
- Heritability ~30–40%; the alarm system (locus coeruleus and noradrenergic circuitry) is constitutionally jumpy in panic pedigrees
- Carbon-dioxide/lactate sensitivity: panic patients' brains over-react to rising blood CO2 (suffocation-alarm theory of Klein); the reason breathing exercises and interoceptive work matter
- Medical mimics/fuels to screen: hyperthyroidism, anaemia, arrhythmias (SVT), asthma (and its salbutamol inhalers, sympathomimetic fuel), caffeine (8 cups of chai), nicotine, steroid/theophylline effects, withdrawal from alcohol/benzodiazepines, vestibular dysfunction (the "vertigo" mislabel), hypoglycaemia in diabetics
- Nocturnal panic: attacks from sleep in a share of patients, waking the person mid-wave, commonly mislabelled as "night terror" or "heart trouble"

**Psychological.**
- The catastrophe misinterpretation: sensations (normal or anxiety-driven) read as dying/madness/loss-of-control
- Anxiety sensitivity: a trait, "bodily sensations are dangerous", measurable and modifiable, the strongest psychological risk factor
- The body-scan habit: vigilant monitoring that manufactures findings

**Social / developmental.**
- Childhood adversity, over-anxious modelling, and major stressors preceding onset (a death, an illness scare, including a real cardiac event in a relative that "teaches" the interpretation)
- Indian context: the real cardiac event in the family (father's MI at 45) is the single commonest interpretive seed in Indian panic patients; the fear is inherited before the first attack

## 6. What happens in the mind/brain

**Story 1: the smoke alarm that fears itself.** The brain has a genuine emergency siren (noradrenergic). In panic disorder, the siren rings once for an ordinary reason (caffeine, a startle, a real worry), and then the MIND calls the fire brigade: "this racing heart is my death". That thought is itself an emergency signal, so the siren rings louder, the chest tightens further, and the loop closes. The treatment teaches the punchline: reading the alarm as a FALSE ALARM (curiosity, not catastrophe) starves the loop at its cognitive link; the fire brigade never comes, and the siren learns to stand down.

**Story 2: the suffocation monitor.** There is an old, elegant theory (Klein's suffocation-alarm) that panic brains run an over-sensitive CO2 meter: small rises in blood carbon dioxide (perfectly normal, caused by shallow fast breathing) trigger "AIR!" as a full emergency, producing the breathless, gasping quality of attacks. This is why the panic cycle is so physical, why hyperventilation feeds it, and why slow-exhale breathing (longer out than in) and interoceptive practice (deliberate straw-breathing) work at the engine-room level.

**Story 3: the shrinking kingdom.** Each escape teaches the map. The bus where the attack happened becomes forbidden territory; the standing queue becomes a trap; the mall with one exit becomes a cave-in-waiting. The kingdom of the safe shrinks (first "alone', then "far', then "out') until the house itself feels like the last castle, and even it is not fully safe (the nocturnal attack strikes inside it). The reversal runs the same geography backwards: graded re-entry, WITH the alarm present, until each territory is re-registered as ordinary. The attack must be felt, not fled; that is the entire treatment in one sentence.

## 7. Symptoms

### The panic attack (the symptom-event)
A sudden surge of intense fear/discomfort, peaking within minutes (typically under 10), with four or more of: pounding/racing heart; sweating; trembling; breathlessness/shortness of breath; choking sensation; chest pain/pressure; nausea/abdominal distress; dizziness/light-headedness/unsteadiness; chills or heat waves; numbness/tingling; derealisation ("world went unreal") or depersonalisation ("I watched myself"); fear of losing control/going mad; fear of dying. Attacks are self-limited (minutes, rarely beyond an hour) and harmless in bodily terms, however apocalyptic they feel.

**Expected vs unexpected:** attacks can arrive out of the blue (the panic-disorder signature) or be situation-triggered (phobic, performance). Panic disorder is built on the UNEXPECTED ones.

### Panic disorder
- Recurrent unexpected attacks
- At least one month of: persistent worry about more attacks or their consequences ("heart disease", "going mad"), or significant maladaptive behaviour change (avoiding exercise, carrying ECG reports, never travelling alone, repeated ER visits)
- Inter-attack life organised around the anticipation: body-scanning, escape-route planning, the bag with the "emergency" medicines, sitting near exits

### Agoraphobia
Fear of ≥ 2 of five situation clusters: public transport; open spaces (bridges, parks, parking lots); enclosed places (shops, theatres, cinema halls); queues or crowds; being outside the home alone. The unified fear: "if the wave comes HERE, I cannot escape/get help". Leads to progressive avoidance, companion-dependency, and, the Indian end-stage, the person who has not crossed the gate alone in years.

### The three-tier presentation to examine
1. The attack itself (minutes, physical)
2. The anticipatory machinery between attacks (days, cognitive-behavioural)
3. The geography of avoidance (months-years, the map)

## 8. Diagnosis

**The logic (DSM-5, paraphrased):** recurrent, unexpected panic attacks; at least a month of attack-related worry or behaviour change; not better explained by substances, medical illness, or another mental disorder's specific fears (social evaluation → social anxiety; contamination → OCD; trauma cues → PTSD). Agoraphobia stands alone: fear across 2+ of the five clusters for 6 months, whether or not panic disorder exists.

**The assessment craft:**
- Get the first attack's story in cinematic detail ("where were you, what happened in your body, what did you think was happening?"): the catastrophic interpretation is the diagnosis's fingerprint, and the story hooks the treatment
- Ask what they now avoid, carry, and do (the safety-behaviour inventory: the beta-blocker in the purse, the water bottle, the companion)
- Map the pattern: frequency, expected/unexpected, nocturnal, situational locks
- Comorbidity screens: depression (its arrival darkens the prognosis), alcohol/benzo self-treatment
- Physical workup, once, properly, and then CLOSED: ECG (age>40 or cardiac-risk profile), TSH, CBC, fasting glucose; caffeine/tea quantification; inhaler review for asthmatics; withdrawal history. The endless-workup patient needs an explicit closing consultation: "your heart has been examined four times; it is the ALARM, not the pump, that needs the cardiologist of the mind now."

**Instruments by name:** PDSS (Panic Disorder Severity Scale); HAM-A; the Agoraphobic Cognitions Questionnaire and Anxiety Sensitivity Index (research measures; the anxiety-sensitivity construct is examinable theory).

### Differential table

| Looks like panic | The distinguishing feature |
|---|---|
| Cardiac arrhythmia/SVT | Onset/offset with ECG signature; no anticipatory psychological build; response to vagal manoeuvres/adenosine |
| Hyperthyroidism | Continuous tremor/heat/weight loss; TSH settles it |
| Asthma/salbutamol overuse | Audible wheeze; inhaler-locked timing |
| Caffeine/nicotine excess | Dose-timing history |
| Vestibular disorders | Positional vertigo (head-turn triggers), nystagmus; no doom-cognition |
| Hypoglycaemia in diabetics | Whipple's triad; sugar-correlated |
| Withdrawal states (alcohol, benzos) | Locked to the substance clock |
| PTSD intrusions | Trauma-cue-locked, with the cluster architecture of PTSD |
| Social anxiety | Situation = scrutiny only; no out-of-the-blue attacks |
| OCD | Contamination-checking themes with rituals |
| Pheochromocytoma (the exam zebra) | Paroxysmal hypertension + sweating + headache; urinary metanephrines: order once in a lifetime, not once a month |

## 9. Management

**The architecture: educate → block the loop → retrain the alarm → reopen the map.**

**Step 1: the physiology consultation (the first treatment session in disguise).** Draw the panic cycle on paper: sensation → catastrophe thought → adrenaline → stronger sensation. State the three facts that restructure everything: (1) the attack cannot harm you, no panic attack in recorded medical history has turned into a heart attack or madness by itself; (2) it always ends, minutes; (3) the white-knuckle fighting makes it louder; the leaning-back curiosity makes it shorter. This session alone reduces attack frequency in a substantial share.

**Step 2, CBT, the specific package:**
- **Cognitive restructuring of the catastrophe**: the heart-attack belief answered with the cardiology data ("four normal ECGs and a normal echo are better evidence than a loud feeling"), the madness belief with the neuroscience ("fear FEELS like madness; madness does not arrive in 10-minute waves")
- **Interoceptive exposure** (the signature technique: deliberate induction of the feared sensations in session) running stairs for racing heart, spinning in a chair for dizziness, breathing through a straw for air hunger, staring at a lamp for unreality; repeated until the sensation itself loses its meaning. This is the fear-of-the-alarm being treated directly, and it is the component that produces the cure rates
- **In-vivo exposure for the avoidance map**: the graded ladder back into buses, queues, malls, with attacks welcomed rather than medicated away
- **Breathing retraining** (slow, exhale-weighted): useful adjunct, NOT to be taught as a safety behaviour ("the breathing as escape valve" trap; if used to STOP attacks it re-enters the loop; used to ride them out, it helps)
- **Safety-behaviour stripping**: the ECG-report pocket, the companion, the water ritual, faded rung by rung

**Step 3, pharmacotherapy:**
- **SSRIs first-line**: paroxetine 10→40 mg, sertraline 25→150 mg, escitalopram 5→20 mg, citalopram, fluoxetine; START LOW (panic brains are jitter-sensitive: the first-week activation mimics panic and drives dropouts, warn, dose-split, review early)
- Full trial 8–12 weeks; maintenance 12 months; slow taper thereafter
- **SSNR** (venlafaxine XR 37.5→225 mg): effective, same jitter warning
- **Benzodiazepines** (alprazolam/clonazepam): fast-acting, real short-term role for the severely disabled (weeks), long-term role as a trap (tolerance, dependence, interoceptive-exposure interference; the drug prevents the learning that the alarm is survivable). Where already dependent: structured taper (see Benzodiazepine Misuse note)
- Combination CBT+SSRI for severity; CBT alone for mild-moderate and for anyone who prefers durable skills over medicines

**Step 4: the reopening.** Exercise prescription (the unused gym membership resumed = interoceptive exposure at scale), travel retraining, and the emergency-department feedback letter ("this was panic, follow the pathway") to close the cardiology circuit formally.

*India note (approx 2026): sertraline 100 mg ≈ ₹80–160/month; paroxetine ≈ ₹100–200/month; the CBT package ≈ 6–12 sessions, private ₹600–1,500/session, district/Tele-MANAS nominal. The Indian systems-lever: emergency rooms and cardiologists need a one-page panic referral protocol, "normal ECG + normal trop RIN + classic description = refer to the anxiety pathway with a reassurance letter", converting the busiest doors in Indian medicine into panic-detection points instead of repeat-customer factories.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **The cardiology circuit dismantling.** The Indian panic patient averages multiple ECGs and at least one echo/TMT before psychiatry. The dismantling ritual: (1) one final cardiology consultation stating closure in writing; (2) the physiology session with the panic-cycle drawing in the patient's language; (3) the ECG report explicitly retired from the purse ("the safety blanket is the illness's uniform, take it off in session 4").
- **The housebound homemaker.** The agoraphobic woman absorbed into the joint family as "she doesn't go out" presents after years, usually via a daughter's insistence or a medical illness. Treatment adaptations: ladders built from the household's geography (gate → street corner → the sabziwala → the temple aangan → the bus), companion-fading as a rung structure (mother-in-law accompanies → waits at the corner → waves from the window → none), and the family module reframing "weak nerves" into the treatable-alarm narrative.
- **Night-attack families.** Nocturnal panics are read in many households as "ghost pressure" or heart disease, and treated accordingly (faith rituals, cardiac registers). Both can coexist with the physiology explanation, ally, don't mock.
- **The panic bag.** Indian patients carry kits: the ECG report, the sorbitrate (borrowed from a cardiac relative, genuinely dangerous self-medication to ask about by name), water, a phone with a son on speed-dial. Inventory it, honour its logic, and fade each element as a rung.
- **Language matters.** "Panic" translates poorly; the working phrases that land: "ghabrahat ka daura" (the surge of fright), "seen ka dhamak bhaag jaata hai"; name the disorder in the patient's words on the prescription paper itself, and the treatment plan follows into the family's vocabulary.
- **Faith and closure.** Where the family's model is ghost-affliction, the temple visit is part of the first-attribution consultation, not a barrier; the treatment alliance accepts the first explanation and adds the second ("the mantrik calmed the spirit; we calm the alarm; both can be true while we test the ladder").

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: the ECG-collecting manager.** A 42-year-old bank manager in Nagpur had his first attack in a traffic jam ("I thought my time had come"), was driven to a hospital, and was discharged with a normal ECG and "gas" advice. Six months later: eleven ER visits, three ECGs, one TMT, a wallet of reports, driving abandoned ("what if it happens on the flyover"), and the sorbitrate habit, his father's, borrowed. Assessment: panic disorder with agoraphobic avoidance and cardiac misinterpretation, father's MI at 48 as the interpretive seed. Management: the closure cardiology consult in writing; the physiology session with the cycle drawn (he asked to keep the paper); SSRI started low (sertraline 25 → 100 mg with the jitter warning); 8 sessions CBT: interoceptive stair-runs and straw-breathing ("my heart RACES up the stairs, and nothing happens; say it again"), the flyover re-entry ladder with the therapist on the pillion for three rides, the sorbitrate retired under supervision, the reports returned to the drawer at home. At 6 months: no ER visits in five months, driving restored, one attack "rode it out in the parking lot and it got bored". Teaching points: (1) the cardiology closure ritual is the entry ticket to psychiatric care; (2) interoceptive work treats the fear of the sensations, which IS the disorder; (3) the safety kit fades element-by-element as exposure rungs, not as a confiscation.

**Case 2: the homemaker behind the gate.** A 51-year-old homemaker in Vijayawada had not crossed her gate alone in four years (attacks in a queue and on a bus; the joint family absorbed her as "weak-nerved"). Presentation was incidental, her daughter, a nurse, insisted on review after an attack at a family wedding. Assessment: panic disorder with agoraphobia, decades-old, plus the companion architecture (always escorted, always near an exit, never alone outside). Management: 14 sessions, physiology drawing in Telugu, interoceptive practice (chair-spinning for dizziness, straw-breathing for air hunger, the "step-up-and-down" for the heart), the household-geography ladder with companion-fading rungs (daughter waits at the corner → waves from the gate → no one), and family sessions retiring the "weak nerves" frame. Fluoxetine 20 mg (slow start, jitter warned) carried the early weeks. At 8 months: the temple aangan alone, the sabzi market alone, one bus ride with a planned attack-welcoming script. Teaching points: (1) the household's geography IS the ladder; ladders are local; (2) companion-fading is an exposure rung, not an administrative convenience; (3) "absorbed disability" patients surface only when a medically-literate relative pulls the file; train the nurses and ASHA workers to spot the woman behind the gate.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way patients and families ask it

**"I genuinely feel I am dying each time. You say it is harmless?"** The feeling of dying is real; the dying is not. In every recorded case, panic peaks and passes within minutes while the body stays safe; the same adrenaline a sprint produces, aimed by a mis-set alarm. Your four normal ECGs are the data; we will treat the alarm that keeps calling the fire brigade.

**"Is there something wrong with my heart that the tests are missing?"** Understandable after a father's or uncle's heart attack; that fear is the most common seed we see. The modern ECG plus troponin catches the dangerous patterns; panic's pattern is the opposite, minutes, waves, normal tests in between, and worst when resting rather than climbing stairs.

**"Why do attacks come at night, in my sleep?"** The over-sensitive alarm monitors CO2 even in sleep; a small rise triggers the siren at 3 a.m.: you wake mid-wave. It is not a ghost, not a heart problem, and it responds to the same treatment as daytime attacks.

**"If I breathe slowly during the attack, will it stop?"** Slow breathing helps you ride the wave; used as an emergency stop-tool it can quietly become another escape behaviour. The real target is letting the wave arrive, peak, and pass while you lean back, the attack learns it no longer frightens you.

**"Will I need medicines for life?"** Most people do not. A treatment season of 8–12 weeks (building to 12 months for frequent relapsers) combined with the therapy, then a planned slow taper with an early-warning list. The therapy skills are permanent; the medicine is scaffolding.

**"The doctor gave an antidepressant: I came for panic, not depression."** Antidepressants are panic medicines at their own doses, they re-set the alarm's threshold over weeks. We start low and warn you about the first-week jitter, because panic brains are sensitive starters.

**"My wife has not left the house alone in years. Can that really reverse?"** Yes, and the reversal is the visible part of treatment, a ladder built from your own gate outward, companion fading step by step, attacks welcomed along the way rather than medicated away. The kingdom re-expands the same way it shrank: territory by territory.

**"Can she travel by flight/train again?"** Almost always, with graded practice (station visits, short train runs first; the flight is a ladder, not a wall). Carry the written attack-riding script, not the pill bottle, as the emergency kit.

**"Will my children get this?"** A tendency, not a certainty. The heritable part is the jumpy alarm; the learnable parts, the catastrophe interpretation and the avoidance habits, are exactly what early treatment and family example can prevent.

**"Should we do the temple rituals the family suggests?"** If ritual brings the family peace, it has a place; our evidence and your faith are not enemies. What must not be missed is that attacks shrink under the specific treatment, and that we measure the map (doors crossed alone) rather than the omens.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**Gates to memorise:** attack peak-within-minutes; disorder = recurrent UNEXPECTED attacks + 1 month of worry/consequence-fear/behaviour change; agoraphobia = 2+ of five clusters for 6 months; DSM-5 decouples agoraphobia from panic (both standalone).

**The classic symptom count**: a panic attack = 4+ of the 13 listed symptoms; know the list by grouping: body-storm (heart, sweat, tremble, breath, choking, chest, nausea), balance/sensation (dizzy, chills/heat, tingling), mind events (unreality, depersonalisation, fear of madness, fear of dying).

**Mnemonic for the five agoraphobia clusters, "TEOQE": Transport, Enclosed, Open, Queues/crowds, outsidE alone.**

**High-yield facts:**
- Panic attacks are common and harmless; the DISORDER is the fear of them
- Anxiety sensitivity = the modifiable psychological risk factor
- Interoceptive exposure = the signature curative technique; straw/spin/stairs as inductions
- Klein's suffocation-alarm/CO2-sensitivity theory: examinable history
- SSRIs first-line; START LOW (jitter); benzos = short bridges only
- Nocturnal panic exists and is not night terror
- MITRAL VALVE PROLAPSE association: historically over-quoted, benign in this context; know it, do not lean on it
- Cardiology-circuit closure is part of treatment

**Classic traps:**
1. Diagnosing panic disorder from situation-locked attacks only (phobia/expected attacks first)
2. Missing the asthmatic on salbutamol or the patient on 10 cups of chai
3. Prescribing full-dose SSRI day one: activation dropout
4. Lifelong alprazolam as "maintenance": the Indian default error
5. Treating agoraphobia without companion-fading (the companion is the hidden safety behaviour)
6. Teaching breathing as an escape tool (loops back into the disorder)

**Indian exam corner:** "panic disorder (criteria, differential from cardiac disease, management", "interoceptive exposure) what and why", "agoraphobia; the five clusters and the DSM-5 change", " nocturnal panic attacks". Indian-context marks: the cardiology-circuit presentation, the housebound-homemaker agoraphobia, NMHS treatment-gap data.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. By definition, a panic attack peaks:**
A. Over hours
B. Within minutes
C. Overnight
D. Over weeks
**Answer: B.** Sudden surge, peak within minutes (typically under 10), the discriminator from generalised anxiety and from cardiac events.

**2. The core cognitive event of panic disorder is:**
A. Fear of contamination
B. Catastrophic misinterpretation of bodily sensations
C. Trauma re-experiencing
D. Fear of scrutiny
**Answer: B.** Sensation → catastrophe → more sensation, the loop treatment attacks.

**3. Interoceptive exposure involves:**
A. Avoiding all triggers
B. Deliberate induction of feared sensations (spinning, straw-breathing) until they lose meaning
C. Imaginal scenes of loss
D. Relaxation only
**Answer: B.** Treating the fear-of-the-sensations directly: the signature technique and the cure-driver.

**4. First-line pharmacotherapy for panic disorder:**
A. Alprazolam lifelong
B. Antipsychotics
C. SSRIs, started low, titrated
D. Beta-blockers alone
**Answer: C.** SSRIs first-line with low starting doses (jitter window); benzos are short bridges; beta-blockers do not treat the disorder.

**5. A patient fears buses, queues and malls but has never had a panic attack. Under DSM-5:**
A. Cannot be diagnosed
B. Agoraphobia is diagnosable without panic-disorder history
C. This is social anxiety
D. This is GAD
**Answer: B.** The decoupling: agoraphobia stands alone on the five-cluster fear.

**6. The most common Indian pathway to a panic-disorder diagnosis runs through:**
A. Direct psychiatry self-referral
B. The cardiology/emergency circuit with normal ECGs
C. School counsellors
D. Neurological stroke units
**Answer: B.** Palpitations drive the ECG-first route; the systems-lever is converting ERs and cardiologists into panic-detection points.

**7. Nocturnal panic attacks:**
A. Do not exist
B. Wake the person from sleep mid-surge, and respond to standard panic treatment
C. Are always temporal-lobe seizures
D. Indicate schizophrenia
**Answer: B.** The CO2-sensitive alarm fires in sleep; frequently misread as night terrors or "ghost pressure".

**8. Long-term benzodiazepines in panic disorder are specifically problematic because they:**
A. Raise cholesterol
B. Interfere with the extinction learning that exposure therapy builds, and build dependence
C. Cause diabetes
D. Are unavailable in India
**Answer: B.** Relief now, cure-blocked later: the benzo paradox in panic.
