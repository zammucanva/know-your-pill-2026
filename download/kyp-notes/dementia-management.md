# The management of dementia — the practical umbrella note

## 1. In one line + overview

**Tagline:** Whatever the cause of a dementia, the management always has the same five floors (**tell it truly, treat what is treatable, drug the symptoms knowingly, engineer the environment, and support the family who does the caring**) and the quality of life five years from diagnosis depends less on which medicine was chosen than on how well those five floors were built.

**Overview, in plain words:** This is the umbrella note: it puts the practical *system* of dementia care in one place and points to the disease-specific notes (Alzheimer's, vascular, Lewy body, frontotemporal, HIV, Parkinson's) for the details each of them owns. Dementia management is a long, multi-year construction project that begins the day of diagnosis. The disease is progressive, but **most of the suffering that families remember was preventable**: the crisis admissions caused by constipation and urinary infections; the lost money and legal rights because planning came too late; the caregiver's own breakdown because nobody asked about her; the fights over bathing and feeding that a change of technique would have dissolved. The order of operations matters: information first, then reversible-load removal, then the right drugs at the right stage, then behaviour handled as communication, and family support as the load-bearing wall under everything. In India, where virtually all care is home care, the "system" is usually one doctor plus one family, so this note is written so that pair can actually run it.

## 2. Learning objectives

1. Organise dementia management into the five floors and explain why the order matters.
2. Run the *reversible-load checklist* that precedes any escalation of symptoms (PAIN-FUSES style).
3. Match the pharmacological options to stage (mild → moderate → severe) and to dementia subtype; including which drugs to *stop*.
4. Manage BPSD as behaviour-as-communication, with the drug ladder as second line and fixed review dates.
5. Build the caregiver programme: education, skills, respite, monitoring and crisis plans.
6. Sequence the legal and financial steps while insight lasts.
7. Manage the final phase: swallowing, feeding decisions, infections and comfort-first care.
8. Design the realistic Indian service pathway: family-doctor spine, PHC/district support, memory-clinic referral points, and what to do when none of these exist.

## 4. Epidemiology (why this note exists at scale)

The disease-specific notes carry the numbers; the management-relevant points are these: most dementia care happens at home, by unpaid family members, for years; the median Indian carer is a 50–60-year-old woman related by marriage or blood, often with no help and with health problems of her own. Global and Indian evidence agree that caregiver depression is common, that caregiver intervention trials (education plus problem-solving) **reduce patient institutionalisation and carer depression**, and that the highest-cost events (admissions, fractures, tube insertions) are the ones structured care prevents. Management is therefore not the soft option after drugs; it is the treatment with the strongest effect sizes in the whole field.

## 5. Causes & risk factors (for management problems)

The disease causes the dementia; these factors cause the *crises that management treats*:
- **Physical:** pain (joints, teeth, constipation), urinary infection, dehydration, drugs with anticholinergic or sedating loads, sensory impairment, sleep disruption, the reversible-load cluster.
- **Psychological:** depression on top of dementia; fear (of the dark, of bathing, of strangers); grief at losses the person can still half-perceive.
- **Environmental:** noise, glare, new rooms, hospital admissions (delirium on dementia), absence of routine.
- **Caregiver:** exhaustion, high expressed emotion, misunderstanding of symptoms as intentional, absence of respite.
- **Systemic:** late diagnosis, no follow-up plan, no written instructions, multiple uncoordinated prescribers, the Indian multi-doctor stack.

## 6. What happens in the body/brain (the management lens)

Two facts about the dementing brain drive everything in this note. **First, the brain has lost its reserve**, a noise, fever or moved bed that a healthy brain shrugs off will tip a demented one into delirium; this is why reversible-load vigilance is a permanent programme, not a one-time workup. **Second, abilities are lost in reverse order of learning**, the most recently acquired functions (newest memories, novel planning, inhibition) go first; the oldest (songs, prayers, habitual routines, procedural skills) persist. Management exploits this: it stops trying to teach the lost functions and instead **builds life on the surviving ones**, routine as memory, ritual as comfort, music as language. The behavioural symptoms (BPSD) are the brain's remaining communication channel: the person who cannot say "my tooth hurts" says it as refusal to eat, as aggression during face-washing, as nighttime wandering. Read the behaviour, treat the cause.

## 7. Symptoms (the management targets)

The floors correspond to four target domains that follow: **cognition** (slow decline, preserve function), **activities of daily living** (independence layer-by-layer: finances → medicines → travel → dressing → toileting → feeding), **behaviour and mood** (BPSD: agitation, aggression, apathy, sleep reversal, wandering, misidentification, shadowing), and **comorbidities** (falls, swallowing, continence, infections, pain, weight). Each disease-specific note describes its own symptom profile; management converts each profile into a written problem list reviewed at fixed intervals (3–6 months early, more often when advanced).

## 8. Diagnosis (the management-relevant parts)

- Diagnosis and subtype are covered in the disease notes (Alzheimer's, vascular, Lewy body, frontotemporal, HIV, Parkinson's).
- Two management acts belong to the *diagnostic* consultation itself: **disclosure done well** (family present, honest, hopeful where hope is real, with written next steps) and the **baseline register** (cognitive score, function level, behaviour checklist, medicines list, caregiver status), the reference point every future visit compares against.
- At every subsequent visit, re-score briefly; the *trajectory* decides drug continuation and stage planning, not any single number.

## 9. Management: the five floors

### Floor 1, Tell it truly: the disclosure and planning consultation
- Name the illness plainly, correct the myths ("old age", "madness", "curse"), state honestly what medicine can and cannot do.
- Register the project: written problem list, named follow-up doctor, 3-monthly reviews, red-flag list for urgent contact ("sudden change", "not passing urine", "fall", "fever with confusion").
- Begin the legal window work (Floor 5) in the same month, insight expires silently.

### Floor 2, Treat what is treatable: the reversible-load checklist
Run it at every review and at every "worsening":
- **P**ain (teeth, joints, abdomen) · **A**ctivity/constipation · **I**nfection (urine, chest, skin) · **N**ight and sleep · **F**aecal impaction (the hidden classic) · **U**rine retention · **S**enses (glasses, hearing, dentures, earwax) · **E**nvironment (new room, noise, light) · **S**edating/anticholinogenic drug burden, strip it.
- Vascular patients: the blood-pressure-sugar-cholesterol regime (see the vascular note). All patients: hearing and vision correction, dental review, footwear, bone health (calcium/vitamin D and osteoporosis assessment, the fracture-prevention package).

### Floor 3: Drug the symptoms knowingly
- **Cognition:** cholinesterase inhibitors (mild–moderate Alzheimer's and Lewy body dementias, best responses in DLB/PDD) and memantine (moderate–severe; add-on): dosing, monitoring and honest expectations in the Alzheimer's and Lewy body notes; **not indicated** in FTD, and vascular benefit is modest.
- **BPSD drugs:** after Floor 2 has failed and non-drug methods (Floor 4) are insufficient: SSRI for mood-driven behaviour; low-dose quetiapine or risperidone with a written review date and the mortality/stroke warning acknowledged, the smallest dose for the shortest time; **forbidden logic in DLB/PDD: no dopamine blockers without the specialist's explicit call** (see the Lewy body note's wallet card).
- **Stop-list discipline:** anticholinergic bladder drugs, first-generation antihistamines, tricyclics, long-acting benzodiazepines, "brain tonics", high-dose multivitamin cocktails, the Indian OPD clean-up list.
- **Deprescribing with honesty:** when advanced disease takes away swallow and speech, tapering cognition drugs is ethical end-of-life care, not abandonment.

*India note: the effective monthly medicine budget of standard dementia care (donepezil/memantine/SSRI in generics) is commonly ₹300–800 (approx, 2026); the real economic burden is supervision time, not tablets. Prescribe within that reality: one review date per prescription, one written change per visit.*

### Floor 4: Engineer the environment and read the behaviour
- **Routine as memory:** fixed sequences for day, meals, bath and bed; keep the family that can be kept; the same sentences at the same times.
- **The unmet-need method:** for every behaviour write, what happened exactly, what preceded it, what followed it, what need could this be? (toilet, pain, fear, boredom, loneliness). Change the antecedent, not the person; redirect, never argue.
- **Dining, dressing, bathing technique:** one instruction at a time; music from their youth; hand-washing before hand-feeding; bathing in their preferred time and sequence; if a technique fails, the technique is wrong, not the person.
- **Wandering and safety:** ID card/bracelet with phone; door alarms; locks above eye-level; traffic and terrace safety audit; night lighting and floor mattress.
- **Sleep programme:** daylight exposure, daytime activity, no daytime chair-napping; evening calm; check the reversible-load list before any sleep drug.
- **Day-care and activity centres** (ARS DI chapters in some cities, dementia-specific NGOs), even two afternoons a week protect caregiver survival.

### Floor 5: Support the family (the load-bearing wall)
- **The caregiver register:** at every visit, one question to the carer ("how are *you* sleeping, eating, coping?"); a carer depression screen once a year; treatment for the carer as a patient when needed.
- **Skills teaching:** the one-hour survival package (communication rules, bathing/dining technique, behaviour diary, red flags, when to call whom), teachable by any doctor or counsellor, deliverable once, transformative.
- **Respite engineering:** sibling rosters, paid attendant for the heaviest hours, day-care, planned short admissions where insurance/schemes allow; prescribe respite in words, with a date.
- **Support organisations:** Alzheimer's and Related Disorders Society of India (ARDSI) chapters, dementia helplines, tele-MANAS for the carer's own mental health (14416).
- **Legal and financial (while insight lasts):** nominee updates, joint mandates, a written family arrangement on property and caregiving responsibilities, supported decision-making and advance preferences under the Mental Healthcare Act 2017, disability certification (unlocks entitlements; do it early, it takes months), and employment disclosure strategy for young-onset families (see the FTD note).

### The final phase: comfort-first care
- **Swallowing failure:** hand-feeding with posture and texture techniques beats early tube insertion for comfort and dignity in advanced dementia (the evidence is consistent; discuss *before* the crisis, ideally at the moderate stage).
- **Recurrent chest infections:** honest conversations about hospital transfers vs symptom comfort at home; each such conversation should happen once, early, not eight times in casualty.
- **Pain and agitation:** regular comfort review; a quiet, warm, pain-free, clean, and company-rich final phase is a realistic goal at home, and Indian families, given one honest plan, usually deliver it.

## 10. Indian practice

- **The realistic team:** one doctor (family physician, psychiatrist or neurologist) + one named family caregiver + (where available) a community worker or ASHA-adjacent link for monitoring; tertiary memory clinics for diagnosis confirmation and difficult BPSD only.
- **The pathway problem:** there is no national dementia care programme yet (the National Programme for Health Care of the Elderly is the nearest platform); ARDSI and WHO's iSupport-type caregiver programmes are the practical support tools to hand families.
- **Tele-follow-up:** video or phone review between physical visits, one call a month to the carer maintains adherence to the plan at near-zero cost; tele-MANAS can be the carer's own support line.
- **Prescribing realities:** generics through Jan Aushadhi; investigations limited to the essential (thyroid, B12, sugar once; imaging when the picture is atypical); no insistence on biomarkers/PET in routine Indian practice.
- **What to write in the file at every visit:** cognition score, function level, top two behaviours, carer status, next review date, four lines that constitute the whole national dementia infrastructure when done consistently.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: the admission that never happened.** A 79-year-old with Alzheimer's disease, stable on donepezil, became agitated each evening for a week; the family reached for "a stronger tablet" and a casualty visit. Telephone triage ran the load checklist: she had not opened her bowels for five days (faecal impaction), and had skipped her evening meal for two days (constipation-appetite cycle). Emergency plan: aperient, hydration, unchanged routine, no new prescriptions, review call in 48 hours. The evening storms subsided with the bowels.

*Teaching points: "sudden worsening" is a load problem until proven otherwise; the checklist is the consultation; one phone call prevented an admission, a CT and an antipsychotic, the highest-value intervention of the month.*

**Case 2: the wall that was not built.** A 72-year-old widower's daughter managed his vascular dementia alone, refusing help "because we are family". Eleven months in, she presented herself with depressive symptoms, weight loss and uncontrolled anger at her father; his function collapsed in the same month (missed medicines, a fall, a urinary infection). The re-management: her treatment as a patient, a two-sibling rotation with written shifts, day-care two afternoons weekly, respite admission planned quarterly, and a behaviour diary. Father stabilised at the pre-collapse baseline; the daughter's depression remitted.

*Teaching points: the caregiver is the infrastructure, when she fails, the patient falls with her; respite prescribed in words with dates beats respite discussed in principle; "we are family" is the sentence that hides the caregiver until she breaks.*

## 13. FAQ

**"What is the plan, overall, in one line?"**
Keep him comfortable, safe and connected at home for as long as possible, with planned reviews, preventable crises prevented, and the family protected from breaking.

**"How often should we see the doctor?"**
Every three to six months in the stable phases, sooner after any fall, sudden change or medicine event, plus whatever phone check-in your doctor offers in between.

**"He suddenly got much worse this week. Is the disease accelerating?"**
Sudden changes are usually *not* the disease; they are a urine infection, constipation, pain, a new tablet or sleep disruption. Run the checklist with your doctor before accepting the decline as permanent.

**"Should he have a feeding tube when swallowing fails?"**
In advanced dementia, hand-feeding with good technique gives comparable comfort and dignity; tubes do not clearly prevent pneumonia or prolong comfort. Discuss this with the family early, so the decision is made in the living room, not the casualty.

**"We are three brothers in different cities. How do we share this?"**
Written rotation shifts, one shared daily log, one medicine-owner, and quarterly respite for the primary one; the doctor should help draft it; "we are family, no formalities" is how caregivers break.

**"Is day-care worth the money?"**
Even two afternoons a week reliably lowers caregiver stress and delays residential placement, among the best value purchases in the entire disease.

**"When should the memory medicines be stopped?"**
When advanced disease removes speech, swallow and daily function; a tapering agreed with the doctor is honest care, not giving up; earlier if side effects outweigh the function being held.

**"Can I leave him alone for two hours?"**
Build up alone-time with safety installed: ID card, gas off, floor mattress, familiar routine, a phone with one-button dialling, and rehearse what he should do when he forgets where you are.

**"What legal steps are still open now that he is already forgetful?"**
If insight remains: nominee updates, supported decision-making documentation under the Mental Healthcare Act 2017, and family agreements; once insight is gone, the options narrow; do it in the earlier window, however uncomfortable.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **Mnemonics to build:** the five floors as **"Tell, Treat, Drug, Dwell, De-family-support"** is clumsy, build your own; the load checklist as **PAIN FUSES**; the BPSD approach as **"Behaviour is a sentence; read it before you sedate it."**
- **Highest-yield facts:** antipsychotic mortality warning in dementia; caregiver intervention effect sizes exceeding drug effects; the reversible-load concept; cholinesterase-responder subtypes (DLB/PDD best); feeding-tube evidence in advanced dementia; MHA 2017 planning tools; disability certification pathway.
- **Classic traps:** (1) escalating antipsychotics before running the checklist; (2) attributing a delirium to progression; (3) forgetting the carer as patient; (4) prescribing tonics to feel helpful; (5) late legal planning after insight expired.
- **Viva favourites:** design a community dementia service (answer: disclosure + carer training + phone follow-up + day-care + crisis red-flag card, the five-floor logic); BPSD stepped algorithm; the evidence hierarchy of non-pharmacological vs pharmacological BPSD management; when to stop cognition drugs.
- **Indian exam corner:** the ARDSI/WHO iSupport framing; tele-MANAS for carers; the family-doctor spine model; "four lines in the file" as a quality improvement answer; the home-based end-of-life conversation.
- **Rehearse one line:** "Dementia is managed like a household project, floors built early, load checked often, and the wall (the family) reinforced before it cracks."

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. A dementia patient's evening agitation worsens over a week. The first response is:**
A. Increase the antipsychotic
B. Run the reversible-load checklist (pain, stool, urine, senses, sleep, drugs)
C. Admit for CT
D. Stop the memory medicine

**Answer: B.** Sudden change is a load problem until proven otherwise; most such episodes dissolve without new prescriptions.

**2. The dementia subtype with the best response to cholinesterase inhibitors is:**
A. Frontotemporal dementia
B. Dementia with Lewy bodies / PDD
C. Vascular dementia
D. Normal-pressure hydrocephalus

**Answer: B.** The deepest cholinergic deficits sit in the synucleinopathies (see the DLB and PDD notes); FTD has no benefit and can worsen.

**3. The evidence-based position on feeding tubes in advanced dementia is:**
A. Tubes prolong comfort reliably
B. Hand-feeding carries comparable comfort and dignity without clear tube benefit
C. Tubes prevent aspiration pneumonia
D. Mandatory once swallow fails

**Answer: B.** Consistent trial and cohort evidence; the conversation belongs early, at home, not in casualty.

**4. Among all interventions in dementia care, the largest effect sizes come from:**
A. Newer antipsychotics
B. Structured caregiver training and support
C. Brain tonics
D. Vitamin megadoses

**Answer: B.** Caregiver intervention trials reduce carer depression and patient institutionalisation, outperforming drug effects on life outcomes.

**5. Legal and financial planning must happen:**
A. After behavioural symptoms settle
B. Early, while insight remains, the window expires silently
C. Only after disability certification
D. Never in dementia

**Answer: B.** Nomination, mandates, family agreements and MHA 2017 tools all require the patient's preserved capacity.

**6. The four lines worth writing at every dementia review are:**
A. Weight, BP, pulse, temperature
B. Cognition score, function level, top behaviours, carer status + next review date
C. Drug list only
D. Family history only

**Answer: B.** Those four lines (plus the date) constitute the trajectory record on which every management decision (drugs, stage, respite) is made.
