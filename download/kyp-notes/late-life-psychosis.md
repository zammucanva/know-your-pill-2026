# Late-Life Psychosis

## 1. In one line + overview

**Psychosis arriving after 40 is a different clinical animal from the young-onset illness (more persecutory and partition-themed delusions and hallucinations, fewer negative symptoms, and frequently RIDDEN on something: untreated deafness, a brewing dementia, a depression, a medication, or a stroke) so the first diagnostic law of the grey-haired patient with "strange ideas" is: never accept the label "late schizophrenia" until delirium, dementia and the treatable engines have been ruled out, because the treatment differs, and the prescribing is treacherous (antipsychotics in elders carry real mortality cautions).**

An elderly woman whispers that the neighbours pump gas through her walls at night; a retired clerk reports his food tastes poisoned and has begun eating only packaged biscuits; a widower hears religious songs from the ceiling fan and, far from frightened, finds them comforting. Three different engines can produce these: a true late-onset schizophrenia-spectrum psychosis (which exists, runs a shallower course than the early-onset form, and responds to small antipsychotic doses); a paranoid-syndrome built substantially on lifelong-unaddressed hearing loss and social isolation; or the opening chapters of a dementia (especially Lewy body's visual hallucinations, delusions of theft in Alzheimer's). The discipline that protects this population: age has REORDERED the differential, delirium first (the great mimic), neurodegeneration second, depression-with-psychosis third, substance/medication fourth, and only then the primary late-onset psychoses. And the treatment law: start low, go slow, review fast, and treat the co-riders (the hearing aid, the cataract, the antidepressant) because in the old brain the drugs themselves are the fastest way to make things worse.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Define the onset cutoffs (late onset >40, very-late-onset >60) and the phenomenological signature of each
- Recite the reordered differential: delirium → dementia → depression → substance/medication → primary psychosis
- Explain the sensory-deprivation engine (hearing loss → suspicion) and its treatment implication (the hearing aid as antipsychotic adjunct)
- Distinguish primary late-onset psychosis from BPSD (the dementia notes' territory) and from delusional disorder's locked pattern
- Run the workup: cognitive testing, sensory testing, MRI, medication audit, mood assessment, in that order
- Prescribe antipsychotics in elders with the cautions: the dementia-related-psychosis mortality warning, the LBD neuroleptic-sensitivity catastrophe, anticholinergic load, falls and stroke risk
- Manage the family: the accused daughter-in-law, the "old age talk" dismissal, the gas-pipe inspection requests
- Handle Indian realities: the deaf-elder epidemic, joint-family pathways, when depot strategies actually help adherence

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Late-onset (>40) schizophrenia-spectrum psychosis: roughly a quarter of all schizophrenia-spectrum cases have their onset after 40; very-late-onset schizophrenia-like psychosis (>60) is rarer and strikingly female-predominant, with strong associations to sensory impairment (deafness above all), social isolation, and premorbid paranoid or schizoid personality traits. Course: notably SHALLOWER than early-onset; fewer negative symptoms, better preserved social functioning, hospitalisation needs fewer, response to lower medication doses. Mortality and functional decline still run above population baselines (the medical-comorbidity freight of the age). The risk architecture: family history is weaker than early-onset; women's late-life estrogen decline is one proposed contributor to the female dominance of the very-late form.

**India.** No dedicated late-onset psychosis survey; the NMHS 2015–16 geriatric mental-morbidity tier showed high prevalence of late-life disorders overall with the service-reality that elderly psychiatric presentations route through general physicians and neurology before psychiatry. Two Indian amplifiers: the untreated-deafness epidemic (decades of unaided presbycusis, the single biggest modifiable paranoia engine in Indian elders) and the joint-family pathway's double edge; more observation AND more accusation surface (the daughter-in-law-theft accusation is a household-level presentation with both illness and family-dynamics layers needing untangling).

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**Biological**
- The aging dopamine system's recalibration (receptor changes with age), a lower "psychosis ceiling"; the reason elders respond to FRACTIONS of young-adult doses and decompensate on small provocation
- Estrogen's late-life decline (the female-dominance of very-late-onset; the window of vulnerability)
- Silent cerebrovascular disease (basal ganglia and white-matter burden in imaging studies of very-late-onset cases; vascular contributions are real)
- Sensory deprivation: presbycusis and cataract, the modifiable layer; late-life paranoia's best-documented association
- Neurodegeneration's opening chapters: Alzheimer's theft-delusions, Lewy body's hallucinations, Parkinson's disease psychosis (the dopaminergic-medication engine)

**Psychological / social**
- Social isolation, widowhood, migration/foreign-language settings (the "foreigner-in-a-strange-land" paranoid states)
- Premorbid paranoid/schizoid traits hardened by decades of low-trust living
- Caregiving stress and dependency-conflicts: the family-dynamics kindling under many "paranoid" presentations

**Medications/substances (the iatrogenic layer)**
- Dopaminergic agents (levodopa, the agonists: Parkinson's psychosis engine), steroids, anticholinergics (the fog doubles), digoxin-toxicity-era classics, and the under-recognised: persistent benzodiazepine or alcohol misuse in elders masquerading as "confusion-with-ideas"

**Indian context**
- The unaided-deaf elder in a joint family: suspicion as the social read of misheard conversation ("they whisper about me"; they do, two rooms away, audibly and incomprehensibly)
- The dependency-conflict family: the elder's accusations as the only remaining power-lever in a household where authority has migrated to the son and daughter-in-law; part illness, part family-system signal

## 6. What happens in the brain

**Story 1: the muffled world engine.** Take a brain that has spent 70 years cross-checking sound with context, then muffle its input (presbycusis) while shrinking its social world (widowhood, retirement, children abroad). Conversations arrive as fragments; the meaning-starved brain does what brains do, fills gaps with narrative. In a low-trust, high-stakes social world (a joint family's inheritance tensions), the gap-filling defaults to suspicion. The result is "paranoia" that is substantially an ENGINEERING problem: the input is broken. Fix the channel (hearing aids, better lighting for the cataract-blurred face-reading) and the system recalibrates. This is why the geriatric psychosis workup treats the EARS as seriously as the mind.

**Story 2: the low-tolerance engine.** The aging brain's dopamine machinery runs closer to its thresholds in both directions: less reserve against delirium's chaos, and lower doses needed for both benefit and harm. An elderly patient who needs 1–2 mg of risperidone where a young adult needed 6 also develops drug-induced parkinsonism, sedation, falls and confusion at doses a 30-year-old would shrug off. The prescribing corollary: the geriatric dose rule (start at half or less, titrate at half-steps, review at fixed dates) and the de-prescribing discipline (the psychotic elder who worsens on medication is a FALLS/confusion assessment, not a dose-escalation mandate).

**Story 3: the borrowed fire engine.** Much late-life psychosis is the dementia's smoke: misidentification syndromes from a failing recognition system (the "phantom boarder", the mirror-sign, the stranger in the mirror being the misread self), theft-delusions from a memory system that cannot find the hidden purse (the "stolen" objects, re-hidden by the patient and reported missing), and Lewy body's formed visual hallucinations (children in the garden, visitors in the sitting room, often with retained insight and a curious calmness at the start). The clinical translation: psychosis in an elder with ANY cognitive signal is the dementia's symptom until proven otherwise, and the treatment runs the BPSD trigger-first ladder (the Dementia Management and Lewy Body notes), not the antipsychotic reflex.

## 7. Symptoms

**The late-onset primary psychosis signature:**
- Delusions: persecutory (poisoned food, gas through walls, neighbours conspiring), partition delusions (the classic: "they come through the wall/ceiling; they are in the flat above/next door", one's home violated through the partitions), misidentification (the impostor-householder), referential and somatic themes (infestation, "something done to me")
- Hallucinations: auditory (voices, running commentary, third-person, often related to the deafness-channel), visual (persons, religious figures), olfactory (poison-smells); hypnagogic visual phenomena in elders are common benign
- The ABSENT or faint layer: formal thought disorder and negative symptoms (the elder converses coherently, is groomed, socially appropriate between delusional islands) the presentation's "island-in-a-preserved-sea" quality that separates it from early-onset
- Function often preserved for years: cooking, accounts, temple-going, with the illness's cost concentrated in the family's siege

**The co-rider patterns (what to hunt before believing the label):**
- Fluctuating consciousness, night-worsening, new inattention → delirium (the Delirium note)
- Formed visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, dream-acting, capgras → Lewy body (the LBD note, and its neuroleptic-sensitivity law)
- Gradual theft-delusions with memory decline → Alzheimer's (the dementia notes)
- A mood-engine: the "guilty-poisoned" content with weight loss and early waking → psychotic depression (the treatment fork: ECT-tier evidence in elders)
- The medication timeline, the substance audit, the iatrogenic layer
- The deafness signature: voices heard "from the fan", conversation-suspicion in noise, television volume wars

**Danger signs:** refusal of food/water on poisoning-logic (the weight-loss clock); the accused-party confrontation (assault risk toward the daughter-in-law, the neighbour); wandering on partition-logic ("I must escape through their flat"); and suicide risk in the depressive engine.

## 8. Diagnosis

**The logic (DSM-5/ICD-11 paraphrased):** late-onset schizophrenia and very-late-onset schizophrenia-like psychosis are dimensional uses of the same criteria as schizophrenia/delusional disorder with onset cutoffs (>40; >60): DSM-5 keeps schizophrenia's criteria with the specifier discipline, ICD-11 carries the late-onset architecture; delusional disorder (the Delusional Disorder note's locked-pattern) is heavily represented in the elderly. But the criteria are the END of the assessment, not the beginning: the reorder-rule governs.

**The workup sequence (the reorder-rule in practice):**
1. **Delirium screen first**: onset timeline (days vs months), attention testing, day-night pattern, recent illness/medication/surgery, the CAM-tier logic (named)
2. **Cognitive testing**: MoCA or the literacy-adjusted instruments (the MCI note's discipline); informant change-report (the family's "what changed first" interview, memory-first vs suspicion-first tells the dementia story apart from the primary-psychosis story)
3. **Sensory testing**: audiometry and vision (the unaided presbycusis finding is the highest-yield test in this population)
4. **Mood assessment**: the geriatric depression screen (GDS-tier, named), with the psychotic-depression probe (guilt, nihilistic, somatic content)
5. **Medication and substance audit**: the full list with anticholinergic-burden counting, the benzo-alcohol honest history, dopaminergic meds where Parkinson's
6. **Medical labs**: TSH, B12, glucose, renal, electrolytes, and the infection screen where acute (the UTI-the-delirium classic)
7. **Imaging**: MRI where affordable/indicated; the structural mimics (silent infarcts, mass lesions, NPH) and the vascular burden; the first-psychosis-at-any-age rule extends to elders with any cognitive or focal signal
8. **The Parkinson/Lewy body examination**: the soft signs, the dream-acting history (RBD), the levodopa-timeline, before ANY antipsychotic decision (the LBD catastrophe prevention)
9. Rating scales (named only): the neuropsychiatric-inventory tier for tracking BPSD-adjacent symptoms; PANSS-tier for primary psychosis tracking in specialist hands

| The elderly patient with "strange ideas" | The distinguishing feature |

## 9. Management

**The frame: rule out first, treat co-riders second, antipsychotic third, and then at the SMALLEST effective dose with a written stop-review date.**

**1: The co-rider treatment (before or with any antipsychotic):**
- Hearing aids and cataract surgery where the sensory engine is real (measurable paranoia reductions in the audiology-anchored literature; the Indian execution: district rehabilitation centre tier, the MCI note's cost map)
- Social-channel rebuilding: the day-structure, the senior-citizens' association, the temple-committee role, isolation's antidote as prescription
- Depression treated (the elderly-depression ladder with ECT for the psychotic melancholic tier, the elderly-depression note's territory, M5)
- Delirium's source treated; the dementia's BPSD managed trigger-first (the Dementia Management note's five floors, carer education, trigger-hunting, environment structuring BEFORE the prescription)
- The medication audit executed: anticholinergic-load reduction, benzo taper (the Benzo note's bridge logic), dopaminergic-dose negotiation where Parkinson's

**2: Antipsychotic pharmacotherapy (the cautious core):**
- **The dose law**: start at a quarter-to-half of young-adult starting doses (e.g., risperidone 0.25–0.5 mg, olanzapine 2.5 mg, aripiprazole 2.5–5 mg, quetiapine 12.5–25 mg at night), titrate at half-steps at weekly-plus intervals, expect effect at fractions of the young-adult effective dose
- **The cautions in order of lethality**: (a) the regulatory increased-mortality/stroke warnings for antipsychotics in dementia-related psychosis (prescribe only for severe risk/distress, at lowest dose, with documented periodic review-and-reduction attempts, the deprescribing discipline, not the prescription-and-forget culture); (b) the Lewy body sensitivity catastrophe (the wallet-card rule from the LBD note: NO typicals; the atypicals at micro-doses with extreme caution, quetiapine the usual compromise); (c) falls, sedation, orthostasis; (d) the metabolic and prolactin layers even at low doses
- **Choice logic for primary late-onset psychosis**: risperidone (the best late-onset evidence base, careful above 1–2 mg), olanzapine (metabolic caution in the sedentary elder), aripiprazole (the lower-sedation option; akathisia watch), quetiapine (the LBD/Parkinson's-compromise when sensitivity risk exists; orthostasis caution); avoid high-potency typicals and pimozide except in the specific delusional-disorder-cardiology-checked cases (the Delusional Disorder note's ECG rule)
- **The maintenance question**: late-onset primary psychoses often hold remission at LOW maintenance doses after 6–12 months, and a supervised slow-taper attempt is reasonable after a stable year, the Indian execution: the dated-review card in the family's hand
- **Adherence engineering in the Indian joint family**: the medicine-administration role (the daughter-in-law who already runs the diabetic-and-BP schedule absorbs the antipsychotic slot), the simplification principle (once-daily dosing), and depot strategies ONLY where the family cannot sustain daily administration and the illness's cost is severe (the geriatric depot doses are fractions of the young-adult ones)
- *India note (approx 2026): risperidone ₹30–150/month, olanzapine ₹50–250, quetiapine ₹100–400, aripiprazole ₹150–600; the geriatric pharmacovigilance reality is that the dose-discipline is documented in fewer than half of Indian geriatric prescriptions; the dated-review card is the counter-architecture*

**3: The family work (the siege-management module):**
- The accused-party counsel: no counter-accusation sieges (the daughter-in-law-versus-mother-in-law war is the illness's fuel); the "assume the symptom" script ("she believes it fully; arguing strengthens it; redirect, don't debate")
- The confrontation-protective planning for the neighbour-wars: the family's designated negotiator, the immediate-neighbour briefing where safe, the legal-risk audit when the accused is escalating
- The carer-load assessment and support (the family's own burnout treating the elder's illness, the caregiver's depression screen)
- The reframe of "possessed/evil-eye" family theories: the medical frame delivered without contempt (the local idiom respected, the mechanism translated, the Acute-Transient note's discipline for possession presentations)

**4, ECT in this population (the honest position):** for psychotic depression in the elderly, for the catatonic and the food-refusing wasting patient, and for the medication-intolerant severe case, ECT is often the SAFEST and fastest option (the modern-modality reality vs the family's film-era terror; the consent-architecture and the cognitive-side-effect honesty: transient memory disturbance, monitored, recovering).

**5, What not to do:** the prescription-and-forget culture (no review dates); the dose-escalation reflex for "worsening" that is actually drug-confusion; the typicals in any patient with parkinsonism; the polypharmacy stacking (antipsychotic + benzo + anticholinergic, the fall-and-fog cascade); and the "she is 75, this is just old age" dismissal, the treatable engines left running for years.

*India note (approx 2026): the geriatric-psychiatry service density is metro-concentrated; the realistic spine is the general-physician + family-pharmacist execution of the dose-and-review discipline, the district-lab/audiometry tier for the co-riders, and the Tele-MANAS/family-physician checkpoints for follow-through. The highest-yield Indian geriatric-psychosis interventions cost almost nothing: the hearing test, the medication audit, and the dated-review card.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **The deaf-elder audit is the highest-yield act.** Indian elders average decades of unaided presbycusis; the "paranoid" widow who hears voices through the fan and whispers-about-her from the kitchen is a frequent audiology patient mis-routed to psychiatry, and psychiatry that skips the audiometry treats the wrong engine at the wrong cost. Pair every late-life paranoia assessment with the hearing test (the district rehabilitation centre route; the MCI note's cost map).
- **The daughter-in-law accusation is a clinical pattern.** The joint family's theft-accusations toward the daughter-in-law carry three layers needing untangling: the illness's misplacement engine (the re-hidden purse), the dependency-power dynamics (the accusation as the last power-lever), and occasionally the real abuse of the elder (the elder's claim is not always illness, the family-violence screen belongs in the assessment before dismissing every accusation as paranoia).
- **The "old age talk" dismissal.** "Budhape ki baat hai": the Indian family's normalisation that delays assessment by years; the counter-frame: "the things she says are not age; they are symptoms; some of them are treatable this month".
- **The Ayurveda-tonic-and-faith-healer tier.** Late-life delusions are read as possession-and-astrology layer; the engagement discipline: translate the idiom, keep the family's engagement, route the engine to medicine (the possession-frame handling in the Acute-Transient-Psychosis note applies to elders identically).
- **The pension-and-property siege.** Persecutory content attaches to inheritance anxieties in Indian households; the family's protective work includes the legal-structuring conversation done EARLY (while capacity stands, the Dementia-Management note's advance-planning floor), which reduces the property-stress that feeds the illness.
- **The single-room observation problem.** Joint families bring the elder for assessment WITHOUT the months of behaviour-history documentation that the diagnosis needs: the three-signs notebook (the MCI note's tool) adapted for psychiatric events (dated entries of what was believed, when it worsened, what followed) converts the family's anecdote-storm into assessment data.
- **The depot temptation.** Indian families sometimes request "a long injection to settle it permanently"; the depot is a legitimate tool for adherence-failure in severe illness, NOT a convenience-chemical-restraint for family peace; the geriatric depot doses and the review discipline apply with double force.

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: the gas through the wall.** A 68-year-old Lucknow widow, living with her son's family, was brought after 14 months of escalating accusations: the upstairs neighbours were "pumping gas through the ceiling" at night (she sealed her windows with tape and cloth), her food "tasted of medicine" (she had shifted to packaged biscuits and lost 6 kg), and her daughter-in-law "searches my box when I bathe". She spoke coherently, was groomed, managed her own pension account, attended temple daily, and scored 26/30 on a literacy-adjusted MoCA. The three-signs notebook (retrospectively assembled by the son) showed a suspicion-first, not memory-first, timeline, no cognitive decline over the year. The audit: audiometry revealed severe bilateral presbycusis (never tested; "she just says 'what' to everyone"); the medication list revealed a nightly anticholinergic bladder tablet and 12 years of alprazolam 0.5 mg; TSH/B12 normal; MRI showed mild age-consistent changes only. Diagnosis: very-late-onset schizophrenia-like psychosis with persecutory and partition themes, riding on a severe sensory-deprivation and sedative-load layer. Management: the co-riders first, bilateral hearing aids fitted (the daughter-in-law accompanied the audiology visit: the first therapeutic act, relationship-wise), the bladder tablet substituted, and the alprazolam converted to a gradual taper; then risperidone 0.25 mg at night (the geriatric dose law), titrated to 0.5 mg over three weeks; the family scripts (no debates on the gas-claims; the redirect-to-temple script; the daughter-in-law's role explicitly re-framed and honoured in the treatment plan; the alliance that would carry the medication schedule); the taped windows ignored rather than fought (the "do not battle the symptom's props" rule); the dated-review card in the son's hand. At 3 months: food refusals gone, weight regained, the gas-claims stated once weekly rather than hourly, window-tape removed by herself ("I don't think they do it now"); hearing aids worn daily (the son's report: "she answers the phone; the house is louder"). At 9 months: on 0.5 mg, remission of the partition-delusion, residual mild referential suspicion; the taper-discussion scheduled. Teaching points: (1) the audiometry was the highest-yield test in the assessment; (2) the co-rider treatment preceded the antipsychotic and was half the treatment; (3) the geriatric dose ran at a twelfth of the young-adult ceiling; (4) the daughter-in-law's alliance was engineered explicitly (the accused-party counsel); (5) the notebook's suspicion-first timeline excluded the dementia story, the differential's spine.

**Case 2: the visitors in the sitting room.** A 74-year-old retired Cochin schoolmaster was brought by his wife for "seeing people at night": for six weeks he had described a "family of four" sitting in the sitting room at dusk, occasionally "two children in the garden", and had begun, in the last two weeks, to call out to them, and once to accuse his wife of "replacing the real one". History assembled: 18 months of "his walking dreams" (shouting, punching in sleep, the RBD flag, unrecognised), a two-year softening of his handwriting and gait (the parkinsonism creeping), a memory decline the wife dated to "a year, maybe more", and daytime naps with fluctuating alertness. The critical medication fact: his GP had started quetiapine 25 mg three weeks earlier for "the visions" and he had become profoundly sedated and twice fallen. On examination: cogwheel rigidity bilaterally, slowed gait, MoCA 19/30 with attention-fluctuation across the session; hallucinations described with curious calm ("they don't trouble me much; I know they shouldn't be there"). Diagnosis: dementia with Lewy bodies, visual hallucinations with emerging delusional misidentification, RBD, parkinsonism, cognitive fluctuation; the quetiapine dose (though the "gentle" choice in principle) had overshot his sensitivity. Management: quetiapine reduced to 12.5 mg at night (the LBD micro-dose reality, the wallet-card discipline applied), the falls audit done (bed rail, night-light, the bathroom route cleared), carer education from the LBD note's package (the hallucinations' "benign-report" channel, engage and assess, don't medicate every vision), and a rivastigmine discussion begun with the neurologist (the LBD's best-evidence cognitive agent). At 3 months: the "family of four" reported weekly rather than nightly, no falls since the reduction, misidentification cleared, and the wife's notebook documenting the pattern (the good days following nights of good sleep, the fluctuation map). Teaching points: (1) the "psychosis" was a dementia's opening chapter, the reorder-rule in action (the LBD workup preceded any primary label); (2) the RBD history, sitting unrecognised for 18 months, was the retro-diagnosis; (3) even quetiapine at "low" geriatric doses can overshoot in LBD, the micro-dose discipline; (4) the hallucination-management for LBD runs carer-education-first (the "don't medicate every vision" doctrine); (5) the misidentification ("the real one") was the family's alarm-signal, correctly routed to assessment rather than to the tantrum-channel.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way families ask it

**"Is she going mad at 70? Is this schizophrenia like her nephew?"** It is a psychosis (but the late-life version is a different animal from the young-adult illness: it runs shallower, often responds to small doses, and frequently rides on things we can treat) her deafness, her tablets, her depression, sometimes a dementia's beginnings. The nephew's illness and hers may share a name in the family's fear; they do not share the course.

**"The doctor said antipsychotics can be dangerous in the elderly. Should we avoid them altogether?"** The caution is real: in dementia-related agitation-psychosis, these medicines carry warnings of increased stroke and mortality, which is why our discipline is: treat the co-riders first, use the medicine only for severe distress or danger, at the smallest dose, with written review dates and periodic taper attempts. Avoided altogether, a severe delusion (food-refusal, assault) can kill too; the skill is in the prescribing architecture, not in refusal.

**"She keeps saying her daughter-in-law steals from her box. Do we confront them both?"** Don't run a trial. The re-hidden purse is the dementia-or-delusion's classic trick; the object is hidden (often by the patient herself) and reported stolen. Arguing strengthens the belief and wages war on the marriage of the household. The script: sympathise with the feeling ("your things feel unsafe, that's upsetting; let's lock the box and keep the key with you"), redirect, and bring the pattern to the treating doctor. And once, just once, quietly verify that no real theft or neglect is happening; elders who are actually mistreated also say so, and are also disbelieved.

**"He is 76 and says people come through the wall. Isn't this just old age talking?"** "Just old age" is the sentence that costs this population years of treatable illness. The partition-delusion pattern is a symptom with a differential (deafness, a dementia's beginnings, a late-onset psychosis) and several of those are treatable this month. Age explains slowness; it does not explain sealed windows and 6-kg weight loss.

**"Her hearing is bad but she refuses the hearing aid; she says it makes the voices clearer."** That complaint is real: an aid amplifies everything, including misheard sound, in the first weeks. The audiology answer is fitting-and-follow-up (the programming adjustment), not abandonment, and in the medium run the aided ear feeds the brain real context, which shrinks the suspicious gap-filling. Start with part-time wearing hours, choose the comfortable model, and let the family's patience carry the first month.

**"Won't risperidone make him shaky like his brother-in-law on those tablets?"** Drug-induced parkinsonism in elders is dose-dependent and common, which is why the geriatric dose starts at a fraction of the young-adult one, titrates slowly, and is reviewed on written dates. If shakiness appears, it is a dose-adjustment signal (or a Lewy body alert), not a life sentence; we adjust, substitute, or stop. The un-prescribed delusion is also a danger; the skill is dosing, not refusal.

**"Should we tell the neighbours she has accused them? It's very embarrassing."** Where the accusation is escalating, a quiet word with a designated senior neighbour (never a public confrontation) prevents the doorstep war; most neighbours, told "it's an illness and the family is treating it", become allies rather than litigants. The embarrassment is the illness's; the handling is the family's dignity, done calmly, it protects both households.

**"She has stopped eating because she thinks we poison the food. What do we do TODAY?"** This is the emergency end of the condition: today; sealed packaged food she opens herself, food she watches being cooked, one trusted feeder, and the doctor the same week (weight-loss clocks matter; this is a treat-this-week severity). And if the food-refusal rides on a depression-with-guilt-content, the response includes the ECT-tier conversation early; in the elderly, that option is often the fastest life-saver, and the film-era fear should not decide it.

**"Can we try without medicine, only the hearing aid and family changes?"** Worth an honest trial in mild cases: the co-riders are half the treatment, and some sensory-driven suspicion states quiet down substantially with the channel fixed, the sedatives cleared and the days re-peopled. But track it with the notebook and a review date, if the sealed windows and food-logic persist past the trial, the medicine joins; "trying without" is a plan, not a hope.

**"Will she need the tablet forever?"** Late-onset psychoses often hold remission at LOW doses after 6–12 months, and a supervised slow-taper attempt after a stable year is reasonable. Some elders relapse at any taper and stay on their fraction-dose for good; at which dose the harm ledger is usually kind. What she never needs is prescription-and-forget: the review card is part of the prescription.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**The classification skeleton for viva:** late-onset schizophrenia (onset >40) and very-late-onset schizophrenia-like psychosis (>60), dimensional use of schizophrenia/delusional-disorder criteria with onset specifiers; the phenomenological signature (persecutory/partition/misidentification, hallucinations, faint negative symptoms, preserved function); female predominance in the very-late form; sensory-impairment association.

**Mnemonic, "D-D-D-S-P" (the reorder-rule):** Delirium, Dementia, Depression, Substances/medications, THEN Primary psychosis. Or "the four before the label": first the flags, then the deafness, then the drugs, then the diagnosis.

**High-yield facts:**
- Late-onset = >40; very-late-onset = >60; ~a quarter of schizophrenia-spectrum cases begin after 40
- Very-late form: female-predominant, strongly associated with deafness/isolation/migrant status
- Course SHALLOWER than early-onset; response to fraction-doses
- Antipsychotics in dementia-related psychosis: increased mortality/stroke warnings → lowest-dose, severe-cases-only, documented periodic review/deprescribing
- The LBD neuroleptic-sensitivity catastrophe (typicals forbidden; atypicals micro-dosed)
- The geriatric dose law: quarter-to-half starting doses, half-step titration
- ECT in elderly psychotic depression/wasting food-refusal: often the SAFEST fast option
- Sensory correction (aids, cataract) = measurable paranoia reduction (the co-rider evidence)
- Partition delusions (gas-through-wall, intruders-through-ceiling) = the classic late-life theme
- Indian audits: hearing test in every late-life paranoia; the anticholinergic-benzo fog audit; the daughter-in-law-accusation three-layer analysis

**Classic traps:**
1. Labeling late-life psychosis "schizophrenia" without the delirium-dementia-depression reorder (the beginning of mistreatment)
2. Missing the deafness engine (treating the muffled channel with dopamine blockers)
3. The quetiapine-25-mg "gentle dose" overshooting in an LBD patient (falls, sedation)
4. Prescription-and-forget geriatric antipsychotics (no review dates, no deprescribing attempts)
5. The daughter-in-law accusation routed to the family-court instead of the clinic (and the real-abuse screen skipped)
6. The benzo-decade unmasked as "confusion-with-ideas", escalated instead of tapered
7. Psychotic depression missed under "paranoid elder" (the guilt-content probe skipped, the ECT-tier fork missed)
8. "Budhape ki baat" dismissal running for years (the treatable engines left untreated)

**Indian exam corner:** expected patterns ("a 70-year-old woman with delusions of neighbours pumping gas) differential diagnosis and management" (the reorder-rule full ladder); "very-late-onset schizophrenia-like psychosis: features"; "antipsychotic use in elderly with dementia: cautions"; "psychosis vs BPSD differentiation". Indian-context marks: the deaf-elder audit, the joint-family daughter-in-law pattern, the Ayurveda-faith-healer engagement route, the dose-and-review card.

**1. Very-late-onset schizophrenia-like psychosis is defined by onset after:**
A. 25
B. 40
C. 60
D. 75
**Answer: C.** >60 (late-onset itself is >40); female-predominant, sensory-impairment-associated.

**2. The FIRST step in assessing an elderly patient with new "strange ideas":**
A. Start a low-dose antipsychotic
B. Rule out delirium, then dementia, depression and medication causes (the reorder-rule)
C. Order genetic testing
D. Arrange admission
**Answer: B.** Age reorders the differential: the primary label is the LAST stop.

**3. The most productive single investigation in late-life paranoia:**
A. EEG
B. Audiometry (the unaided presbycusis engine)
C. PET scan
D. Lumbar puncture
**Answer: B.** The muffled-world engine, and the hearing aid becomes part of the treatment.

**4. A 74-year-old with formed visual hallucinations, parkinsonism and dream-acting develops severe sedation and falls on quetiapine 25 mg. The lesson:**
A. Quetiapine is contraindicated in all elderly
B. Lewy body sensitivity, even "gentle" atypical doses can overshoot; micro-dose, and typicall are forbidden
C. Double the dose to control the visions
D. Switch to haloperidol
**Answer: B.** The LBD catastrophe rule: the wallet-card discipline applied at geriatric scale.

**5. The regulatory position on antipsychotics in dementia-related psychosis:**
A. First-line for all BPSD
B. Warnings of increased mortality/stroke: severe cases only, lowest dose, documented periodic review/deprescribing
C. Proven safe in elders
D. Only typicals are restricted
**Answer: B.** The prescribing architecture that distinguishes discipline from refusal.

**6. Compared with early-onset schizophrenia, late-onset psychosis typically shows:**
A. More negative symptoms
B. Persecutory/partition themes, faint negative symptoms, preserved function, response to fraction-doses
C. Chronic hospitalisation courses

**Answer: B.** The island-in-a-preserved-sea signature: the shallow course.

**7. An elderly woman with psychotic depression and food refusal; the fastest life-saving option is often:**

**9. The daughter-in-law-theft accusation in a joint family warrants:**
