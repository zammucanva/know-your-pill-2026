# Mental Health Law — Capacity, Responsibility and the State's Duty

## 1. In one line + overview

**Mental health law answers three questions, can this person decide for herself (capacity), can she be held responsible (criminal liability), and who must answer when things go wrong (negligence and the duties of authorities), and the modern answer to all three is functional, individual and case-by-case, never status-based.**

The Oxford chapter teaches legal reasoning for psychiatrists through the English system, chosen because its principles travel. The **capacity revolution**: the shift from status law (children, "defectives", the married could not decide) to the **functional approach**, competence judged for *this decision, at this time*, through the four abilities of **Grisso and Appelbaum** (understanding, appreciation, reasoning, expressing a choice), codified in the Mental Capacity Act 2005 with its best-interests checklist and challengeable-decisions architecture. **Criminal responsibility**: mental disorder enters through the mental element of offences (a complete defence if absent), the insanity defence, **diminished responsibility** (the partial defence that converts murder to manslaughter), and intoxication's specific-intent/basic-intent distinction. **Negligence and psychiatric injury**: the Bolam standard, the primary/secondary-victim and "shocking event" rules for compensating psychiatric harm, the objective standard applied even to the mentally disordered defendant, and the courts' struggles with authorities' duties to protect third parties (Hill, Clunis, Osman). The chapter's deep theme: protection and rights are not opponents, good law protects the vulnerable *by* respecting their personhood.

## 4. Capacity: the decision revolution

**From status to function.** Law once answered capacity by category: minors could not consent (until *Gillick* recognised the mature minor's decision-making in 1985); a "defective" under the Sexual Offences Act 1956 could not consent to sexual activity *however competent she might actually be*, with the absurd corollary that marriage (competence-assessed at the ceremony) was lawful while the same woman's consent to sexual activity was legally void, disabling even legitimate protective sex education. Modern law abolishes categorical exclusion: the presence of mental disorder is not incapacity.

**The functional test.** Competence is **related to the particular decision, at the particular time it must be made**. The four abilities (Grisso & Appelbaum): **understanding** the relevant information, **appreciating** its application to one's own situation, **reasoning** with it (weighing), and **expressing a choice**. The abilities required vary by decision: *Banks v Goodfellow* (the classic wills case) demanded understanding of the business, recollection of the property, knowledge of the natural beneficiaries, and the manner of distribution, appreciation and reasoning at a will-writer's level, not a lawyer's. Crucially, an **unwise outcome is not proof of an unacceptable process**: the eccentric testator may lawfully disinherit dependants; questionable results are not the same as incapacity.

## 5. Deciding for the incapacitous: the MCA architecture

**Two philosophies**: paternalistic best interests vs **substituted judgement** (what this person would have chosen). The modern English synthesis: what the person wanted *is* in her best interests; where her wishes are known (past and present wishes, written statements, beliefs and values), they govern; where nothing is known, best interests is the only available approach. The old "responsible medical opinion" version of best interests (whatever a doctor would approve) was rightly criticised for professionalising a whole person's interests; judicial development required (1) that where several acceptable options exist, only one is truly in her best interests, and (2) that the calculation includes social, welfare and emotional factors, not merely medical ones. The **Mental Capacity Act 2005** codifies the checklist: not determining merely by age, appearance or behaviour-prompting assumptions; considering regaining capacity; permitting and encouraging participation; life-sustaining decisions untainted by a desire to bring about death; and weighing the person's past/present wishes, beliefs and values, and the views of anyone named, carers, attorneys, and court-appointed deputies.

**The architecture of safeguards**: some decisions excluded as too personal (marriage, consent to sex); independent mental capacity advocates where carers' views are unavailable; research under intrusive-treatment safeguards; the Court of Protection with preference for court decisions over deputy appointment; and, the chapter's central design point, **no initial judicial trigger, but universal challengeability**: most decisions for incapacitous people are made properly by carers, the system is manageable and inexpensive, and rights are protected by access to court rather than by routing every decision through it.

## 6. Criminal responsibility

Mental disorder enters criminal law at four doors. **First, the mental element (mens rea)**: most serious crimes require intention, recklessness, knowledge or belief; mental disorder may show the defendant lacked it, a **complete defence**. **Second, the insanity defence** (McNaghten rules): defect of reason from disease of the mind with not knowing the nature and quality of the act or that it was wrong, rarely used, and famously unable to accommodate irresistible impulse. **Third, diminished responsibility** (Homicide Act): "abnormality of mind (whether arising from a condition of arrested or retarded development of mind or any inherent causes or induced by disease or injury) as substantially impaired his mental responsibility for his acts and omissions in doing or being a party to the killing", a partial defence converting murder to manslaughter; impairment is decided by the **trier of fact**, though expert evidence (including, pragmatically, degree-of-impairment opinions that arguably exceed clinical expertise) steers the court; its original rationale was avoiding the death penalty, and its current reach includes arguments of incapacity to resist impulse, which McNaghten excludes. **Fourth, intoxication**: where drink or drugs negate intent, the defence succeeds **only for crimes of specific intent** (murder, theft, burglary, acquittal or conviction of a lesser basic-intent offence) and fails for basic-intent crimes (manslaughter, rape, unlawful wounding, the recklessness of voluntary intoxication itself supplying the mental guilt). Related: psychiatric considerations in fitness to plead, infanticide provisions for postpartum mothers, and the rare neurotic/factitious interfaces (the offending chapters detail these).

## 7. Negligence, psychiatric injury and the duties of authorities

**The negligence skeleton**: duty of care (foreseeability + proximity + just-and-reasonable per Caparo), breach of the objective reasonable standard (for doctors, the **Bolam** responsible-body-of-medical-opinion test, unless the practice fails logical analysis), causation, and tangible damage. With drug-risk information, courts move toward a stricter informed-consent standard; causation defences include the "would have consented anyway" argument.

**Psychiatric injury**: the law's awkward child. Physical injury plus psychiatric injury: uncontroversial. Psychiatric injury alone: hedged by floodgates and fraud fears (the chapter's critique: neither supported by empirical evidence; a system that fails genuine claims because fraud is *possible* is a strange justice). The machinery: **primary victims** (physically injured or within foreseeable physical-injury range) vs **secondary victims** (close physical proximity to the trauma, a close tie of love and affection, or rescuers), plus the **impact rule**: the event must strike the unaided senses, be sudden, and shock a person of ordinary fortitude; ordinary grief, fear and distress are not compensable, only recognised psychiatric injury. *Sion* (a father's slow 14-day watch, not shocking) versus *Tredget* (the chaotic delivery, sudden, shocking); US and Singaporean courts have rejected the shocking-event requirement; **creeping trauma** (contamination fear: the growth-hormone/CJD claims) is decided on foreseeability and proximity where the claimant group is small and identifiable. Acute stress reaction, as a recognised condition, should be compensable (modestly).

**The mentally disordered defendant** is held to the **objective reasonable-person standard**; the learner driver must drive like a competent driver (Nettleship v Weston); a duty tailored to the actor "has no place in the law of tort", because tort compensates victims rather than blames wrongdoers. Exceptions flicker at the edges (Mansfield v Weetabix: no liability where the defendant could not reasonably have known of his condition; carers and professionals who *do* know of instability may sometimes assume its risk).

**Authorities' duties for patients' violence**, the emotive line of cases: **Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire** (the Yorkshire Ripper investigation): police owe no general duty to individual victims to catch a specific criminal, later qualified by *Osman* (European Court: blanket immunity violates Article 6). **Palmer v Tees** (a discharged patient killed a child known to be in a relationship with him, no duty to *unidentified* future victims of identified patients: the "palpable" test failed on specificity). **Clunis v Camden and Islington** (a discharged patient stabbed a stranger at a station, the authority *owed the patient* a duty to provide care and aftercare under the Mental Health Act, and could be liable to the victim through the patient's person; the case turned on systemic failure to follow the care programme). The teaching: liability attaches to identifiable relationships and systemic statutory duties, not to a general duty to protect everyone from everyone; the European Convention increasingly presses against blanket immunities. **Treatment refusals and force-feeding**: section-63-type treatment provisions, the anorexic refusing food (treatment under the Act where the refusal is part of the disorder), and Bland-era precedent respecting competent refusals (Bouvia) frame the treatment/consent frontier.

## 8. Diagnosis: how the psychiatrist enters the courtroom

The psychiatrist's legal roles: capacity assessments (the four abilities, decision-specific, documented with the *process* as well as the outcome); expert evidence on criminal responsibility (mental state at the time of the offence, reconstructed from records, informants and interviews; knowing that degree-of-impairment opinions are legally demanded though epistemologically fragile); fitness-to-plead assessments; negligence-case expert reports; and risk-assessment reports that respect the Clunis lesson (care-programme substance, not mere prediction).

## 9. Management: practising lawfully

- **Assess capacity functionally**: never "she has dementia, so she cannot"; always "for *this* decision, at *this* time, she understands/appreciates/reasons/expresses, or does not".
- **Document the best-interests process** when capacity is absent: wishes and beliefs canvassed, participation encouraged, family and carers consulted, narrowest option chosen.
- **Prefer supported decision-making**: capacity may fluctuate; wait, explain differently, involve the familiar person.
- **In reports**: describe the reasoning, not just the conclusion; distinguish clinical opinion from legal verdicts (impaired responsibility is for the trier of fact).
- **In treatment refusals**: check capacity, check the statutory treatment provisions, check the advance statement, in that order.
- **In risk work**: the care programme is both the clinical and the legal protection; Hill and Clunis teach that systems, not predictions, are what courts judge.

## 10. India lens (local counterpart: flagged as non-Oxford context)

The Indian legal architecture differs in statute but converges on principle. The **Mental Healthcare Act 2017** replaced the Mental Health Act 1987 with a rights-based instrument: presumed capacity; **advance directives** and nominated representatives (India's statutory versions of the substituted-judgement logic); specific procedural safeguards for admission (independent admission reviews, prohibitions on chaining and unmodified ECT); and decriminalisation of attempted suicide (s.115: mirroring the chapter's protection-vs-punishment theme). Capacity jurisprudence Indian-style flows through guardianship (National Trust Act for ID and developmental disabilities), the RPwD Act, and evolving case law on consent and restraint, all functional in aspiration, patchy in application. Indian negligence law retains Bolam as the touchstone with Supreme Court refinements; the psychiatric-injury rules of English tort resonate in Indian motor-accident and compensation jurisprudence without exact parallel. The forensic psychiatry interface runs through BNS provisions (unsoundness of mind; diminished-responsibility analogues) and the Erwadi-chaining legacy that made the 2017 Act's humane provisions necessary. For Indian clinicians the practical translation: the functional capacity question, the documented best-interests process, the advance-directive and nominated-representative machinery, and the Clunis-style discipline of written, followed care plans: Indian law's answers to the same three questions.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: The unwise will.** An 84-year-old man with mild dementia and a difficult family proposes a new will leaving the bulk of his estate to a caretaker nephew and disinheriting his son. The family's lawyer requests a capacity assessment, expecting the dementia to settle it. Assessment: he understands he is making a will and what he owns (recites the property accurately), knows his son exists and what disinheritance means, appreciates the consequences for the relationship, gives his reasoning ("my son visited twice in ten years; Ravi has cared for me daily for four years"), polarised, perhaps, but reasoning nevertheless. Conclusion: testamentary capacity present; the unwise outcome is not the unacceptable process. Safeguarding note: witnessed assessment, contemporaneous documentation, and a separate check for undue influence (a legal, not medical, question) referred back to the solicitor. Teaching points: capacity is decision-specific; this man might fail a treatment decision and pass a will; the four abilities tested at will-writer level; eccentricity is not incapacity.

**Case 2: The discharged patient and the stranger.** A man with schizophrenia is discharged from an Indian district hospital with a diagnosis, a prescription and no follow-up plan; two months later, off medication and unwell, he pushes a stranger on a platform. The family of the victim demands to know who is responsible; the hospital's defence is that "psychiatrists cannot predict". The lawful-practice audit the case forces: was there a statutory duty to the patient (Indian discharge and aftercare norms)? was a care plan made, communicated, and followed (Clunis logic)? was the risk to identifiable persons ever flagged (Palmer specificity)? The clinical corrective: relapse-prevention planning, an appointed carer contact, medication supervision arrangements, and a crisis card, the systemic protections the law ultimately judges. Teaching points: courts examine the *system's* operation, not the clinician's clairvoyance; Hill and Clunis frame the duty question; documentation is the practical shield, and the genuine protection.

## 13. FAQ

**"She has dementia, doesn't that mean she can't consent?"**: No: capacity is decision-specific and time-specific. She may lack capacity for complex financial decisions while fully retaining it for choosing her carer or accepting a blood test. Assess the four abilities for *this* decision, each time it matters.
**"His choice is foolish, surely that proves incapacity?"**: The law separates outcome from process: people are entitled to unwise decisions made by acceptable reasoning. Only a defective process (not understanding, not appreciating, unable to weigh, unable to express) establishes incapacity.
**"Who decides for someone who cannot?"**: First, what she would have chosen (wishes, values, advance statements); failing that, best interests through the statutory checklist, participation encouraged, family and carers consulted, the narrowest effective option.
**"Does mental illness excuse crime?"**: It can negate the mental element (a complete defence), support insanity (rarely), or reduce murder to manslaughter through substantially impaired responsibility, but most offending by people with mental disorder involves neither, and disorder often coexists with full responsibility.
**"Can drunkenness be a defence?"**: Only narrowly: so drunk that the specific intent (for murder, theft, burglary) could not be formed, and then the basic-intent alternative remains; for most crimes the recklessness of getting drunk supplies the guilt.
**"Can we be sued when a patient harms someone?"**: The courts' answer: not for failing to predict, but possibly for failing to *care*; identifiable patients, specific victims, and abandoned care plans are where liability lives. The written, followed, communicated plan is both medicine and law.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**Q1.** The functional approach to capacity holds that competence:
- (a) Depends on diagnostic status
- (b) Is global and stable across decisions
- (c) Relates to the particular decision to be made, at the particular time it must be made
- (d) Ends with any psychiatric diagnosis

**Answer: (c).** Decision-specific and time-specific testing: the revolution that replaced status categories like minority or "defective" status.

**Q2.** Which set contains the four abilities identified by Grisso and Appelbaum?
- (a) Understanding, appreciation, reasoning, expressing a choice
- (b) Reading, writing, arithmetic, orientation
- (c) Memory, attention, language, visuospatial skill
- (d) Obedience, docility, gratitude, insight

**Answer: (a).** Understanding information, appreciating its personal application, reasoning with it, and communicating a stable choice, the core of treatment-consent capacity.

**Q3.** Under the best-interests approach of the MCA 2005, the decision-maker must NOT:
- (a) Encourage the person's participation
- (b) Consider past and present wishes and beliefs
- (c) Determine the matter merely on the basis of age, appearance, or behaviour-prompting assumptions
- (d) Consult carers and attorneys

**Answer: (c).** The statutory checklist explicitly forbids assumption-based determinations while mandating the participatory and consultative steps.

**Q4.** Diminished responsibility as a partial defence to murder requires:
- (a) Any intoxication
- (b) An abnormality of mind (from arrested/retarded development, inherent causes, disease or injury) substantially impairing mental responsibility
- (c) A diagnosis of depression
- (d) The victim's consent

**Answer: (b).** The Homicide Act formulation: decided by the trier of fact on expert-informed evidence; it converts murder to manslaughter, and can accommodate impulse-resistance arguments that McNaghten excludes.

**Q5.** A claim for psychiatric injury by a secondary victim (a parent who witnessed her child's accident) requires, in addition to proximity and a close tie of love and affection:
- (a) Nothing further
- (b) A sudden event shocking to a person of normal fortitude, impacting the unaided senses, causing a recognised psychiatric injury
- (c) Physical injury to the claimant
- (d) A prior psychiatric history

**Answer: (b).** The impact rule and the recognised-injury requirement: Sion (slow deterioration, failed) versus Tredget (sudden chaos, succeeded).

**Q6.** In Clunis v Camden and Islington, the health authority's liability exposure arose from:
- (a) A general duty to protect all citizens from all patients
- (b) Failure in the duty owed to the patient himself, systemic failure to provide arranged care and aftercare, through which the victim's claim proceeded
- (c) The psychiatrist's poor handwriting
- (d) Predicting violence insufficiently accurately

**Answer: (b).** The courts locate liability in identifiable relationships and abandoned statutory/systemic duties, care programmes, not clairvoyance.
