# Parasomnias (Sleepwalking, Sleep Terrors, REM Behaviour Disorder & Co.)

## 1. In one line + overview

**Parasomnias are behaviours that erupt out of sleep itself (walking, screaming, eating, acting out dreams) because the sleeping brain has partially woken into a mixed state; the two great families are the NREM "sleep-walking" kind (confused, unresponsive, eyes open, no memory, first-third-of-night) and the REM "dream-acting" kind (the dream being physically performed, a specific warning sign of future Parkinsonian illness).**

A sleeping brain is not uniformly "off" (see the Sleep Basics note). In parasomnias, the sleeping and waking states get GLUED together in part: enough motor system wakes to walk, scream or eat while the conscious, memory-forming system stays asleep. The NREM family (confusional arousals, sleepwalking, sleep terrors) runs on deep sleep in the first third of the night: the sleepwalker who roams with open eyes and blank face, the child who screams with terror and pushes away comfort, neither remembering anything by morning. The REM family runs on the dream-rich final hours: the nightmare (frightening dream, remembered, in a paralysed body) and its dangerous inversion, REM sleep behaviour disorder, where the dream's paralysis fails and the person physically fights, runs, or punches while fully asleep, and where, in older men, this is the earliest red flag of Parkinson's disease and Lewy body dementia appearing years later. Almost every family has a sleepwalker story; the clinical tasks are separating the benign from the dangerous, protecting sleepers from terraces and stairs, treating the triggers (sleep deprivation, fever, alcohol, sedatives), and, in the over-50 dream-fighter, sending for neurological evaluation.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Place each parasomnia on the night's map (NREM first-third vs REM last-third), the master discriminator
- Distinguish sleepwalking, confusional arousals and sleep terrors from each other, and from nocturnal epilepsy
- Distinguish nightmares from sleep terrors (the classic confusion)
- Recognise REM sleep behaviour disorder as a prodromal synucleinopathy red flag and route accordingly
- Run the safety-and-trigger protocol for NREM parasomnias (including the Indian terrace/stair reality)
- Use scheduled awakening (anticipatory waking) for frequent sleepwalking/sleep terrors in children
- Name imagery rehearsal therapy for nightmare disorder and the clonazepam/melatonin tier for RBD
- Handle the forensic and family questions: automatism, blame, the "possessed at night" frame

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Sleepwalking: up to a fifth of children have at least one episode; a few percent of adults (episodes continuing or recurring); runs in families strongly (twin-heritability data). Sleep terrors: common in young children (a few percent), usually outgrown by adolescence. Confusional arousals: very common in children, frequent in adults woken from deep sleep. Nightmare disorder: ~4% of adults, higher with PTSD (where nightmares are core). REM sleep behaviour disorder: rare in the young (present in narcolepsy and antidepressant-use states as a secondary form); in men over 50, the prevalence rises sharply, and the majority of these men develop a synucleinopathy (Parkinson's disease or Lewy body dementia) over the following 5–15 years, one of the most important prodromal windows in neurology.

**India.** No specific epidemiology; the Indian realities are architectural and cultural. Architecturally: the joint-family and urban-terrace sleep environment multiplies the DANGER surface, sleepwalkers on terraces, stairwells, near water tanks, balconies with low railings; the safety protocol is Indian-geography-specific. Culturally: NREM events at night are read through possession and spirit frames (the screaming child at 2 a.m. becomes a "haunted" child; the sleepwalking elder becomes "called by an ancestor"), routing families to healers before clinics; and PTSD nightmare populations (disaster, riot, violence cohorts) carry untreated nightmare disorder for years with sleep-fragmentation consequences. Epilepsy's nodal burden in India keeps frontal-lobe epilepsy (the great mimic) on every differential.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**NREM parasomnias (the arousal-disorder family):**
- Genetic loading: family history is one of the strongest risk factors known (sleepwalking in a parent multiplies a child's risk)
- Age: childhood peaks (the deep-sleep-rich years)
- The trigger stack, each deepening N3 or fragmenting sleep: sleep deprivation (the number-one trigger), fever and illness, alcohol (first-half sedation then rebound-fragmentation), sedatives/Z-drugs, stress, full bladder, night noise, sleep apnoea (fragmentation-driven events, the under-recognised amplifier)
- Priming: sleeping somewhere new, shifts in schedule

**REM-related parasomnias:**
- RBD: over-50 male, synucleinopathy prodrome (the core risk factor),narcolepsy (the young secondary form), antidepressant-associated (SSRI/SNRI/mirtazapine REM changes, common and commonly missed), withdrawal states
- Nightmare disorder: PTSD (the core driver), stress, medications (dopamine agonists, beta-blockers, SSRIs), withdrawal (alcohol, REM-suppressants stopping)

**Other parasomnias by name:** sleep enuresis (children: normal-timeline wetting vs the secondary-onset wetting that flags medical/psychological events); sleep-related eating disorder (often Z-drug-associated); bruxism (grinding: dental-visit territory, stress-correlated); sexsomnia (the rarest forensic NREM behaviour).

## 6. What happens in the mind/brain

**Story 1: the half-lit house.** Deep sleep is a house with the lights off and the staff rotating through repair departments (the N3 department). In the NREM parasomnias, a power-surge partially lights one wing: the motor wing wakes while the consciousness-and-memory wing stays dark. The sleepwalker is a house with the hallway lights on and the office locked, ambulatory, navigating familiar rooms by procedural memory, blank to questions, and writing nothing to the memory disk. This is why morning brings amnesia, why trying to wake the person mid-episode prolongs confusion, and why the treatments either deepen the night's staffing (sleep-extension, trigger-removal) or pre-empt the surge (scheduled awakening before the usual event time).

**Story 2: the dream engine with the brakes cut.** Normal dreaming runs with the body's brake cable cut BY DESIGN; REM-atonia paralyses the dreamer so the brain's night-flight simulator runs on paper. In REM behaviour disorder, the brake line is severed: the dream's fight-or-flight now drives real limbs, the sleeper who dream-punches the intruder breaks the bedside lamp, or his wife's orbital bone. The biological culprit sits in the brainstem's atonia-switching circuitry, the same territory that the synuclein diseases (Parkinson's, Lewy body) erode over years, which is why the dream-fighter of 55 becomes the tremor-patient of 65 in a majority of cases. RBD is the smoke alarm ringing years before the fire is visible.

**Story 3: the terror broadcast from the wrong studio.** Sleep terrors look like fear at maximum volume, but the script is autonomic (a surge from deep sleep, not a dreamed danger): the scream is real, the terror physiology real, and the child is actually LESS accessible than in a calm dream. Contrast the nightmare: a fully scripted frightening dream, remembered in detail, body paralysed, the child wakes INTO consciousness and seeks comfort (and can describe the monster). The two are opposites in every clinical dimension (memory, timing, mobility, responsiveness) and the treatment differs accordingly.

## 7. Symptoms

**NREM family (first-third-of-night, from deep sleep):**
- **Confusional arousals**: sitting up, mumbling, confused, slow-waking or returning to sleep; eyes may be open; 5–15 minutes of fog; amnesia
- **Sleepwalking**: walking, roaming familiar routes (to the door, the bathroom, the kitchen), performing semi-purposeful acts (arranging objects, unlocking doors, the danger acts), blank face, poor responsiveness, resistance to waking, amnesia; childhood-common, adult-continuation significant
- **Sleep terrors**: abrupt scream from deep sleep, terror physiology (sweating, racing heart, dilated pupils), non-responsive and may push comfort away (the "worse if held" sign), 10–20 minutes, returns to sleep, TOTAL amnesia (while confusional; the child may recall a vague pressure of fear, not a story)

**REM family (final-third-of-night, dream-locked):**
- **Nightmares**: frightening dreams with recall; wake instantly into full consciousness; fear persists; movement minimal (paralysis intact); the PTSD-nightmare population's signature (and the insomnia-and-fear-of-sleep cascade)
- **REM behaviour disorder**: dream-enactment, shouting, punching, kicking, leaping from bed during clearly dream-like scenarios; the history comes from the BED PARTNER ("he was fighting off dacoits"); injuries to self and partner; the dream remembered immediately after waking, matching the enacted movements; over-50-men predominance (or the young narcolepsy/antidepressant-associated forms)

**Other parasomnias in brief:** sleep enuresis (the wetting after age-expected dryness, primary vs the secondary-onset flag); sleep-related eating (empty wrappers, kitchen chaos, no memory: Z-drug-associated); bruxism (morning jaw pain, dental wear); sleep sexsomnia (rare, forensic NREM behaviour).

**The red-flag audit for every parasomnia case:** injuries beyond bruises (the fall, the window), events in the second half of the night with stereotyped movements (epilepsy), daytime sleepiness with snoring (apnoea driving the events), and the over-50 dream-fighter (RBD→neurology referral).

## 8. Diagnosis

**The logic.** A convincing history of events erupting FROM SLEEP with the family-typic signature (NREM: first-third, confused/automatic, amnesia; RBD: last-third, dream-enacted, partner-observed); triggers identified; mimics excluded (chiefly nocturnal frontal-lobe epilepsy, stereotyped, clustered, brief events with postictal features; nocturnal panic attacks: full awakening with panic physiology and memory; dissociative states: longer, memory-poor, daytime-linked trauma patterns; the Z-drug complex-behaviour overlap).

**The assessment craft:**
- Video (the smartphone has made parasomnia diagnosis a family production), the blank face, the route, the responsiveness captured
- The bed-partner interview for anything REM-suspect: "what do his movements look like, is he fighting something?"
- The trigger inventory: sleep-debt week, fever, the wedding-season alcohol, the new Z-drug, the snoring
- The night-map: which third of the night? — the master sorting question
- For suspected RBD: polysomnography confirms loss of REM atonia, the definitive test; neurological examination and follow-up planning
- For suspected epilepsy-spectrum events: prolonged EEG/video-EEG referral

**Instruments/tests by name:** polysomnography with expanded EEG where epilepsy is on the differential; video-EEG telemetry for the refractory/unclear; no standard rating scales in general practice (questionnaire research tools exist for RBD).

| Event at night | The distinguishing features |
|---|---|

## 9. Management

**NREM parasomnias: the safety-and-trigger protocol (the treatment is mostly NOT a tablet):**
1. **Safety engineering first**: the sleeper's environment audited for the actual local hazards, Indian edition: terrace-door locks (high, outside reach), stair gates, balcony railings assessed, kitchen knives stored, the bathroom route cleared of furniture, ground-floor-room allocation for the frequent sleepwalker, ground-floor-room priority, window locks; in hostels: the lower bunk, the far-from-stairs bed
2. **Trigger removal**: sleep-debt repaid (earlier nights), fever managed, alcohol cut before bed, the triggering sedative swapped or stopped, nocturia treated (the full bladder as an arousal trigger), and, the hidden amplifier, sleep apnoea treated (the fragmentation driver)
3. **Guidance for events**: do not force-wake (prolongs confusion); gently redirect back to bed ("the autopilot accepts steering, not shouting"); supervise until settled
4. **Scheduled awakening** (the specific behavioural treatment for frequent childhood events): the parent wakes the child 15–30 minutes BEFORE the usual event time for 2–4 weeks, then fades, pre-empting the deep-sleep surge; evidence-supported for sleep terrors and sleepwalking in children
5. **Medication tier (rarely needed, severe/injury-risk cases)**: clonazepam at night in small doses (the tradition), or low-dose imipramine-class, specialist-tier decisions, always AFTER safety and triggers, always time-limited with review

**Nightmare disorder:**
- Treat the driver: PTSD treatment (see the PTSD note, trauma-focused therapy reduces the nightmare load itself)
- **Imagery rehearsal therapy**, the specific technique: the recurring nightmare rewritten with a changed ending, the new version rehearsed by day, 10 minutes daily; a well-supported, medication-free treatment
- Screen the medication list (dopamine agonists, beta-blockers, SSRIs) and withdrawal states (alcohol; stopped REM-suppressants)

**REM behaviour disorder:**
- **Confirm with polysomnography; refer to neurology for synucleinopathy-risk counselling and follow-up** (the 5–15-year window; research-stage neuroprotective trials run in this population, an honest, hopeful framing)
- Symptom protection: the bedroom re-engineered (padding, floor-mattress placement, sharp objects out, the partner to a separate bed in injury-risk cases)
- **Melatonin 3–12 mg** (the front-line in most programmes, gentle) and **clonazepam 0.5–2 mg** (the traditional agent; falls and cognition weighed in elders); with the partner's report as the outcome measure
- Antidepressant-associated RBD: the SSRI/SNRI review conversation (weighing psychiatric need vs REM-atonia effects)

**Other parasomnias:** enuresis (the child-wetting workup and behavioural programme, alarm systems where accessible; the DESMOPRESSIN tier via paediatrics; the shame-management briefing to families); sleep-related eating (the Z-drug stop); bruxism (dental guards, stress work).

*India note (approx 2026): melatonin 3–10 mg ≈ ₹150–400/month (quality variance, reputable brands); clonazepam ≈ ₹30–80/month (the dependence conversation owed when prescribed); polysomnography with expanded EEG is metro/medical-college tier (₹6,000–15,000 private); video-EEG telemetry scarcer. The free treatments dominate: the safety-audit walk-through of the house (the terrace-lock prescription), the scheduled-awakening protocol (a sheet of paper and two weeks of parent discipline), sleep-debt repayment, and imagery rehearsal (a notebook and ten minutes a day). The Indian forensic-context note: sleepwalking-based automatism defences in criminal cases are rare, contested, and hinge on PSG/video-documented parasomnia history; the clinician's role in documented cases is precise record-keeping, not courtroom improvisation.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **The terrace and the staircase are the Indian parasomnia's teeth.** Most Indian sleepwalking injuries cluster on the architecture: terraces with low parapets, open stairwells, overhead water tanks, balcony access from the bedroom. The safety audit is done by walking the house WITH the family; the terrace door gets a high bolt OUTSIDE the child's reach, the frequent walker's bed moves to the ground floor, the stair gate goes up. This walk prevents more harm than any prescription.
- **The possession frame.** Night-screaming children and wandering elders are read as spirit-afflicted in many households; the healers arrive before the clinic. The engagement script (see the previous notes' faith stance): ally with the peace the ritual brings, add the physiology ("the scream comes from deep sleep, not from a visitor; the child does not even remember it"), and let the safety-and-trigger protocol carry the outcome. Mocking the frame loses the family; borrowing its language carefully ("the nights are troubled; let us quiet them") keeps the treatment in the house.
- **The joint-family night-watch.** Grandmothers run an informal scheduled-awakening system already ("I wake him before the time it comes"); formalise it: give the pattern a sheet, a week of times, a fade plan, and the family's existing vigilance becomes the evidence-based intervention.
- **The over-50 dream-fighter is a neurological referral, not a family quarrel.** Indian RBD patients surface through two doors: the wife with bruising presenting to orthopaedics/medicine, and the family describing "he fights with someone in his sleep" attributed to daytime conflicts. The PSG confirmation and neurology referral route into the prodrome window; the moment when Parkinsonian motor signs are absent but treatable-watchful planning is possible.
- **The Z-drug household.** India's OTC Z-drug economy manufactures complex sleep behaviours (night eating, night driving in extreme cases), every parasomnia history includes the "what tablet do you take for sleep, and is it from the chemist without paper?" question; the behaviour usually resolves on withdrawal.
- **PTSD nightmare cohorts.** Disaster-, riot-, and violence-exposed populations carry nightmare disorder for years; the district psychologist trained in imagery rehearsal (a teachable, low-cost technique) can run it in groups, a rare example of specialist-technique scalability.
- **Enuresis and shame.** The bedwetting school-age child is punished and hidden in many households; the clinic's first act is the family briefing (wetting is a sleep-bladder maturity timeline, not defiance), the second is the workup, the third is the programme.

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: the terrace-walking boy.** An 11-year-old in a Jaipur joint-family household had twice been found on the terrace at 2 a.m. (once unlocking the door, once standing at the parapet edge), the family's terror doubled as a spirit-story ("the grandfather calls him"). Events clustered in exam weeks and fever months; a detailed history showed first-third timing, blank unresponsiveness, total amnesia, a sleepwalking father, and daily 6.5-hour nights (the debt). Management: the safety walk (terrace door re-bolted high outside reach; bedroom moved ground-floor; stair gate made from a shop rack); trigger protocol (sleep extended to 9.5 hours; fever-nights pre-planned with the grandmother as monitor); scheduled awakening at 1:15 a.m. (fifteen minutes before the typical event) for three weeks, then faded; the family's spirit-frame engaged respectfully (the ritual retained, the physiology added). Events fell from twice-monthly to one mild wandering in six months. Teaching points: (1) safety engineering precedes everything in Indian parasomnias; the parapet was the diagnosis's real danger; (2) the father's history = the genetic loading the exam wants; (3) scheduled awakening is the evidence-based behavioural core; (4) the spirit-frame ally not enemy.

**Case 2: the man who fought dacoits in his sleep.** A 58-year-old retired schoolmaster in Thrissur was brought by his wife after he leapt from bed "fighting off attackers" and fractured his metacarpal against the almirah; two years of escalating episodes (shouting, punching the air, once kicking her) always in the last hours of sleep, always with a vivid dream recalled immediately ("dacoits at the door; I was blocking them"). He was on mirtazapine for sleep (started by a GP). PSG confirmed REM sleep without atonia: REM behaviour disorder; antidepressant contribution weighed; neurology examination normal at baseline. Management: mirtazapine tapered with the GP's concurrence; the bedroom re-engineered (floor mattress, almirah moved, sharp objects out, wife's bed temporarily relocated); melatonin 6 mg escalated to 12 mg; neurology follow-up scheduled annually with the honest prodrome counselling ("this can precede Parkinson's-type changes by years; we watch, and research programmes exist for this exact window"). At 6 months: episodes reduced to rare mild episodes (the wife's report as the outcome measure), no injuries; the couple's own line: "we sleep in peace again, and we know what to watch for". Teaching points: (1) RBD's diagnosis lives in the bed-partner's history, "what does he fight?"; (2) the over-50 dream-fighter gets PSG + neurology referral (the prodrome window); (3) melatonin-first pharmacology with clonazepam in reserve; (4) antidepressant-associated RBD deserves the taper conversation, weighed against psychiatric need.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way patients and families ask it

**"My son walks in his sleep. Is something wrong with his brain?"** The sleepwalking brain is healthy; it is a family trait that shows itself when sleep gets too deep or too short. With the safety fixes, the sleep-hours restored, and (if needed) a brief waking-protocol, most children outgrow it. What IS wrong is leaving the terrace door reachable, that, we fix today.

**"The child screams like something is hurting him, but remembers nothing. What was it?"** That is a sleep terror, a surge from the deepest sleep, not a dream and not pain. He is truly unresponsive and will not remember; holding him tightly usually makes it worse. The rule: keep him safe, let the storm pass, and do not try to wake him hard.

**"Is he possessed? Our elders say an ancestor calls him at night."** The night-events are real and frightening, and your elders' frame deserves respect, but the pattern we see (blank face, no memory, same time of night, worse with fever and lost sleep) is the fingerprint of deep-sleep partial waking. Keep whatever rituals bring the house peace; add the safety locks and the sleep hours; the nights will quiet.

**"He acts out his dreams: actually fights. Should we be worried about his mind?"** His MIND is intact; his dream-paralysis is not. This specific condition appears in older men and can be the earliest signal of Parkinsonian changes years later, so we treat the bedroom's safety now and arrange watchful neurological follow-up. Fear of the dream-fights is managed with melatonin and simple measures; the future gets monitoring, not dread.

**"Can she hurt herself? How do we stop it?"** Injuries happen not from the walking but from the ROUTE, stairs, terraces, sharp furniture. We walk your house together and rebuild the route: high bolts, gates, cleared floors, and for the frequent walker, the ground-floor room. The behaviour fades; the safety net must not.

**"The priest/exorcism was done. The episodes reduced."** Two things can be true: the house gained peace (which settles nights), and the protocol gained traction (which settles the deep-sleep surges). If the events return with fevers or lost sleep, that pattern is the physiology, and the protocol is the response, keep both your rituals and our sheet.

**"Should we wake him during an episode to break it?"** No: forced waking prolongs the confusion and can trigger frightened, flailing resistance. Walk alongside, steer gently back to bed ("the autopilot accepts steering"), and stay until settled. Waking is only for danger, and even then, from a distance, with noise rather than hands.

**"My daughter has the same nightmare every week. Can anything be done?"** Yes, a specific technique works: we rewrite the nightmare's ending together, and she rehearses the new version by day for a few minutes daily. It sounds simple; it is one of the better-supported treatments in sleep medicine. If the nightmares carry trauma's fingerprints, the trauma work itself treats them.

**"I took a sleeping tablet from the chemist, and now I eat at night with no memory."** That tablet (a Z-class or sedating antihistamine) can cause complex sleep behaviours, eating, walking, even driving asleep. This resolves with stopping the tablet. We will manage your sleep the structured way instead (see the Insomnia note), safely and without a night-kitchen.

**"He wets the bed at 9. Is he lazy?"** Wetting after the expected age is not laziness; it is a maturity timeline in the bladder-to-brain wiring, and at 9 with new-onset wetting, we also check medical causes. Punishment (still common) worsens everything; the programme (bladder training, alarms, the bedtime rules) does the work.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**The master grid to memorise (write it in every answer):**

| | NREM terrors/walking | Nightmares | RBD |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sleep stage | N3, first-third | REM, last-third | REM, last-third |

| Movement | Automatic roaming | Paralysed (minimal) | Enacted fighting/leaping |

| Core treatment | Safety + triggers + scheduled awakening | IRT + driver treatment | PSG, neurology, melatonin/clonazepam |

**Mnemonics:**
- NREM triggers, "**D-FABS**: Deprivation, Fever, Alcohol, Bladder, Sedatives (apnoea the amplifier)"
- RBD triad: "**Dream-acted, Partner-witnessed, Parkinson's-preceding**"

**High-yield facts:**
- Sleepwalking's strong familial loading (the exam's genetics point)
- Sleep terrors vs nightmares: the perennial one-marker
- RBD = prodromal synucleinopathy (majority conversion over 5–15 years in older men), the modern classic
- Antidepressant-associated RBD exists (young, missed)
- Scheduled awakening = the behavioural evidence answer for childhood terrors/walking
- Imagery rehearsal therapy = nightmare disorder's specific treatment
- Nocturnal frontal-lobe epilepsy = the great mimic (stereotyped, clustered, postictal)
- Z-drug complex behaviours: the pharmacological-forensic overlap
- Melatonin (front-line) vs clonazepam (tradition) in RBD
- Safety engineering before sedation: the principle that separates protocols from prescriptions

**Classic traps:**
1. Diagnosing "nightmares" in a first-third, amnesiac, screaming child (sleep terrors)
2. Missing RBD in the over-50 man with night-injuries (treating it as a "behaviour problem")
3. Sedating a child with sleepwalking BEFORE safety and trigger work
4. Missing apnoea as the fragmentation driver of "refractory" parasomnias
5. Missing frontal-lobe epilepsy in stereotyped clustered events
6. Missing the Z-drug behind night-eating/night-walking

**Indian exam corner:** " differentiate nightmares from sleep terrors" (guaranteed), "parasomnias (classification and management", "REM behaviour disorder) significance", "sleepwalking, precipitants". Indian-context marks: terrace/stair safety engineering, the possession-frame engagement, PTSD nightmare cohorts, the Z-drug economy's complex behaviours.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. Sleep terrors differ from nightmares in that terrors:**
A. Occur in the last third of the night with vivid recall
B. Erupt from deep N3 sleep (first third) with screaming, non-responsiveness and amnesia
C. Are remembered in detail
D. Leave the child fully awake and comfort-seeking
**Answer: B.** The stage-timing-memory-responsiveness grid: the perennial discriminator.

**2. A 58-year-old man enacts violent dreams. The MOST important referral and reason:**
A. Psychiatrist: for psychosis
B. Neurology: RBD is a prodromal marker of synucleinopathies (Parkinson's/DLB)
C. Orthopaedics only
D. ENT
**Answer: B.** PSG-confirmed RBD in older men precedes Parkinsonian disease in a majority over 5–15 years.

**3. The evidence-supported behavioural treatment for frequent childhood sleep terrors:**
A. Nightly sedation
B. Scheduled awakening 15–30 minutes before the typical event time, faded over weeks
C. Punishment protocols
D. Forced waking during events
**Answer: B.** Pre-empting the deep-sleep surge: the paper-and-discipline treatment.

**4. The trigger stack for NREM parasomnias, pick the set:**
A. Sleep deprivation, fever, alcohol, full bladder, sedatives
B. Caffeine only
C. Daylight exposure
D. Exercise
**Answer: A.** With obstructive sleep apnoea as the under-recognised amplifier.

**5. Imagery rehearsal therapy treats:**
A. Sleepwalking
B. Nightmare disorder
C. Sleep terrors
D. Enuresis
**Answer: B.** Rewriting the nightmare's ending and rehearsing the new version by day.

**6. A Z-drug-associated complex sleep behaviour classically includes:**
A. Night-eating or night-walking with amnesia
B. Morning headache
C. Snoring
D. Bedwetting
**Answer: A.** The pharmacological parasomnia: resolves with withdrawal.

**7. During a sleepwalking episode, the recommended response is:**
A. Shake the person awake firmly
B. Gently redirect/steer back to bed without forcing wake
C. Restrain physically
D. Splash water
**Answer: B.** The autopilot accepts steering, not shouting, forced waking prolongs and agitates.

**8. The great epilepsy-mimic of NREM parasomnias is:**
A. Absence epilepsy
B. Nocturnal frontal-lobe epilepsy (stereotyped clustered motor events, postictal features)
C. Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy
D. Temporal-lobe epilepsy with déjà vu
**Answer: B.** Stereotypy + clustering + postictal features, video-EEG when suspected.
