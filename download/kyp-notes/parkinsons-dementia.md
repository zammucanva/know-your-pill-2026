# Dementia in Parkinson's disease

## 1. In one line + overview

**Tagline:** Many people living with Parkinson's disease slowly develop a thinking-and-perception decline on top of the movement disorder, **Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD)**, and the same two safety rules that govern Lewy body dementia apply here: **protect the brain chemistry with rivastigmine-type drugs, and treat any psychosis without triggering the movement catastrophe that old antipsychotics cause.**

**Overview, in plain words:** Parkinson's disease brings tremor, stiffness and slowness first. Over the years, the rule of thumb is that dementia arriving **more than a year after the movement problems** earns the label PDD, while dementia arriving before or alongside them is dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB, the previous note), roughly a quarter to a third of Parkinson's patients develop a dementia, and the longer they live with the illness, the higher that proportion climbs. Its face is the "subcortical" one: **slowing of thought, planning failure, visuospatial trouble and poor attention, while memory storage holds up longer** than it does in Alzheimer's. Alongside it travel hallucinations (frequently *caused or worsened by the very medicines that help the walking*) and the treacherous problem that almost every psychiatric drug interferes either with the movement disorder or with the psychosis. The good news is genuine: the chemical that most restores thinking and softens hallucinations is depleted in PDD, and replacing its supply buys real function. The rest of the treatment is timing, drug-list surgery, and family education about what is disease and what is a side effect.

## 4. Epidemiology

### Global
- Point prevalence of dementia in Parkinson's: roughly **25–40%**; cumulative incidence rises with follow-up duration and age; in long-horizon cohorts the majority of long-surviving patients are eventually affected.
- Strongest risks: **older age** (and older age at Parkinson's onset), motor severity/predominance of the non-tremor (postural-instability) type, early hallucinations, REM sleep behaviour disorder, and baseline cognitive slowness.
- Median interval from motor onset to dementia ≈ **10 years**, with wide spread.

### India
- Indian Parkinson's clinics report comparable dementia frequency to international series; the gap is in **recognition**; cognitive screening is rarely part of routine Parkinson's follow-up in busy government OPDs.
- Dopamine agonist and amantadine use, and continued anticholinergic (trihexyphenidyl) prescriptions in the elderly, are the commonest Indian sources of "sudden psychiatric" referrals from Parkinson's clinics.
- Caregivers are typically spouses in their sixties managing both rigidity and evening hallucinations at home, with no day-care support.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

- **Biological:** Lewy body spread from the brainstem into limbic and cortical areas; co-existing Alzheimer's pathology (common in old brains, "mixed" cases behave more amnestic); deep cholinergic loss from nucleus basalis degeneration; noradrenergic and serotonergic depletion adding apathy, depression and sleep problems.
- **Disease markers of risk:** postural-instability-gait-predominant motor picture, early autonomic failure, early hallucinations, RBD, excessive daytime sleepiness, baseline MCI-type deficits.
- **Drugs as causes of *symptoms* (not the dementia):** anticholinergics (trihexyphenidyl, benzhexol), dopamine agonists, amantadine, tolterodine-type bladder drugs, sedatives; each can manufacture a psychiatric picture on top of a vulnerable brain. Always audit the list before blaming the disease.
- **Indian context:** pillbox polypharmacy from multiple prescribing doctors (a neurologist, a physician and a local practitioner each adding to the stack) is the single commonest reversible "dementia-worsener" in Indian Parkinson's follow-up.

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: the protein climbs the brainstem.** The same alpha-synuclein process that started in the movement-control stations does not stay there. Over years it climbs into the memory gate, the perception centres and the frontal manager offices, the cortex. When the cortical load crosses the individual's reserve threshold, thinking begins to fail. The one-year rule is really a rule about *which door the disease entered through*, the motor door first (PDD) or the cortical door first (DLB).

**Story 2: the emptied fuel tank.** Acetylcholine, the chemical of attention and learning, is made by a small basal-forebrain nucleus whose fibres blanket the cortex. In PDD this system fails even more completely than in Alzheimer's. The practical translation: attention flickers, visuospatial processing blurs, and hallucinations surface, and rivastigmine, which preserves remaining acetylcholine, is the one drug that pushes back on all three at once.

**Story 3: the drug tightrope.** The Parkinson's brain is chemically balanced like a see-saw: dopamine on one side (movement), acetylcholine on the other. Every psychiatric drug pushes one side. Old antipsychotics block dopamine: movement collapses. Anti-Parkinson drugs raise dopamine: psychosis blooms. Great PDD care is the art of walking this see-saw: **reduce the dopaminergic load first, restore acetylcholine second, and only then, if needed, touch an atypical antipsychotic with extreme caution.**

## 7. Symptoms

**Cognitive, subcortical-flavoured:**
- Slowing of thought (answers delayed, gear-changes laborious), word-finding difficulty on demand.
- Executive failure: cannot sequence a medication schedule or handle money change; poor set-shifting and dual-tasking (walking while talking deteriorates; itself a falls risk).
- Visuospatial trouble: misjudging steps, difficulty parking or threading the needle of a doorway.
- Memory: retrieval is slow, but storage holds relatively better than in Alzheimer's, patients often recall with cueing.
- Fluctuating attention and daytime sleepiness are common and family-visible.

**Neuropsychiatric:**
- **Visual hallucinations:** classically well-formed people, animals or "someone sitting quietly in the corner"; initially benign with insight, later threatening as insight fades. Frequently *emerge or amplify* after dopamine-agonist or amantadine changes.
- Delusions of infidelity, theft or persecution; less common than in DLB but corrosive when present.
- Depression, anxiety and apathy; apathy is commonly misread as laziness or depression (apathy is flat; depression is painful).
- **Impulse-control disorders from agonists:** pathological gambling, compulsive shopping, binge eating, hypersexuality; families may hide these out of shame; always ask privately and specifically. Sleep attacks and vivid dreams also track agonist dose.

**Somatic-mental overlaps:**
- Falls (postural instability + visuospatial failure), drooling and swallowing difficulty, urinary urgency, constipation, each of these amplifies BPSD when unmanaged.

## 8. Diagnosis

### The logic (paraphrased)
DSM/ICD logic: a major neurocognitive disorder **occurring in the context of established Parkinson's disease**; by the convention, parkinsonism present **more than one year before** cognitive decline; the profile typically shows bradyphrenia, executive and visuospatial deficits, with relatively preserved naming and recall-to-cue. There is no biomarker; it is a clinical-plus-course diagnosis.

### Practical workup
- **Collateral history of timeline**: motor onset vs cognitive onset is the diagnosis's backbone. Digg into old prescriptions to date the levodopa start.
- **Cognitive testing with executive/visuospatial weighting**: clock-drawing, trail-type tasks, fluency under time, dual-task observation; memory-weighted screens under-sell PDD (as they under-sell FTD).
- **Medicine audit line by line**, anticholinergics, bladder drugs, sedatives, agonists, amantadine: which of these is treatable today?
- Rule-out bloods as in any dementia (thyroid, B12; see the Alzheimer's note; one fact, one home).
- MRI where affordable/available: mainly to exclude vascular burden, hydrocephalus or a second process.
- Check: hearing, vision, pain, sleep, constipation, the five hidden cognitive-loaders in Parkinson's.

### Differentiation snapshot
| Against | Deciding clues |
|---------|----------------|
| DLB | Motor-first by >1 year = PDD; dementia-first/within-1-year = DLB |
| Alzheimer's | Amnestic-first, smooth, no parkinsonism beyond drug effects |
| Vascular dementia | Stepwise, infarct burden, no response to cholinesterase inhibitors |
| Drug-induced psychosis | Timeline follows a prescription change; improves with drug-list surgery |
| Lewy body "misperception" of medication side effects | Ask "when did the visions begin relative to the last medicine change?" |

## 9. Management

### (a) Drug-list surgery: always the first prescription
- **Stop anticholinergics** (trihexyphenidyl first among equals in elderly Indians); swap bladder anticholinergics for timed toileting or alternatives; review sedatives; **prune the dopamine agonist** (with the neurologist), psychosis and impulse-control disorders often recede with dose reduction, accepting a little more motor stiffness as the price of a sane evening.
- Keep **levodopa as the backbone** (least psychotogenic of the anti-parkinson drugs); timing and smoothness of dose often matter more than quantity.

### (b) Cognition and hallucinations: the acetylcholine lever
- **Rivastigmine** (oral or patch) has trial evidence in PDD for cognition, activities of daily living and psychotic features; donepezil is the practical alternative. Same dosing logic as the Alzheimer's note.
- **Memantine**: modest add-on, reasonable tolerability, helps a subset.
- Expectation-setting: function steadies, months are bought; the underlying disease still progresses.

*India note: generic rivastigmine capsules are affordable (approx ₹200–450/month, 2026); the patch is not. In government settings where even this is hard, the free levers (drug-list surgery, hearing/vision, sleep and constipation management) do most of the day-to-day work.*

### (c) Psychosis beyond the above
- If hallucinations are non-frightening and insight persists: **reassure, do not medicate**; even lighting at dusk; explain "the visions are chemistry, not ghosts or madness".
- When danger or distress persists after the above steps: **quetiapine** 12.5–25 mg titrated cautiously is the pragmatic Indian default (weak evidence but best availability/risk balance); **clozapine** has the best efficacy evidence (including in Parkinson's psychosis specifically) and paradoxically *improves* tremor, but needs weekly-then-periodic blood counts for agranulocytosis, tertiary-centre use. **Pimavanserin**, the US-approved specific for Parkinson's psychosis, is not marketed in India; know the name for exams.
- **Never haloperidol, risperidone or olanzapine as planned therapy**, rigidity, swallowing failure, malignant-type reactions; the wallet card rule from the DLB note applies identically here.

### (d) The body matters to the mind
- Physiotherapy for gait and balance; falls-proof the home; swallow surveillance (video-swallow where available; posture, texture modification, hand-feeding techniques: see the Alzheimer's note's late-stage discussion).
- Constipation and urinary urgency managed systematically (impaction and nocturia are top BPSD triggers).
- Depression: SSRIs with minimal anticholinergic load (sertraline, escitalopram); avoid tricyclics entirely in PDD.

## 10. Indian practice

- **Where care lives:** neurology OPDs own the Parkinson's; psychiatry is consulted when "visions" or "behaviour" arrive; usually after someone has already prescribed risperidone. The consultation's most valuable act is often undoing a prescription.
- **The medicine stack problem:** patients often see a neurologist, a general physician and a local practitioner, each adding tablets; nobody owns the whole list. One clinic habit fixes it: ask the family to bring *every* tablet and tonic in one bag, and rebuild the chart from the physical evidence.
- **Costs:** levodopa and generic rivastigmine/donepezil are cheap; quetiapine is cheap; clozapine is cheap in itself but its monitoring costs the follow-up discipline; the true costs are the caregiver's, and the falls/fractures.
- **Faith explanations:** hallucinations of deceased relatives are commonly interpreted spiritually. Respond by joining the family's frame respectfully ("what you saw matters; it is also a signal of brain chemistry and we can make it calmer") rather than fighting it.
- **Primary care role:** the annual cognitive check at every Parkinson's follow-up (clock-drawing takes two minutes), drug-list vigilance, and knowing the antipsychotic rule, the three cheapest lifesavers in Indian PDD care.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: the visions that followed the new tablet.** A 74-year-old with 9 years of Parkinson's disease was referred for "psychosis": three weeks of seeing a silent crowd at the far end of the room each evening. Timeline: amantadine had been added two months earlier, and trihexyphenidyl had continued for years. Amantadine was stopped and the anticholinergic tapered with the neurologist; no antipsychotic was given. The crowd thinned over two weeks and vanished by the fifth; motor function held with small levodopa retiming. Rivastigmine was started later for declining planning ability.

*Teaching points: ask the prescription timeline before the symptom timeline; drug-list surgery replaced an antipsychotic; the "visions" were chemistry, and the chemistry was ours.*

**Case 2: the gambling the family hid.** A 61-year-old on pramipexole for five years was brought for "confusion", but the private truth elicited from his son was ₹9 lakh of online betting losses in a year, with nightly "visions of children" as the visible tip. Agonist reduction (accepting slightly worse gait), rivastigmine initiation, gambler-debt counselling and UPI/wallet locks together restored household finances and calm evenings.

*Teaching points: impulse-control disorders hide behind shame, ask privately and specifically ("betting, shopping, eating, sex, any of these stronger than before?"); one drug caused both the behaviour and the visions; and the treatment included a banker's tool as much as a prescription.*

## 13. FAQ

**"Is this the same as Alzheimer's?"**
No. Both are dementias, but this one runs with the Parkinson's illness, attacks planning, speed and visual processing first, and preserves memory storage longer. The medicines that help are also different.

**"He sees his late brother sitting by the window every evening. Should we be frightened?"**
If he is calm about it and knows it is a vision, no. This is extremely common in this illness and often needs no medicine, but it does need a medicine review, because sometimes a tablet is inviting the visitor.

**"The doctor stopped one of his Parkinson's tablets and the visions went. Why?"**
Because some Parkinson's medicines push the chemistry that produces visions. Reducing them is often the safest psychosis treatment in this disease; a little more stiffness is a fair price for a calm evening.

**"He has become lazy and refuses to help at home."**
That is probably apathy, not laziness; a flat loss of drive that is part of the brain disease. Schedules, routine and one-step requests work better than scolding; and it is not depression unless sadness is present.

**"Is there a memory tablet for this?"**
Yes: the rivastigmine family, and it specifically helps thinking, attention and even the visions in this disease. It does not stop the underlying illness, but it buys real function.

**"He fell again last night."**
Falls are the emergency of this disease. Ask for a home falls audit (rails, night lighting, footwear), a lying-standing blood-pressure check, and a physiotherapy balance review; each fall risks a hip fracture that ends walking forever.

**"Are we allowed to give sedatives at night so we can sleep?"**
Carefully, and only with the treating doctor: sedatives can worsen balance, memory and confusion overnight. Better first moves: daytime activity, evening light-dimming, and treating any night-time pain or full bladder.

**"Should we hide the betting debts from the doctor?"**
Please do not. Compulsive gambling or shopping can be *caused by a Parkinson's medicine* and often improves when that medicine is reduced; hiding it keeps the cause in place.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **One-year rule**: the PDD vs DLB hinge; quote it in every answer involving either.
- **Highest-yield fact:** rivastigmine has evidence in PDD (cognition + psychosis), while dopamine-blocking antipsychotics risk motor catastrophe, the same tightrope as DLB.
- **Classic traps:** (1) prescribing risperidone to a Parkinson's patient with hallucinations in casualty; (2) blaming dementia for what a dopamine agonist manufactured; (3) forgetting anticholinergic burden (trihexyphenidyl still common in Indian prescriptions); (4) missing impulse-control disorder behind "family dispute"; (5) treating apathy with antidepressants by reflex.
- **Viva favourites:** subcortical cognitive profile; why clozapine is uniquely suited among antipsychotics (evidence + actual tremor benefit + no D2 blockade profile to speak of); the see-saw chemistry explanation; RBD as shared marker; cumulative dementia incidence in long-surviving Parkinson's.
- **Indian exam corner:** drug-bag reconciliation as polypharmacy management; the spiritual-framing consultation skill; costs of generic anti-Parkinson/dementia drugs; neurology-psychiatry co-management as a short note.
- **Rehearse one line:** "In Parkinson's psychosis, first take away what the prescriptions added, then give what the brain lacks."

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. Dementia appearing 5 years after parkinsonism is labelled:**
A. DLB
B. Parkinson's disease dementia
C. Alzheimer's with parkinsonism
D. Vascular dementia

**Answer: B.** The one-year rule: motor disease established more than a year before dementia = PDD.

**2. The most evidence-backed drug for cognition and psychosis in PDD is:**
A. Haloperidol
B. Rivastigmine
C. Risperidone
D. Amitriptyline

**Answer: B.** The deep cholinergic deficit is the therapeutic target; rivastigmine has trial evidence for cognition, function and psychotic features in PDD.

**3. A Parkinson's patient develops gambling and evening visual hallucinations. The first suspect is:**
A. Rivastigmine
B. A dopamine agonist
C. Memantine
D. Donepezil

**Answer: B.** Agonists (pramipexole/ropinirole) cause impulse-control disorders and psychotic phenomena; dose reduction is the treatment.

**4. Which must be on the forbidden list for PDD psychosis?**
A. Quetiapine
B. Clozapine
C. Haloperidol
D. Melatonin

**Answer: C.** D2 blockade catastrophically worsens rigidity and swallowing; quetiapine and clozapine are the cautious options.

**5. The typical PDD cognitive profile is:**
A. Amnestic-first
B. Executive and visuospatial slowing first, memory relatively preserved
C. Pure aphasia
D. Pure amnesia with fluent speech

**Answer: B.** Subcortical-frontal and visuospatial failure dominates; recall improves with cueing, unlike early Alzheimer's.

**6. The cheapest first intervention for new hallucinations in a Parkinson's patient on multiple tablets is:**
A. Add quetiapine
B. Stop anticholinergics / prune the dopamine load
C. Start an SSRI
D. Order a PET scan

**Answer: B.** Drug-list surgery treats the commonest reversible cause before any new prescription.
