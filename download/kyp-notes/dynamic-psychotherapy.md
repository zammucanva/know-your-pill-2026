# Psychoanalysis & Long-Term Dynamic Psychotherapy

## 1. In one line + overview

**Dynamic therapy is the treatment built on one observation: the patterns that hurt a person's life (the partners they keep choosing, the anger they cannot feel, the closeness they flee) were learned so early and so deeply that they now run automatically, outside awareness; the therapy makes these patterns visible in a live relationship and reworks them, slowly, with a trained guide.**

Every school of psychotherapy descends, directly or in reaction, from psychoanalysis. Whatever one thinks of the couch, the founding ideas are now part of everyday clinical medicine: that the mind has automatic habits we do not notice (the unconscious, reframed); that painful feelings get converted into symptoms, slips and defences; that every patient brings their earliest relationships into every new one, including the one with their doctor (transference); and that a relationship built on reliability can itself correct old learning. This note explains the core ideas in plain language, what long-term and brief dynamic therapy actually do in the room, what the evidence honestly supports, and what any of this has to do with Indian practice, where trained dynamic therapists are scarce, where "psychological-mindedness" must be assessed without jargon, and where a retired schoolteacher in Kolkata and a software engineer in Bengaluru will meet these ideas in very different clinics.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Explain the unconscious in its modern, plain form: not a hidden basement but automatic relational habits (procedures learned before words)
- Name and recognise the common defence mechanisms in everyday clinical encounters, and use them as diagnostic information rather than trivia
- Define transference and countertransference, and spot both in a five-minute outpatient encounter
- Describe the structure of psychodynamic work: the frame, free association, the here-and-now pattern, interpretation, working through
- Draw Malan's two triangles (conflict and person) and explain how brief dynamic therapy compresses the work
- State the honest evidence position: effects for dynamic therapy in common disorders and personality pathology, equivalent to other bona fide therapies for many, with a particular role for complex, chronic, relational presentations
- Place dynamic therapy in India: the therapy-desert reality, the Indian psychoanalytic tradition (one of the oldest outside Europe), training routes, fees, and the referral question every psychiatrist faces

## 4. The core ideas

### Idea 1, The unconscious, updated: not a basement but an autopilot

The cartoon version of the unconscious is a locked basement full of forbidden wishes. The version that survives modern neuroscience is humbler and more useful: most of what the mind does never reaches words. A child learns how relationships work (how closeness feels, what happens after anger, whether needs get met) years before language is available to record it. This early knowledge is stored as *procedures*, the way bicycle-riding is stored: the body and the emotions know how to do it, and there is no sentence anywhere that says it.

The clinical consequence: a person can sincerely pledge to change a pattern ("I will never again fall for someone who needs saving") and then produce the identical pattern again, because the pledge lives in the verbal system while the pattern lives in the procedural one. Insight delivered as advice speaks to the wrong system. Dynamic therapy's wager is that procedural learning can only be re-learned procedurally, inside a live relationship, over time, with the old pattern activated and answered differently. That is why it is slow, and that is also why it is not replaceable by a lecture.

### Idea 2, Defence mechanisms: the mind's immune system

When a feeling is unbearable (rage at a dying parent, envy of a sibling, dependence the world will not allow) the mind does not merely "not think about it". It converts it. The conversions are the defences, and they are as individual as fingerprints. The mature clinician reads them the way a physician reads a fever pattern: not as oddities but as data about the underlying condition.

In plain clinical terms, the everyday set looks like this:

- **Repression**: the feeling is shelved, and its existence is denied: "I wasn't angry, just tired."
- **Denial**, the external fact is refused: the man with a recent infarct booking a trek.
- **Projection**: the feeling is relocated into someone else: "You're the one who's angry with me."
- **Projective identification**: the feeling is induced in the other until they carry it: the patient leaves each nurse quietly furious, and cannot say why.
- **Splitting**: people are all-good or all-bad with no middle: the idealised doctor of Tuesday is the villain of Thursday.
- **Displacement**, the emotion lands on a safer target: the son shouted at, the saucepan thrown at the maid.
- **Reaction formation**: the unacceptable feeling is over-reversed: excessive sweetness where hatred sits; scrupulous care where resentment lives.
- **Somatisation**: the conflict is transcribed into the body: the "gas" that rises with every mention of the arranged marriage.
- **Intellectualisation**, feelings are fled into vocabulary: the relative who asks about transmitter systems at his father's bedside.
- **Rationalisation**: a dignified motive is invented for a plain one.
- **Acting out**: the feeling is discharged in behaviour instead of words: the self-harm after the breakup, the drink after the insult.
- **Altruism, sublimation, humour**, the mature conversions: the feeling is spent in forms that build rather than destroy.

Two clinical disciplines follow. First, defences are on a ladder from primitive (splitting, projective identification, typical of borderline organisation) to mature (humour, sublimation); the rung a patient habitually uses is diagnostic of personality organisation, not a moral grade. Second, defences only change when the underlying feeling becomes speakable; attacking the defence head-on ("stop intellectualising!") merely drives the pattern underground. Respect the defence (it was the best solution available at the time it was built) and aim for the feeling underneath.

### Idea 3, Transference and countertransference: the patient's history, live in your room

The most exportable psychoanalytic discovery is this: patients do not meet doctors as neutral new figures. They meet us *through* their template. The deferential patient who cannot say the medicine caused diarrhoea, the hostile patient who "knew you would dismiss me like everyone else", the one who brings sweets and needs you to be delighted; each is running an old programme on new hardware. That is transference: the displacement of early relational patterns onto the present figure.

Three practical uses, none requiring a couch:

1. **Diagnosis**: the feeling a patient reliably produces in staff is often a sample of what every relationship in their life produces. The ward that dreads one patient is holding information.
2. **Prognostic leverage**: the transference shows you precisely which relationship pattern the therapy, if it happens, will be reworking. The man who tests every caregiver until they abandon him will test you, and the first time the test is answered with neither retaliation nor flight, treatment has begun.
3. **The doctor's side**: countertransference. The clinician's own feelings toward the patient (rescue-longing, dread, contempt, sleepiness) are not noise; they are often induced by the patient's procedures, and therefore data. The discipline is to feel them, own them, and use them, not to act them out (the rescue-prescription, the cold discharge) nor to pretend they are absent. In a country where consultation minutes are few, countertransference is the one instrument that never runs out of batteries; it works in a three-minute OPD interaction as well as a fifty-minute hour.

### Idea 4, What the therapy actually is: frame, association, pattern, interpretation, working through

Strip away the iconography and a dynamic session has a plain architecture:

- **The frame**: same time, same room, same duration, cancellations spoken about, not bureaucracy, but the experimental constant against which the patient's automatic pattern will show itself, including in how the frame itself is treated (the late-comer, the gift-bringer, the bill-ignorer).
- **Free association, in practice**: the invitation to say whatever comes, uncensored, impossible to achieve perfectly, and the *resistances* to it (the sudden blankness, the topic-swerve, the arrival of the prepared speech) are themselves the material.
- **The here-and-now pattern**: as the weeks pass, the patient's core pattern reliably appears *toward the therapist*; the need to please, the fear of being a burden, the waiting for the trap. The therapy deliberately works on this live sample rather than only on second-hand history.
- **Interpretation**: the offered link that names the pattern aloud; tentatively, at the moment it is running ("I notice each time you start to feel something here, you make a joke and turn to my pen. The joking arrived in your life around the same years as..."). A good interpretation connects feeling, defence and history: Malan's triangles in one sentence.
- **Working through**: the unglamorous truth that one correct interpretation changes almost nothing, and the pattern must be met, named and answered dozens of times in dozens of moods before the procedural learning actually updates. This is the honest reason dynamic therapy has a *duration*, not a course length.

### Idea 5, The brief versions: focus instead of open-endedness

Classical psychoanalysis (three-to-five sessions a week, years, often the couch) is now a small, specialised corner. The clinically important descendants are **short-term dynamic therapies** (typically 16–40 weekly sessions) built on Malan's compression logic: agree early on ONE core conflict pattern, keep every session tied to it, work actively with the transference as it arises, and accept partial rather than total character change. Davanloo's intensive format and, in India's neighbourhood of relevance, the time-limited models used in public services descend from this compression. Selection for brief work matters: adequate ego strength, ability to form a relationship, a circumscribed conflict, not the multiple comorbid, fragile-organisation patient, who does better with longer, more supportive pacing.

The modern offshoots most clinicians will actually meet: **mentalization-based treatment** (Bateman and Fonagy) for borderline personality, teaching the skill of holding minds in mind, with a sturdy frame and a team behind it; **transference-focused psychotherapy** (Kernberg's line) for borderline and narcissistic organisation; and **dynamic-interpersonal therapy**, a 16-session protocol used in Britain's national service for depression and increasingly familiar in exam syllabi. Each keeps the engine (pattern, relationship, defence, the past in the present) with a manual gearbox.

### Idea 6: The evidence, stated honestly

The caricature, "psychoanalysis is untested mysticism", deserves a careful answer rather than a cheer. Fair tests of long therapies are hard: sessions differ, therapists differ, blinding is impossible, and the outcomes that matter most (relational life, character) are measured worst. What the evidence does support:

- For common disorders (depression, anxiety, somatic symptom presentations), bona fide short-term dynamic therapy performs roughly on par with CBT in meta-analyses, effect sizes in the same band, no consistent winner across trials, with some evidence of gains continuing after therapy ends (the "sleeper effect" reported in several meta-analytic discussions).
- For personality pathology, structured dynamic treatments (MBT, TFP) have randomised-trial support for reducing self-harm, hospitalisation and symptom burden in borderline personality, standing alongside DBT in guidelines.
- Long-term dynamic therapy for complex, comorbid, chronic presentations shows benefit in observational and some randomised follow-ups (the large LPP longitudinal cohort in Norway being the best-known), but with honest caveats about control conditions.
- Classical psychoanalysis proper remains largely evidence-by-case-series and process research, say so plainly to students, and note that exam answers crediting "insight" as the sole mechanism will earn half marks at best: the relationship is now understood as a co-equal engine.

The honest bottom line for a clinician: dynamic therapy is one well-supported general-purpose instrument, uniquely suited to presentations that are *about relationships themselves* (repeated destructive patterns, chronic emptiness, personality-level problems) where symptom-protocol therapies thin out. It is not the default for circumscribed phobias or first-episode panic, where CBT's protocols are faster and better targeted.

## 5. Why it matters clinically

- **You already do psychodynamics, or you do it badly**: the ten-minute OPD encounter contains transference (the patient who cannot disagree with you about a side-effect), defences (the somatic screen), and countertransference (your irritation with the "heart sink" list). The concepts are clinical optics, not a referral form.
- **The defence profile is diagnostic**: recognising splitting and projective identification at the first consultation changes your next prescription, your documentation, and your expectation of the medication follow-up; you will plan for the idealisation-to-devaluation cycle instead of being ambushed by it (see the Personality Disorders notes for the management frame).
- **Medicine's own defence mechanisms waste money**: the workup that will not stop on the "gas and burning" patient is intellectualisation purchased at radiology rates. Reading the defence correctly is the most cost-effective investigation in the room.
- **Psychological-mindedness is assessable in one question**: not "do you have insight" but "when things go wrong between you and someone you love, what is your part in it, usually?" A patient who can sketch their own recurring pattern at first meeting will make use of any therapy, dynamic or otherwise. One who answers only in terms of others' faults needs psychoeducation and structure before interpretation.
- **Referral discipline**: dynamic therapy earns its place for the relational-repeat presentations, chronicity after adequate protocol therapy, and personality-level difficulties, not as a default, not as a punishment ("non-compliant, refer for psychotherapy"), and not as a substitute for treating the episode in front of you.
- **The rupture is the treatment moment**: in any therapy, the patient will eventually reproduce the core pattern on the therapist, miss sessions, test the boundary, provoke the abandonment. Ordinary clinical reflex treats rupture as failure; the dynamic frame treats it as the most valuable session of the year, provided it is repaired rather than retaliated against. This principle exports to every doctor-patient relationship in the building.

## 6. India lens

- **The desert, described plainly**: trained dynamic psychotherapists in India number in the low hundreds against a population whose need is measured in tens of millions. They are concentrated in the metros (Mumbai, Delhi, Bengaluru, Kolkata, Chennai, Pune), in private practice, in a handful of NGO and university clinics. The average Indian patient's realistic psychological treatment will be brief, eclectic, counselling-flavoured, or delivered by a psychiatrist's ten minutes plus medication. Pretending otherwise in a lecture is dishonest; the referral question is therefore triage, not preference.
- **The tradition nobody taught you**: psychoanalysis in India is older than the republic's psychiatry infrastructure. Girindrasekhar Bose of Calcutta corresponded with Freud from the 1920s, founded the Indian Psychoanalytic Society (1922, the first psychoanalytic body outside Europe and North America), and famously dissented on the Oedipus complex, arguing from the Indian joint-family's dense, multi-caregiver structure that the child's drama is less a single triangle than a crowded stage, with dependence threaded differently through the life-cycle. That dissent, whatever one makes of it, is the first serious non-Western theoretical correction to Freud, and Indian candidates answer exams better for knowing it exists.
- **The cultural fit question, answered without romance**: classic technique assumes an individual who is the unit of change; Indian selves are more family-embedded, and secrets are family property. The practical adjustments are real, not theoretical: the family commonly attends, funds and monitors the therapy; confidentiality needs explicit contracting ("what, exactly, may I tell your husband?"); and the therapist will be asked, legitimately, for guidance on marriage, duty and parents. A rigidly imported technique that pathologises the family-Self misreads the patient; a spineless one that reports everything to the uncle is not therapy. The skilful Indian dynamic therapist works the *seam* between individual wish and family claim; often the exact conflict the patient is producing symptoms from.
- **Money and minutes**: metro private dynamic therapy runs roughly ₹800–3,000 per session, more in premium practices (approx 2026; NGO and institute clinics slide from ₹100–500; a few training-clinic seats are free with trainees supervised). Long-term work at weekly frequency is therefore a middle-and-upper-class purchase, and class bias in access is a fact the profession should say aloud. The honest tiering for most patients: psychoeducation and brief focal work through government and NGO channels (see the Primary Care Psychiatry note), protocol therapies where indicated, and dynamic referral for the minority who both need and can use it.
- **Training routes, for the interested**: the Indian Psychoanalytic Society (Kolkata) and a small number of affiliated bodies offer formal analytic training (personal analysis, supervised cases, curriculum, years, and now also online-affiliated); university routes run through applied-psychology and clinical-psychology programmes (NIMHANS, CIP Ranchi, Ambedkar University Delhi, TISS Mumbai and others) where psychodynamic modules sit inside broader psychotherapy training; and short courses in dynamic-interpersonal and brief work are increasingly available through private institutes. A caution for the buyer: "psychoanalytically informed" on a visiting-card covers everything from a supervised training to a weekend workshop; ask the therapist what supervision they are currently in.
- **Where you will actually meet the concepts**: Indian postgraduate exams love defence mechanisms, transference and the triangles; viva examiners probe the difference between obsession (a symptom) and the obsessive personality (a structure), a distinction the dynamic tradition built. In service terms, the concepts quietly run the better family-intervention programmes (the schizophrenia psychoeducation literature is downstream of systems and dynamic thinking), the AA-style cultures of disclosure, and the reflective practice now entering supervision in clinical-psychology programmes. The country that cannot afford the couch is still running on its engineering.

## 7. Active recall + FAQ

**Recall prompts:**
- State the procedural-learning version of the unconscious in four sentences, and explain why advice cannot retrain it.
- Give the impulse-anxiety-defence chain for one of your own recent consultations, in the patient's words.
- Which two primitive defences announce borderline-level organisation at the first meeting, and what do they look like on a ward round?
- Name the three vertices of Malan's triangle of person, and link them to one sentence of an interpretation you could actually say.
- What is a "rupture", and why do dynamic therapists value it rather than dread it?
- State the honest evidence position for short-term dynamic therapy versus CBT in depression, in two sentences.
- Give the Bose-Freud disagreement in three lines, and the Indian clinical implication you draw from it.

**FAQs (in patients' and families' words):**
- *"Will I have to lie on a couch and talk about my childhood for ten years?"*: Almost never in India today. Most dynamic therapy is face-to-face, seated, weekly, and often time-limited (roughly four months to a year). The couch belongs to classical analysis, a rare speciality.
- *"Is it not just expensive talking? The medicine does the real work."*: For a clear depressive episode, the medicine plus your own resources may indeed carry it. For repeated patterns (the same marriage twice, the same job crisis, the same emptiness) no tablet yet made teaches a new way of relating. That is the work this therapy does.
- *"My family wants to know what I say in sessions."*: They will be told, with your written consent, only what you agree to share, and usually a summary serves everyone. The rule is contracted at the start; family involvement can be high without breaking confidentiality; decide together what goes out.
- *"How do I know my therapist is properly trained?"*: Ask two questions: what is your training in this specific method, and are you currently in supervision? A confident answer to both is a good sign anywhere, and the habit protects you in every kind of therapy, not just this one.
- *"Is it suitable for my schizophrenia? For my son's autism?"*: Not as the primary treatment: psychosis needs medication-based care and family intervention, autism needs structured support. Dynamic ideas may still guide the clinician's understanding, but that is different from prescribing the therapy.
- *"Will the therapist just blame my mother?"*: A bad one might; a good one treats defences and parents' solutions with respect; they were the best available then. The aim is understanding the pattern, not convicting its origin.
- *"Can it be done online?"*: Since the pandemic, much of it is, and it works acceptably for many; the frame is just contracted differently (same time, private room, headphones). Severe instability is better seen in person.
- *"Doctor, you are my only counsellor, is that a problem?"*: For brief focal work, no. For long-term therapy with a dependent, fragile patient, one clinician doing both prescribing and open-ended therapy loses the outside eye; the ideal is a team, and in most of India the realistic compromise is honest supervision, whoever provides it.

## 8. Evidence sources + self-test

1. Freud S: foundational papers on repression, the clinical theory and technique (1900s–1920s); read today as the source of concepts, not as current evidence.
2. Bose G: early Indian psychoanalytic correspondence and dissent on the Oedipal triangle (1920s–30s); Indian Psychoanalytic Society records.
3. Malan D: *Toward the Validation of Dynamic Psychotherapy* (1973) and the brief-therapy texts: the two triangles, focus, follow-up outcomes.
4. Davanloo H: intensive short-term dynamic psychotherapy (ISTDP) literature from the 1970s–80s onward.
5. Leichsenring F, Abbass A et al.: Cochrane and journal meta-analyses of short-term psychodynamic therapy for common mental disorders (2010s; equivalence-band findings with CBT).
6. Shedler J, "The efficacy of psychodynamic psychotherapy" (*American Psychologist*, 2010), the widely cited synthesis, read with its critics.
7. Bateman A, Fonagy P: randomised trials and texts on mentalization-based treatment for borderline personality (2000s–2020s).
8. Kernberg O, Clarkin J, Yeomans F: transference-focused psychotherapy for borderline organisation.
9. Leichsenring & Rabung (2008) and later updates, the long-term psychotherapy meta-analyses and the debate they triggered (read as contested, not settled).
10. Ulvenes P et al. / the Oslo LPP cohort (Høglend and colleagues), longitudinal randomised and naturalistic follow-up of long-term dynamic therapy.
11. Fonagy P (ed.): *Affect Regulation and the Development of the Self* / contemporary relational-developmental synthesis (with allied infant-research literature).
12. NHRC–NIMHANS and NMHS 2015–16 materials for the Indian treatment-gap context; DCP–Guilt ("Decriminalisation of Attempted Suicide" coalition reports) where relational burden in Indian families is documented; Ambedkar University / IAPS; Indian journal sources on therapy access and fees, context tier.
13. Hollon M et al. mentalization / briefly: BATMAN-Fonagy MBT RCT for BPD in general psychiatry settings, contemporary generalisability line.

**Self-test MCQs:**

**1. A patient with depression begins every follow-up by praising you extravagantly, then reports the medicine is useless and you are the only one who understands her. The dynamic reading most useful to the *prescriber*:**
A. Flattery: request a different doctor
B. Splitting with idealisation: expect the devaluation cycle, keep documentation steady, and consider personality-level work in the plan
C. Hypomania: reduce the antidepressant
D. Poor insight: refer for ECT
**Answer: B.** The all-good doctor is the same machinery as the future all-bad doctor; the prescriber who plans for the swing keeps the alliance and the chart intact.

**2. The modern, plain-language definition of the unconscious best suited to clinical teaching:**
A. A basement of forbidden sexual wishes
B. Dream-content only
C. Early relational learning stored procedurally: automatic pattern-knowledge that runs without words
D. Everything the patient lies about
**Answer: C.** The procedural version explains why advice alone fails to change patterned behaviour, and why live relational learning is the corrective.

**3. Impulse → anxiety → defence. The patient who responds to his wife's illness by researching journal articles on her tumour markers day and night is using:**
A. Displacement onto doctors
B. Intellectualisation, defending against grief and helplessness
C. Projection onto the wife
D. Acting out
**Answer: B.** The vocabulary stands where the feeling should be; the clinical move is to make the feeling speakable, not to confiscate the reading.

**4. Short-term dynamic therapy versus CBT for uncomplicated major depression, the honest evidence statement:**
A. Dynamic therapy clearly superior
B. CBT clearly superior
C. Broadly comparable outcomes in meta-analyses, with CBT more protocolised and faster to deploy; dynamic gains reported to continue post-therapy in some syntheses
D. Neither beats placebo
**Answer: C.** Equivalence-band findings; the choice turns on pattern-depth, patient preference and availability, not on a league table.

**5. The "rupture" in dynamic therapy refers to:**
A. The termination date arriving
B. A break in the frame (missed sessions, boundary-testing, sudden hostility) reproducing the core pattern in the therapy relationship
C. A psychotic relapse
D. A change of therapist
**Answer: B.** Handled with repair rather than retaliation, the rupture becomes the highest-yield material of the treatment.

**6. Bose's historical dissent from Freud, and its practical Indian echo:**
A. Denied the unconscious exists; hence Indian therapy should be advice-based
B. Argued from the joint-family stage that the child's relational world is multi-caregiver, with dependence threaded differently, echoed today in contracting family involvement rather than pathologising it
C. Proved Oedipal theory correct in Bengal
D. Founded behaviour therapy
**Answer: B.** The first non-Western structural critique of the triangle, and a live lesson: the family-Self is the clinical material, not an obstacle to it.
