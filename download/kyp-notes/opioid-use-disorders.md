# Opioid use disorders

## 1. In one line + overview

**Tagline:** Opioid dependence is the addiction with the **highest overdose death risk and the best evidence-based medicines**, methadone and buprenorphine maintenance genuinely save lives and rebuild them, overdose is reversible with naloxone in minutes, and the treatment philosophy has moved decisively from "detox and pray" to **long-term stabilisation on a medicine, like any chronic disease.**

**Overview, in plain words:** India sits between two opioid stories: the traditional heroin routes through the north-western states, and the newer wave of **pharmaceutical opioids (tramadol, codeine syrups, injection pentazocine-type drugs**) leaking from pharmacies. Dependence develops fast because opioids pay the brain in its deepest currencies at once: the warm contentment of the endorphin system and the "safety" signal that silences distress. Within weeks to months, the brain re-wires: without the drug, everything feels like illness (body aches, diarrhoea, gooseflesh, yawning, restlessness) the withdrawal that is miserable rather than dangerous, which is precisely why **relapse and overdose, not withdrawal itself, kill people**. Three deaths by overdose happen quietly across the world every minute of every year, and **naloxone (a few hundred rupees, safe, impossible to misuse) reverses them if someone nearby knows**. Treatment's backbone: **buprenorphine** (usually with naloxone combined) or methadone as daily stabilisation, tapered only when life is rebuilt; psychosocial recovery and treatment of the pain or trauma underneath complete the picture. The Indian family needs one sentence from us early: *"This is a medical illness of the brain's calming system; the medicines for it are real; and the first rule at home is never use alone."*

## 2. Learning objectives

1. Describe the opioid withdrawal syndrome and its timeline (short-acting vs long-acting agents) with its "miserable, not lethal" framing.
2. Recognise overdose on sight and act: naloxone, recovery position, airway, transfer.
3. Prescribe opioid agonist treatment (OAT): buprenorphine-naloxone induction, stabilisation, maintenance logic; methadone where available.
4. Run symptomatic detoxification for those refusing OAT, and counsel honestly about relapse risk.
5. Use naltrexone (oral/long-acting) for the highly motivated, with its timing rules.
6. Screen and manage the medical riders: HIV, hepatitis B/C, TB, injection-site infections, endocarditis.
7. Map the Indian opioid landscape: north-west belt, pharmacy leakage of tramadol/codeine/pentazocine, and the NDPS Act's treatment-vs-possession realities.
8. Deliver the family overdose kit teaching and the never-use-alone rule.

## 4. Epidemiology

### Global
- Around **60 million people** use opioids worldwide; about **two-thirds of drug-related deaths** are opioid-attributable; overdose deaths in North America drove the fentanyl era, the cautionary tale for every pharmacy-leaky country.
- Opioid agonist treatment (methadone/buprenorphine) remains the single most evidence-based treatment in all of addiction medicine, retaining people in care, reducing illicit use, HIV transmission, crime and death.

### India
- The national survey lineage: about **2–2.1% of adults** use opioids; the **north-western belt (Punjab, Haryana, Himachal, Delhi-adjacent, Rajasthan in parts)** carries the country's highest rates; heroin and pharmaceutical opioids (tramadol, propoxyphene historically, codeine-containing cough syrups) dominate, with injection use significant in pockets.
- The pharmacy leakage problem is structural: tramadol and codeine are cheap, and prescription oversight is thin, making the Indian wave "pharmaceutical" more than "fentanyl" so far.
- Treatment access: NDDTC/AIIMS-led centres, government de-addiction centres, some NGO/faith-based programmes; opioid agonist availability is expanding but still metro-concentrated; harm-reduction (needle-syringe programmes, naloxone) is available in some states through NGO networks.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

- **Biology:** mu-receptor pharmacology that silences both distress and cough (the cough-syrup link); rapid tolerance development; chronic pain patients escalated by inadequate pain relief, a genuine pathway in India's pain-desert.
- **Psychological:** relief-function powerful, trauma, grief and untreated depression travel under opioid use; the warm "everything is fine" state anesthetises shame and hypervigilance.
- **Social:** the migrant-labour and unemployment economies; peer-network initiation; pain-medication leftovers in households; pharmacy access without prescription enforcement.
- **Indian structural:** the north-west heroin geography; high-unemployment youth pockets; codeine-syrup culture; and the historical under-treatment of pain that leaves patients self-escalating.
- **Course:** transition from swallowing/smoking to injecting as money tightens; the event to prevent with early agonist treatment.

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: the volume knob on distress.** Deep in the brain stem sits the noradrenergic alarm bell (locus coeruleus) that rings out all distress. Opioids turn its volume to zero: that is the calm. With weeks of silence, the bell's amplifier grows (receptor up-regulation) to be heard at all. Stop the drug, and the alarm rings at full volume with no drug to mute it: the yawning, sweating, diarrhoea, gooseflesh, aches, every cell's alarm at once. This is withdrawal: recovery of a volume system, not punishment.

**Story 2: the self-forgiving disaster (overdose).** Opioids also silence the brain-stem's breathing clock. When someone with deep tolerance stops for a week (detox, jail, hospital), the tolerance fades; the old dose then silences the breathing clock beyond recovery, especially with a benzodiazepine or alcohol on board. The person is not "poisoned by a bad batch"; he is **sedating beyond a tolerance he no longer has**. Naloxone throws the drug off the breathing clock for 30–90 minutes, and someone must call for help, because naloxone is shorter-acting than most opioids.

**Story 3: the medicine that holds the door.** Buprenorphine grips the mu receptor tightly but only *partially* activates it; enough to end withdrawal and craving, not enough to sedate dangerously, with a ceiling on respiratory depression. Because it grips so tightly, it also blocks street heroin's effect, so the patient on stable buprenorphine feels no high from a relapse, and the relapse loses its point. That is maintenance: **a single daily medicine holding the door the illness keeps knocking on.**

## 7. Symptoms

**Intoxication:** pinpoint pupils, drowsy contentment, slurred speech, slowed breathing (danger sign at <10/min or blue lips), itching, nausea.

**Withdrawal, the "terrible flu" (inexact but fair):** craving and anxiety first (6–12 h for short-acting), then the full set, muscle aches, yawning, tearing, running nose, sweating, gooseflesh (the "cold turkey" skin), diarrhoea and cramps, vomiting, dilated pupils, insomnia, restless legs that make sitting impossible. Peak misery day 1–3 (short-acting) or day 3–8 (methadone/long-acting); acute phase resolves by ~1–2 weeks (short-acting); sleep, mood and craving distortions continue for months (the PAWS grey zone; see the overview note).

**Dependence cluster:** daily use to feel normal (not to get high); doctor/chemist shopping for codeine/tramadol; injection marks and abscesses; money-collapse and crime-involvement markers; risk-taking under withdrawal desperation; and the classic Indian family sign: the theft of household valuables in withdrawal.

**Medical riders (examine for):** injection sites, abscesses, hepatitis (jaundice, liver), HIV clinical signs, tuberculosis, endocarditis (fever, murmur), dental decay, constipation/haemorrhoids, testosterone-related loss of libido and fatigue.

## 8. Diagnosis

- The **single-disorder, severity-graded logic** (see the overview note) applied to opioids, with withdrawal and pharmacology as the two most common entry signals.
- **Urine/blood screening** (opioid immunoassays: note: tramadol and fentanyl-type agents are missed by some standard panels; ask the lab what it actually detects).
- **Quantified history:** agent(s), route (swallow/smoke/snort/inject), daily spend, last use, longest clean period and its ending, prior treatment episodes, overdoses (including witnessed ones, the family often knows of near-deaths never told to doctors).
- **The rider screen:** HIV and hepatitis B/C testing offered to every opioid-dependent person (with counselling), tuberculosis symptom screen, pregnancy test where relevant, injection-site and cardiac examination.
- **Differential notes:** tramadol misuse also produces **seizures**; codeine-syrup misuse presents as constipation, dental decay and lethargy; pentazocine-type injection leaves classic scars; and the chronic-pain patient legitimately escalated: diagnose dependence by the control/harm pattern, not by the presence of pain.

## 9. Management

### (a) Emergency: overdose, teach every family this drill
1. **Shout and shake**, unresponsive? Call for help.
2. **Breathing check**: slow/absent or blue lips = **naloxone immediately** (intramuscular 0.4 mg or intranasal; repeat every 2–3 min; it is safe; the worst honest outcome is triggering withdrawal, which is survivable).
3. **Recovery position, airway watch**: naloxone wears off faster than most opioids: **transfer to hospital whatever the response.**
- Never leave the person "to sleep it off" alone; never inject salt/milk (folk remedies that cost minutes).
- The home rule that prevents most deaths: **never use alone, never mix with benzodiazepines or alcohol.**

### (b) The backbone: opioid agonist treatment (OAT)
- **Buprenorphine-naloxone (sublingual):** induction when the patient is already in **mild-to-moderate withdrawal** (at least 6–12 h after short-acting opioids; longer after methadone: precipitated withdrawal is the induction error to avoid); titrate over days to a stabilising dose (commonly 4–16 mg/day range, individualised); **daily supervised dosing early**, take-home privileges earned; the combined tablet's naloxone component deters injection.
- **Methadone** (full agonist, daily oral): effective, cost pennies; requires daily attendance and ECG awareness (QT prolongation at higher doses); availability in India is centre-limited.
- **Maintenance duration:** think years, like any chronic disease control; taper only when life is stable, slowly, with a relapse plan in writing. Patients and families should hear the honest line: *"Shorter treatment = higher relapse; the medicine is not a substitute addiction; it is the treatment of one."*
- **Why not "just detox" alone?** Detoxification without maintenance carries very high relapse within weeks, and because of **tolerance loss, that relapse is exactly when death risk is highest**. This single paragraph is the evidence-based answer to "why replace one drug with another?"

### (c) Symptomatic detoxification (when OAT is refused/unavailable)
Clonidine/lofexidine-type alpha-2 agents (blood-pressure caution), anti-diarrhoeals, antiemetics, NSAIDs for aches, benzodiazepines briefly for sleep and agitation, hydration, typically 5–10 days for short-acting agents; counsel explicitly about the post-detox overdose danger and provide the overdose kit + never-use-alone rule on discharge.

### (d) Naltrexone (opioid antagonist)
For patients fully detoxified (7–10 days minimum, negative urine screen, often a naloxone-challenge test) and highly motivated, with family supervision: blocks opioid effect entirely; long-acting injections exist abroad, limited in India. **Contraindication logic:** any current opioid use or pain conditions needing opioids. Its best niche: the employed, motivated patient with a strong family anchor.

### (e) Psychosocial and rider care
- Contingency-structure incentives (employment, family privileges tied to clinic attendance); relapse-prevention CBT; theistic/AA-adjacent (NA) fellowships; family contract as in the alcohol note (medicines held, money rules, relapse plan).
- **HIV/HCV care linkage**, TB treatment, contraception and pregnancy services; **needle-syringe exchange and safe-injection teaching** where harm-reduction programmes exist, hepatitis vaccination; condom counselling.
- **Pain management planning** for chronic-pain patients: opioid-sparing strategies, physiotherapy, clear contracts; in pregnancy, **never detoxify abruptly**: continue OAT (buprenorphine/methadone) and manage the neonatal abstinence with paediatrics.

### (f) India's legal frame (NDPS)
- NDPS governs possession and trafficking; **voluntary treatment seekers have protection from civil/criminal liability for the quantity kept for personal use when declaring for treatment**; counsel patients and families accurately, not mythically; petty-possession penalties remain harsh, so early treatment entry and documentation protect.
- Prescribing buprenorphine/methadone requires centre enrolment and record-keeping; know your state's availability map.

## 10. Indian practice

- **The referral reality:** families arrive after theft, job loss, overdose or police involvement; the productive posture is medical and non-moralising from the first minute; the patient has usually already been tried by everyone.
- **Where OAT is available:** AIIMS-NDDTC lineage, government de-addiction centres, some private programmes; **waiting lists are the enemy**; every waiting week is an overdose-risk week; use interim symptomatic protocols plus the family kit while arranging.
- **Chemist-driven dependence:** codeine syrups and tramadol dependence should be treated as opioid dependence proper (they are), naloxone-safe, buprenorphine-responsive; and the pharmacy chain must be closed as part of treatment.
- **The family kit teaching session (30 minutes, lifesaving):** overdose recognition, naloxone use, recovery position, never-use-alone rule, and the written relapse-response plan, deliver it before discharge, not after a death.
- **Stigma's worst habit:** calling methadone/buprenorphine "substituting one addiction for another"; families echo this and stop treatment; the antidote is the diabetes-insulin analogy said plainly at initiation.
- **Pregnancy pathway:** OAT continuation, obstetric co-management, neonatal abstinence planning, and no guilt framing of mothers; India's default is to blame them into abstinence, which harms both mother and infant.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: the tractor mechanic who kept detoxifying.** A 29-year-old Punjab mechanic, heroin-dependent for 7 years (chasing → injecting), had completed four 15-day "detox camps" and relapsed within weeks each time; his tolerance each time was lower than his habit. After his third relapse he survived a witnessed overdose (his brother's shouting brought a neighbour with naloxone). Admission to buprenorphine-naloxone maintenance: induction on day 2 of withdrawal, stabilised at 12 mg, supervised daily dosing at the district centre, family contract with his father as medicine-escort and employer at the family workshop. At 14 months: working, injecting-episode-free, HCV treated. Two brief lapses (a wedding season; a job setback) handled as data, not discharge.

*Teaching points: repeated detox without maintenance is the overdose conveyor; tolerance loss makes the relapse-after-detox the lethal moment; supervised maintenance plus employment is the evidence-based package; family-as-infrastructure (escort, employer, overdose teacher).*

**Case 2: the "cough" that was dependence.** A 33-year-old shop assistant was treated for "chronic cough" at three clinics over two years, each prescribing codeine-based syrup, each prescription refilled at the same chemist. He presented with constipation, lethargy, dental decay and morning "body pain" relieved by the syrup. Screening and history established pharmaceutical opioid dependence; the pharmacy was closed to him by family arrangement; buprenorphine-maintained detoxification then maintenance for 6 months with CBT-based cue work, treating his underlying generalised anxiety (which had driven the "cough" relief-function). Two-year follow-up: abstinent from opioids, anxiety on SSRI maintenance.

*Teaching points: "chronic cough" with constipation, dental decay and morning aches is codeine dependence until excluded; iatrogenic chain-prescription via multiple clinicians; treat the anxiety underneath; the family-pharmacy arrangement is part of the prescription.*

## 13. FAQ

**"Is this a disease or a police problem?"**
It is a treatable medical illness of the brain's calming system; the law still governs possession and supply, which is exactly why early, documented treatment entry protects legally as well as medically.

**"Why do you give one opioid to treat addiction to another?"**
The medicine holds the receptor quietly: it removes withdrawal, craving and the heroin high, and lets the person hold a job and a life, the way insulin holds diabetes. Calling it "one more addiction" mistakes the treatment for the disease.

**"How long will he be on buprenorphine?"**
Usually long: months to years; length is decided by stability, not by a calendar. Every early taper multiplies relapse risk, and the first months after stopping are when overdoses happen.

**"What do we do if we find him blue and not breathing?"**
Shout, call help, give naloxone (repeat if needed), turn him on his side, and take him to hospital even if he wakes; the naloxone wears off before the drug does. Never leave him "to sleep it off".

**"He says the withdrawal will kill him."**
Withdrawal from opioids is misery, not danger to life (unlike alcohol withdrawal); the danger is the relapse afterwards at lost tolerance, and the overdose. That is why we treat with maintenance rather than heroic stops.

**"Can he not just use painkillers occasionally?"**
For a dependent brain, "occasionally" reawakens the whole system; craving returns, then daily use. The plan's honesty is: one system, one medicine, one life rebuilt around recovery.

**"The chemist gives it without prescription. What can we do?"**
One family pharmacy with the family informed, prescriptions documented, and complaints where needed; closing the supply is part of the treatment plan.

**"She is pregnant and using. Should she stop suddenly?"**
No: abrupt detox harms mother and baby. Continue opioid agonist treatment under obstetric and addiction care; the baby's withdrawal is treatable, and a treated mother is the best outcome for the baby.

**"Are methadone and buprenorphine available in our district?"**
Availability is expanding through government de-addiction centres; if the nearest centre is far, ask about interim symptomatic protocols and travel/telephonic supervision, never accept "no treatment" as the answer.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **Mnemonics:** overdose drill as **"Shout–Breathe–Naloxone–Side–Send"**; withdrawal set as **"Yawning→Aches→Gooseflesh→Gut"**; maintenance logic as **"the medicine holds the door."**
- **Highest-yield facts:** opioid withdrawal is miserable-not-lethal; alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal is lethal; overdose kills via respiratory depression; naloxone is shorter than most opioids (re-dosing and transfer); buprenorphine ceiling effect; precipitated withdrawal; post-detox tolerance loss; OAT as the strongest evidence in addiction medicine; tramadol-missed-on-standard-urine-panels.
- **Classic traps:** (1) detox-only treatment plans; (2) inducing buprenorphine before withdrawal is established; (3) forgetting HIV/HCV/TB screens; (4) naltrexone started with opioids still on board; (5) "cough syrup is not an opioid problem"; it is.
- **Viva favourites:** locus coeruleus rebound; buprenorphine partial-agonist pharmacology; methadone QT; NDPS treatment-protection provisions; pregnancy and OAT; neonatal abstinence; needle-syringe programme evidence.
- **Indian exam corner:** national opioid prevalence ~2% with the north-west belt; pharmacy-leakage wave (tramadol/codeine); NDDTC/AIIMS lineage; state-wise OAT availability; the family overdose kit as a public-health short note.
- **Rehearse one line:** "In opioid dependence, the treatment is not the obstacle to recovery; tolerance loss after untreated detox is the killer, and maintenance is what keeps the door held."

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. Opioid withdrawal, compared with alcohol withdrawal, is:**
A. More lethal
B. Medically dangerous but rarely fatal, with high relapse and overdose risk afterwards
C. Fatal in 30%
D. Never significant

**Answer: B.** Miserable-not-lethal is the teaching phrase; the lethal events are overdose during relapse after tolerance loss (and co-sedation with benzodiazepines/alcohol).

**2. The family finds him unresponsive with slow breathing. The correct sequence is:**
A. Wait for him to sleep it off
B. Shout/call help, give naloxone, recovery position, transfer to hospital even if he wakes
C. Give him coffee and walk him around
D. Inject salt solution

**Answer: B.** Naloxone outlasts neither most opioids nor the risk; transfer is mandatory; folk rescue methods cost lives.

**3. Buprenorphine induction given too early (patient not in withdrawal) causes:**
A. Enhanced euphoria
B. Precipitated withdrawal
C. Serotonin syndrome
D. Nothing

**Answer: B.** Its high receptor affinity displaces the full agonist, hence the wait (6–12 h short-acting; much longer for methadone).

**4. The main reason detoxification-only treatment fails fatally is:**
A. Withdrawal mortality
B. Relapse at pre-detox doses with lost tolerance, overdose
C. Liver failure
D. Psychosis

**Answer: B.** Tolerance fades within days of abstinence; the "old dose" then stops breathing; maintenance prevents this.

**5. Which pair of screens belongs in every opioid-dependent patient's baseline?**
A. EEG and ECG only
B. HIV and hepatitis B/C (plus TB screen)
C. Skin allergy testing
D. Cardiac enzymes routinely

**Answer: B.** Injection-linked infections define the medical riders; hepatitis vaccination and TB symptom screening are standard.

**6. For a highly motivated patient fully detoxified for 10 days, a reasonable option is:**
A. Methadone
B. Oral naltrexone with family supervision
C. Codeine syrup taper
D. Pentazocine

**Answer: B.** Antagonist treatment suits the fully detoxified, highly motivated with a family anchor; methadone would serve dependence not yet stabilised, and pharmaceutical opioids as "tapers" re-feed the illness.
