# Acute Stress Reactions (Acute Stress Disorder)

## 1. In one line + overview

**An acute stress reaction is the mind and body's emergency-mode response to a terrifying event (shock, numbness, trembling, confusion, sleeplessness and replaying) that appears within hours and, in most people, settles naturally within days to a few weeks.**

Right after a road accident, a fire, an assault or the collapse of a house, the nervous system does what it was built to do: it floods the body with alarm chemistry and pulls blood away from "later" functions like digestion and sleep. Shaking, blank staring, restlessness, nausea, a racing heart and a mind that will not stop replaying the event are all normal responses to an abnormal situation, not signs of madness. Most people recover on their own with sleep, safety and the presence of people they trust, the single most important sentence in this whole note. Acute stress DISORDER is the clinical label used when these reactions are intense enough to disable the person in the first month (DSM-5: 3 days to 1 month after trauma); if the same picture persists beyond a month, the diagnosis changes to PTSD. The doctor's main jobs in this window are: recognise normal distress, protect sleep and safety, avoid harmful interventions (including routine single-session "debriefing"), and keep watch for the minority who will need real treatment.

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Acute stress reactions themselves are near-universal after severe trauma; the clinically diagnosable ASD is a narrower cut: prevalence after traumatic injury ranges widely (roughly 6–20% in assault and injury cohorts; higher after interpersonal violence and sexual assault, lower after motor accidents). Important and counter-intuitive: many people who later develop PTSD never met ASD criteria first, and many with ASD recover fully within weeks. ASD is therefore a *warning sign*, not a destiny. Predictors of persisting problems: dissociation at the time (peritraumatic), severe acute arousal, prior psychiatric history, prior trauma, and, very practically, ongoing pain, injury severity and lack of support.

**India.** Indian exposure is regrettably high: road-traffic injury (among the world's largest burdens), industrial and fire disasters, floods and cyclones, building collapses, riots and communal violence, and sexual violence. After events like the Odisha super-cyclone, the Kosi floods, or major train accidents, district teams see thousands of acute reactions within days, the majority normal, resolving without formal care. Indian family and community networks do spontaneously what PFA teaches; the system's task is to support them and to spot the minority deteriorating in the crowd. Sexual-assault survivors face a second gauntlet (medical-legal examination, police statements and social stigma) which itself prolongs the acute reaction; the POCSO/medico-legal pathway should be trauma-informed, not merely procedure-complete.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**The trigger (definitionally required):** exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violation, directly, witnessing, learning of a close person's violent/accidental death, or repeated extreme exposure (first responders, police body-handling, journalists).

**Who reacts harder (risk factors):**
- Biological: prior psychiatric illness (especially anxiety and depression), prior trauma history, female sex for interpersonal trauma, head injury (a double-hit: the injured brain is both the site and the subject), ongoing pain
- Psychological: high peritraumatic dissociation ("I left my body"), panic during the event, catastrophic interpretation of symptoms ("I am losing my mind", the fear of the fear)
- Social: isolation, evacuation/displacement, loss of home or livelihood, ongoing threat (abuser in the family, communal tension)
- Indian context: displacement into relief camps, loss of documents (ration card, Aadhaar) which converts trauma into a months-long administrative struggle, and stigma around sexual assault that suppresses disclosure and treatment

**Protective:** an available trusted person, immediate practical stability (shelter, food, information), continuing cultural/religious coping, and pain/medical issues treated quickly.

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: the alarm flood and its hangover.** The amygdala (the brain's smoke alarm) fires the instant threat is detected; adrenaline and cortisol pour in; heart, muscles and senses go to maximum. This saved your ancestors' lives. The problem arrives AFTER the fire is out: the alarm was so loud it now misfires, at a slammed door, a horn, a flashback. The system needs days of safety and sleep to re-calibrate. Most brains re-calibrate fine. This is the natural history, and explaining it in these words to patients ("your alarm is doing its job; it will settle") is itself treatment.

**Story 2: why sleep is the hinge.** During deep sleep the brain files the day's memories into long-term storage and, over successive nights, strips the raw emotional charge off them; the memory becomes "something that happened" rather than "something happening now". After trauma, the brain is too aroused to enter this filing state, so the event stays "hot" and keeps intruding in flashes and nightmares. Each night of good sleep files a portion. This is why clinicians attack insomnia aggressively in week one; it is PTSD prevention by another name.

**Story 3: dissociation, the circuit-breaker.** When the incoming event is unbearable in real time, the mind may trip a breaker: the person feels outside themselves, the scene goes flat and dream-like, time stretches. During the event this is protective. Persisting strongly AFTER the event, it is the single best-recognised predictor that recovery will be slower; the memory has not been properly "recorded" and keeps returning as raw fragments. Document it; follow such patients closely.

## 7. Symptoms

**The acute reaction hours-to-days after the event (normal territory):**
- Physical: trembling, sweating, nausea, palpitations, appetite loss, exhaustion, startle
- Emotional: numbness OR overwhelming fear, guilt, anger, grief swings
- Cognitive: confusion, poor concentration, fragmented memory of the event
- Behavioural: pacing, withdrawal, clinging, tearfulness, irritability, talking it through obsessively OR refusing to speak of it
- Sleep: difficulty falling asleep, nightmares replaying fragments

**Acute stress disorder (the clinical cut, DSM-5): 9+ symptoms across five clusters, lasting 3 days to 1 month, causing real impairment:**
1. **Intrusion**: unwanted memories, nightmares, flashbacks (feeling it is happening again), intense distress at reminders
2. **Negative mood**: inability to feel positive emotion; food tastes of nothing, children's laughter does not land
3. **Dissociative**: seeing oneself from outside, world unreal ("like a film"), amnesia for chunks of the event, dazed stance
4. **Avoidance**: steering away from the place, the people, the conversations, the news
5. **Arousal**: sleep trouble, irritability, hypervigilance (checking doors, scanning roads), concentration failure, exaggerated startle

**What it looks like in Indian wards:** the accident survivor who keeps narrating the crash scene to every visitor on loop; the fire survivor who sits silent, staring, refusing food, whose relatives are told "she is not responding"; the assault survivor who cannot remember the middle hour of the event; the relief-camp child who will not let the mother out of sight and wakes screaming.

**Red flags in the first two weeks (follow closer):** heavy dissociation persisting beyond the first day; no recovery of appetite/sleep by week two; escalating rather than settling symptoms; new heavy alcohol use; psychotic-level symptoms (rare: consider brief psychotic disorder or organic injury); suicidal statements.

## 8. Diagnosis

**The logic.** A qualifying trauma occurred. Symptoms began right after (DSM-5 requires them to last at least 3 days before you can even diagnose, protecting normal first-night reactions from diagnosis). Clusters pile up (9+ across the five). Functioning is impaired. Duration is under a month. Past that month with PTSD-level symptoms, re-diagnose PTSD; if symptoms are intense but sub-threshold, they are still clinically important; treat by need, not by label.

**Assessment craft:**
- See the person WITH a trusted companion where possible
- Screen medical first: head injury (did they lose consciousness?), pain control, blood loss, intoxication at the time (memory gaps may be alcohol, not dissociation)
- Ask open: "People often keep seeing or hearing the accident after it's over, is that happening with you?", concrete questions beat abstract ones ("do you have intrusive re-experiencing?" does not travel)
- Document dissociation specifically: it changes prognosis and follow-up intensity
- Instruments: the Acute Stress Disorder Scale/Interview (named, not reproduced) for structured assessment; ICD-11 keeps an acute stress reaction concept spanning up to 4 weeks from a shorter onset

### Differential table

| Looks like | Clue pointing elsewhere |
|---|---|
| Normal grief burst | Bereavement without threat-to-life exposure; waves tied to the lost person, no arousal cluster |
| Delirium (head injury!) | Fluctuating attention, disorientation to place/time, worse at night, image/observe as needed |
| Brief psychotic disorder | True delusions/hallucinations, not flashbacks; disorganisation beyond anxiety |
| PTSD | Symptoms persisting > 1 month |
| Panic disorder | Attacks began before trauma or persist without trauma-locked triggers |
| Substance withdrawal | Onset locked to last use; tremor, sweating, autonomic pattern |
| Dissociative (conversion) presentations | Motor/sensory loss without anatomical logic, often shaped by local illness models, common Indian post-traffic presentation (see somatoform concepts) |

## 9. Management

**Week one: do less, do it well (the evidence-backed hierarchy):**
1. **Safety and body first**: treat injuries, control pain, food, shelter, accurate information about what happened and what happens next. Pain uncontrolled is a PTSD risk factor.
2. **Psychological first aid (PFA)**, the operational standard: calm tone, meet basic needs, do NOT force retelling, do NOT promise "you'll be fine by Friday", reconnect with family, practical problem-solving (documents, transport, employer calls), and normalise: "everything you are feeling is a normal reaction to an abnormal event".
3. **Attack insomnia early**: sleep hygiene + short-term hypnotic (e.g., zolpidem 5–10 mg or temazepam-class for 3–7 nights, or melatonin where preferred; Trazodone 25–50 mg an option in depression-flavoured insomnia). Label it as a bridge, not a habit.
4. **Avoid single-session debriefing** (forcing a structured emotional retelling once, in the first days, as a "preventive procedure"): trials repeatedly show it does not prevent PTSD and can slow natural recovery. Ventilation on the person's OWN timetable is different; follow, don't force.
5. **Watchful waiting with scheduled review**: review at 1–2 weeks and again at 4–6 weeks. The people still symptomatic at 2–4 weeks are your treatment candidates.

**Week 2–4: treat the non-resolvers:**
- **Trauma-focused CBT** (imaginal and in-vivo exposure work, cognitive processing) is the first-line treatment for persistent acute symptoms and the best-evidenced PTSD prevention; brief courses of 6–12 sessions work in this window
- Medication is NOT routinely indicated for acute-phase prevention; SSRIs enter when symptoms are persisting toward the PTSD picture, depression is clear, or severity demands, not as a blanket early measure
- Manage alcohol requests directly: "drinking to sleep" is the most common Indian self-treatment; name it as a trap that strips deep sleep

*India note: a district hospital after a disaster does not need a psychiatrist on site in week one; it needs nurses, ASHA workers and teachers trained in PFA basics, a sleep-friendly ward arrangement (lights off, families co-sleeping allowed), and one clinician flagging non-resolvers for the week-2 and week-6 review lists. Trauma-focused CBT availability concentrates in medical colleges and tele-psychiatry (Tele-MANAS 14416 provides counselling-level support); train district psychologists in TF-CBT via the DMHP/NIMHANS distance programmes. Costs: generic zolpidem ≈ ₹40–100/week, sertraline ≈ ₹60–150/month (approx 2026). Disaster compensation paperwork (State Disaster Response Fund claims) IS mental-health work: unfinished claims keep the wound open.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **First responders are family, not ambulance.** In most Indian trauma events, relatives arrive first, carry the injured, and stay. Direct their natural energy well: keep the person company, keep them away from crowds of onlookers (the Indian "assembly" response; crowds re-traumatise), meet food and money needs, and ONE family spokesperson for police/press so the survivor does not repeat the account twelve times.
- **The police statement problem.** Multiple retellings in the first 48 hours, with pressure and judgment, can entrench memories. Wherever procedure allows, minimise repetition, allow a support person to be present, and schedule statements when the person is rested.
- **Disaster settings.** After floods/cyclones/collapse: mental-health teams integrated with relief, PFA-not-debriefing as the doctrine, child-friendly spaces (children process through play and routine; restore school attendance as fast as shelters allow), and a written list of flagged non-resolvers handed over before the team leaves. The 4–6 week handoff to district PHC/DMHP staff is where Indian interventions usually fall through; build the referral into the first contact, not the last.
- **Sexual violence.** One-stop-centre and Durga-bali-type mechanisms (state-specific), emergency contraception, STI prophylaxis, medico-legal examination with consent and dignity, and psychological support from the first contact; the acute reaction here is doubled by the system's handling. Confidentiality is treatment.
- **Cultural coping.** Prayer, ritual bathing, tonsuring, and collective mourning are functional coping for millions. The clinician's stance: ally with what calms and connects; only intervene where coping blocks recovery (e.g., a survivor who refuses to leave the house for ritual reasons while avoidance generalises).
- **Workers' compensation and return-to-work** after industrial accidents: driving the paperwork actively reduces chronicity. Return to the actual site (graded, accompanied) before full return to duty prevents the workplace becoming a permanent avoided territory.

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: the guard who saved the passengers.** A 38-year-old railway gateman in Bhopal witnessed a truck smash through his closed gate into a passenger train's path; he ran, pulled two injured passengers clear, and then could not stop the scene replaying. That night he could not sleep; next day at the OPD he was pale, hypervigilant, started at every horn, narrated the scene repeatedly, and felt "it is happening in front of my eyes again" twice. He met ASD criteria at day 4. Management: pain and medical review (bruises only), his wife stayed with him, single night of zolpidem 5 mg × 3 nights, PFA-style contact with a scheduled 2-week review, explicit "your mind is doing its job; most people settle in weeks, and you have the second duty of your life ahead". At 2 weeks: sleep restored, intrusions halved. At 6 weeks: mild startle around gates only, functioning full. No formal psychotherapy needed; one review session to consolidate. Teaching points: (1) the diagnosis came to him, no need to hunt pathologies in normal responders; (2) sleep was the single target; (3) scheduled reviews caught the trajectory instead of hoping.

**Case 2: the flood teacher who stopped eating.** A 29-year-old schoolteacher in a Kerala flood-affected panchayat was evacuated by boat after watching a neighbour's house collapse with the family inside. On day 2 in the relief camp she sat staring, refused food, answered in monosyllables, and could not remember the boat ride. On day 4 she was flagged by a PFA-trained teacher: heavy dissociation persisting. She was placed in the quiet corner of the camp with her sister, seen daily by the district psychologist, and started sleep restoration (temazepam-class short course plus a dark, fan-cooled space). By day 10 she was eating, by day 18 she began narrating fragments; the psychologist began brief trauma-focused work at day 22 as symptoms persisted. By week 6 she was back teaching in the camp school; the routine being itself treatment. Teaching points: (1) persisting dissociation is the flag that selects a person for active follow-up; (2) in camps, arrangements (quiet space, family co-sleeping, a job to do) out-prescribe prescriptions; (3) starting TF-CBT at 2–3 weeks for non-resolvers is the correct timing, neither day-2 forcing nor month-3 delay.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way patients and families ask it

**"Doctor, am I going mad? I keep seeing the accident again."** No: you are reacting, not going mad. The mind replays an overwhelming event to file it away; almost everyone who goes through something like this has these replays for a while. Most people find they fade over the coming weeks, especially once sleep returns. We will keep checking on you and step in with proper treatment if it stays.

**"Should we make her talk about it to get it out?"** Do not force it. Talking helps many people, but on their own schedule, when they choose, with someone they trust. Forcing a structured retelling in the first days has been tested and does not prevent later problems; it can even delay recovery. Being present without pressure is more powerful than it feels.

**"He has not cried at all. Is that worse?"** Not necessarily. Numbness is a common early protective response. What matters more is the direction of travel: eating, sleeping and engagement returning over the coming days is good; a person still blank, sleepless or unable to function at two weeks is the one we watch closely.

**"She cannot remember the accident clearly. Is that brain damage?"** After overwhelming events the brain records fragments, not a video; patchy memory is common. But ANY head impact with confusion or loss of consciousness needs its own medical check first. Once we have excluded injury, the patchy memory itself is expected and often fills in.

**"The doctor gave sleeping tablets, are they addictive?"** For a few nights, at the smallest useful dose, they are a bridge, and they serve a real purpose: sleep is the brain's filing system. We bring them down as natural sleep returns, and drinking to sleep instead is the trap we specifically want to avoid.

**"Will everyone who saw this get PTSD?"** No: the large majority recover without any formal disorder, especially with support, safety and restored routine. The acute reaction you see in the first days is not PTSD; it is the normal response. Our job is to watch for the minority whose symptoms persist past the month mark, and treat them properly then.

**"Should we take him to the temple / do the rituals?"** If ritual brings calm, connection and meaning to your family, it supports recovery, keep it. What we watch for medically sits alongside faith: sleep, food, function, and symptoms that refuse to settle. The two systems can work together.

**"He has started drinking heavily since it happened."** That is common and it is a warning, alcohol knocks out the deep sleep that processes the trauma, so the wound stays raw longer. Bring this to us directly: there are better tools for the nights, and we can help him use them.

**"She is back at work but jumpy and exhausted. Force rest or normalcy?"** Graded normalcy. Routine and work are medicine when returned to in steps, with a support person and without the survivor being thrown straight back into the exact scene. Full avoidance shelters the fear; full immersion floods it: the middle path, planned together, heals it.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**Time gates to memorise:** ASD = symptoms ≥ 3 days AND < 1 month after trauma (DSM-5); ICD-11 acute stress reaction within minutes-to-days, up to a few weeks; beyond 1 month → PTSD. Also memorise: PTSD does NOT require prior ASD; a person can skip straight to PTSD; absence of ASD does not mean absence of future PTSD.

**Five clusters, mnemonic "INDA-A": Intrusion, Negative mood, Dissociation, Avoidance, Arousal** (9+ total symptoms for the DSM-5 cut).

**Debriefing doctrine for exams:** single-session compulsory emotional debriefing (CISD-style) shortly after trauma is NOT supported; trials show equal-or-worse outcomes vs no intervention; the supported early package is PFA + watchful waiting; treatment begins for persisting symptoms at 2–4 weeks (trauma-focused CBT first-line).

**High-yield facts:**
- Peritraumatic dissociation = the most-studied early predictor of later PTSD
- ASD prevalence after assault/sexual violence > after motor accidents
- Pain severity and ongoing medical illness predict persistent acute symptoms
- First-session intervention: medical/pain assessment before psychological labels
- Sleep restoration in week one is PTSD prevention
- ASD is a risk marker, not a stage everyone passes through

**Classic traps:**
1. Diagnosing ASD on day 1 (the 3-day minimum exists precisely to protect normal reactions)
2. Prescribing compulsory retelling of the event as "counselling"
3. Missing closed head injury behind "dissociative amnesia"
4. Prescribing long hypnotic courses "to be safe", bridging only
5. Ignoring alcohol as self-medication because "he is an adult coping his way"
6. Diagnosing normal grief as ASD when there was no qualifying trauma (natural death of a parent is not, by itself, DSM-5 trauma, unless violent/accidental circumstances)

**Indian exam corner:** "differentiate acute stress reaction from PTSD", "psychological first aid vs debriefing" (a modern favourite), "mental-health response after disasters, the Indian DMHP model", "normal grief vs traumatic grief", and " dissociative vs organic amnesia post-accident". The Odisha super-cyclone and later flood responses are quotable Indian disaster-mental-health history.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. The DSM-5 window for diagnosing acute stress disorder is:**
A. 1 hour–72 hours
B. 3 days–1 month
C. 1–6 months
D. Any time within the first year
**Answer: B.** Minimum 3 days (protecting normal reactions), maximum 1 month (then PTSD).

**2. A survivor of a building collapse, day 2, is dazed, cannot sleep, and keeps re-experiencing the fall. Your best response is:**
A. Structured single-session debriefing today
B. PFA, pain/medical check, sleep restoration, review at 1–2 weeks
C. Start an SSRI immediately to prevent PTSD
D. Encourage repetition of the story to all visitors
**Answer: B.** Support + sleep + scheduled review; debriefing is not recommended; routine early SSRI is not indicated for prevention.

**3. The strongest early predictor that acute stress symptoms will become PTSD is:**
A. Male sex
B. Peritraumatic dissociation severity
C. Blood group
D. Body mass index
**Answer: B.** Dissociation during/around the event is the best-replicated predictive marker.

**4. A patient meets ASD criteria fully at week 2 after an assault. Correct plan:**
A. Reassure that all ASD resolves
B. Begin trauma-focused CBT now and review at 4–6 weeks; consider SSRI if severity persists
C. Admit for observation
D. Benzodiazepines for 2 months
**Answer: B.** Non-resolvers at 2 weeks are the treatment candidates; TF-CBT is first-line; long benzodiazepine courses are specifically not indicated (they impair extinction learning).

**5. Regarding single-session compulsory debriefing after trauma, the evidence shows:**
A. It halves PTSD rates
B. It has no effect at best, possible harm, not recommended
C. It works only in children
D. It works if done by police
**Answer: B.** The Cochrane-backed conclusion; PFA and watchful waiting replace it.

**6. A truck driver with head injury and fragmented memory of the crash is labelled "dissociative amnesia". Your first move:**
A. Accept the label: fragmentation is expected
B. Exclude closed head injury (concussion) first, then reassess
C. Start hypnotics
D. Refer for CBT
**Answer: B.** Post-traumatic amnesia from brain injury must be separated from dissociative amnesia before the psychological label sticks.

**7. Which statement about ASD and PTSD is correct?**
A. Everyone with PTSD passed through diagnosable ASD
B. PTSD can develop without ASD ever being present
C. ASD always becomes PTSD
D. ASD requires 3 months of symptoms
**Answer: B.** Many PTSD cases never met ASD criteria earlier; the pathway is not a mandatory staircase.
