# Sleep Disorders in Children & Adolescents — The Paediatric Rules Are Different

## 1. In one line + overview

**Children are not miniature adults in sleep medicine: their sleep problems are defined by parents, caused largely by parenting and development, present as over-activity rather than sleepiness, and are commoner, more serious, and more treatable than assumed.**

The Oxford chapter (Stores) opens by rejecting the comfortable belief that children's sleep problems are transient infant-phase trivia. The real picture: **20–30% of children from infancy to adolescence have significant sleep problems**; sleep disturbance is part of most child psychiatric disorders; sleep loss damages mood, behaviour, cognition, and even physical growth; and paediatric sleep medicine is barely taught, so treatable conditions are misread as ADHD, laziness, or bad behaviour. The chapter's distinctive architecture: sleep physiology changes radically across childhood (REM-dominant infancy, slow-wave-rich early childhood, phase-delayed adolescence); parents define, cause, maintain, and must therefore help treat the problem; the same disorder presents differently at different ages; the sleepiness that slows adults makes children *hyperactive*; and precise diagnosis separates the three complaint families: sleeplessness, excessive sleepiness, and parasomnias.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Describe age-specific sleep physiology (duration norms, state organisation, body-clock timing, SWS dynamics, adolescent phase delay).
- Explain the parental dimension: who defines the problem, what causes and maintains it, hidden motives, and effects on the family.
- List the developmental consequences of persistent sleep disturbance, including the growth-hormone failure-to-thrive link.
- Quote the 20–30% prevalence figure and identify the high-risk groups.
- Take a structured paediatric sleep history (the 24-hour review, sleep diary, three screening questions) and choose investigations (PSG, MSLT norms, actigraphy).
- Manage sleeplessness by age: infant prevention principles, toddler behavioural programmes, school-age fears and conditioned insomnia, adolescent delayed sleep phase syndrome with chronotherapy.
- Work the differential of excessive daytime sleepiness (insufficient sleep / disturbed sleep / increased need) including paediatric OSA, narcolepsy and Kleine–Levin syndrome.
- Recognise and manage the childhood parasomnias, separating them from nocturnal epilepsy.

## 4. Epidemiology

**20–30% of children from infancy to adolescence** have sleep problems considered significant by them or their parents; true prevalence of severe persistent disorders is unknown (methodological barriers). Rates run far higher in: children with psychiatric disorders (high rates across the board); children with **learning disability/intellectual disability** (particularly high and severe, yet amenable to treatment, and parents wrongly believe the problems are inevitable and untreatable); homeless children; children of mothers with affective illness (increased rate and severity). Obstructive sleep apnoea affects **at least 2% of children** (peak onset 2–6 years), with much higher rates in Down syndrome and other learning-disability syndromes. Narcolepsy: **4–9 per 10,000** in US estimates, onset by adolescence in a high proportion, first sign sometimes just prolonged overnight sleep. Nocturnal enuresis: ~5% of 7-year-olds at least weekly.

**India.** No comparable national paediatric sleep data exist; the clinical realities are distinctive, co-sleeping with parents (normal, adaptive, but making Western "independent settling" advice misfire), joint-family noise and shared rooms, late evening family routines, and an adolescent **coaching-class/night-study culture** that manufactures exactly the sleep debt the chapter describes, compounded by early school start times and long commutes. Paediatric sleep services (polysomnography for children) are scarce outside metros, making the history-based approach this note teaches the primary tool.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

- **Developmental biology:** progression of state organisation complete by ~6–12 months; body clock established by ~6 months; REM prominence in infancy (brain maturation role; fragile sleep); pronounced slow-wave sleep in early childhood (predisposes to arousal disorders); pre-pubertal years of especially sound night sleep and maximal daytime alertness; adolescence: decreased SWS, physiologically delayed sleep phase, sleepiness rising, physiologic sleep need no longer falling.
- **Parenting practices:** prolonged night-feeding beyond ~6 months; failure to set bedtime limits; inconsistency; unhelpful bedtime associations (stimulating bedroom activities, threats, recriminations); failure to teach self-soothing.
- **Parental psychopathology:** maternal affective illness raises children's sleep problems (connection debated); parental cognitions shaped by their own childhood.
- **Psycho-social adversity:** family disorganisation; homelessness.
- **Physical disorders:** OSA (usually large tonsils and adenoids, *not* obesity as in adults), other respiratory problems, pain and chronic illness, medications.
- **Adolescent lifestyle:** late-night social/recreational activity, nicotine, alcohol, caffeine, illicit drugs; plus stimulants to stay awake and sedatives to come down.
- **The psychiatric-comorbidity loop:** sleep disturbance is part of most child psychiatric disorders; sleep loss itself produces mood, behaviour and cognitive deficits; some "ADHD" is actually OSA, periodic limb movements or a circadian disorder, improving when the sleep disorder is treated.

## 6. What happens in the brain: three short stories

**The maturation story.** Sleep architecture is a developing organ: the newborn's sleep is REM-heavy, reflecting (perhaps actively running) brain maturation, and therefore fragile, explaining why infant sleep is so easily disrupted. By early childhood, slow-wave sleep dominates, and arousal disorders (sleepwalking, sleep terrors) arise *from* deep slow-wave sleep, which is why they cluster at this age. The pre-pubertal years bring the most efficient sleep of life; puberty then delays the phase biologically while society demands an early start, manufacturing the adolescent epidemic of sleep debt.

**The sleepiness-that-looks-like-hyperactivity story.** In adults, sleepiness reduces activity. In young children it *increases* activity: irritability, tantrums, restlessness, poor concentration, impulsiveness, aggression. So the sleepy child is labelled difficult or hyperactive, and treated with stimulants, while the sleep disorder underneath goes untreated. Some ADHD cases are OSA, periodic limb movements, or circadian rhythm disorder with behavioural improvement following sleep treatment.

**The growth story.** Growth hormone secretion is tightly linked to slow-wave sleep. Severe, early-onset sleep disruption, classically OSA, can produce **failure to thrive**: impaired growth as a *sleep* consequence. The chapter's wider warnings: impaired immunity and endocrine disruption from chronically poor sleep. Sleep is not downtime; it is a physiological work shift.

## 7. Symptoms: the three complaint families

**A. Sleeplessness (by age).**
- **Infants:** problems are largely preventable, bad habits form early. The prevention principles: a consistent 24-hour routine with a bedtime routine of cues; night-feeding not prolonged beyond ~6 months; teaching the baby to fall asleep alone (self-soothing; night waking is normal at all ages; the skill is returning to sleep without parental intervention); clear day-night differentiation; a sleep-conducive environment. Safety counselling belongs here (SIDS prevention: supine sleep, firm mattress, face uncovered, smoke-free room, no co-sleeping after parental alcohol/sedatives, no overheating).
- **Toddlers and pre-school children (~30% of this age):** not going to bed at the required time and/or repeated night waking with demands, including coming into the parental bed. After excluding medical factors, the four behavioural causes: separation anxiety; unhelpful bedtime associations; inadequate limit-setting; failure of self-soothing. Behavioural treatment is very effective, even in severe, long-standing cases and in children with learning disability or autism; graded change and desensitization preferred over "controlled crying" (quick but unacceptable to many parents).
- **School-age children:** night-time fears (content evolving with cognition: shadows and noises → ghosts and monsters → realistic health fears, usually transient; phobic intensity needs attention, and the content can reveal causes including abuse; fears may be one aspect of an anxiety disorder or PTSD with nightmares; sleep-onset insomnia from worry about school; **conditioned insomnia**: the habit of lying awake agitated persists after the original concern resolves; bedtime too early: the evening "forbidden zone" of wakefulness means a child put to bed before sleep-ready lies awake; **advanced sleep phase**: bedtimes so early that sleep needs are met before the family wakes: early-morning waking disturbs everyone; **idiopathic (childhood-onset) insomnia**: lifelong constitutional difficulty, diagnosed retrospectively).
- **Adolescents:** consistently high insomnia rates; biological (phase delay, less SWS) plus psychological and social pressures; nicotine, alcohol, caffeine and illicit drugs; difficulty falling asleep often = **delayed sleep phase syndrome** (below), misread as adolescent defiance; recriminations replace the chronotherapy that is actually needed.

**B. Excessive daytime sleepiness.** Under-recognised because parents, teachers and children don't see sleepiness as medical (read as laziness or disinterest, or misdiagnosed as depression or limited intelligence); lesser degrees in children produce irritability and over-activity rather than nodding off; high pre-pubertal alertness can mask sleepiness entirely. Distinguish **sleepiness** (need to sleep) from **fatigue/lethargy** (anaemia, endocrine disease, usually with other signs), and beware simulated sleepiness to escape situations. The differential has three columns: **insufficient sleep** (late nights + early school; irregular schedules; **delayed sleep phase syndrome**); **disturbed night sleep** (caffeine/alcohol/nicotine and illicit drugs; medical and psychiatric disorders and their treatments; frequent parasomnias; periodic limb movements; **OSA**); **increased need for sleep** (**narcolepsy**: REM physiology disorder; may begin as simply prolonged overnight sleep; classic tetrad may be slow to assemble; low CSF hypocretin/orexin is diagnostic; misinterpretation as laziness, depression or conversion disorder is common; **idiopathic CNS hypersomnia**; depression; substance abuse; neurological disease; **Kleine–Levin syndrome**: episodic sleepiness with overeating, hypersexuality and out-of-character behaviour, often mistaken for psychiatric disorder; menstruation-related hypersomnia).

**Delayed sleep phase syndrome (DSPS): the adolescent centrepiece.** Diagnostic features: persistently severe difficulty falling asleep; *uninterrupted sound sleep* once asleep; great difficulty waking for school; morning sleepiness and under-functioning giving way to evening alertness; pattern maintained by very late weekend lie-ins. It begins with habitually late nights (illness, bedtime battles, weekend socialising) until the phase is *physiologically* delayed; sleep earlier by choice becomes impossible. Treatment: gradual advance (15 minutes/day) for delays ≤~3 hours; for severe delays, progressive delay in 3-hour steps "round the clock"; maintenance with early-morning bright light and firm agreements on schedules. **Melatonin's role remains unclear** (uncertainties and potential reproductive-physiology hazards). Watch for "motivated sleep phase delay" (delay maintained to avoid school) and comorbid depression, both of which defeat treatment.

**Paediatric OSA: the contrast table.** Adults: obese, middle-aged, male, loud snoring, daytime under-functioning, CPAP-responsive. **Children: usually *not* obese; the usual cause is large tonsils and adenoids (adenotonsillectomy is the treatment); equal sex ratio; partial obstruction with hypoventilation rather than prolonged apnoeas; daytime presentation is over-activity and disruptive behaviour rather than visible sleepiness.** Night signs: snoring (but only ~1 in 5 habitual snorers has OSA), breathing-difficulty noises, paradoxical chest-abdomen movement, unusual sleep positions with neck extension, restless sleep, sweating, nocturnal enuresis, distressing obstructive awakenings. Day signs: mouth breathing, adenoidal facies, morning headache and bad mood, behaviour problems. Assess clinically; PSG with respiratory measures for severity (blood-gas effects can exceed the clinical impression); treat, usually adenotonsillectomy, to prevent physical (growth) and psychological complications.

**C. Parasomnias.** More common in childhood than adult life; different types can co-exist (arousal disorders with OSA); most cause parental alarm rather than child pathology. **Primary parasomnias:** sleep-related rhythmic movement disorders (headbanging, almost always remits spontaneously by 3–4 years; pad the cot, don't panic; unlike daytime headbanging in severe neurodevelopmental disorder); hypnagogic and hypnopompic hallucinations (common, can frighten); **confusional arousals, sleepwalking and sleep terrors** (partial-arousal disorders from SWS; the child remains asleep and unaware; extreme agitation looks like suffering; waking and comforting attempts *increase* distress and should be discouraged; violence can occur; protective measures needed); true nightmares (frightening dreams: frequent nightmares with intense bedtime fears suggest an anxiety disorder and the content may reveal the cause); **nocturnal enuresis** (~5% of 7-year-olds weekly; usually delayed maturation; loss of previous control flags physical or psychological causes; behavioural treatment very effective). **Secondary parasomnias:** nocturnal epileptic seizures, especially **benign rolandic epilepsy** and nocturnal frontal-lobe seizures (both sleep-related and easily misread); also asthma attacks, OSA-related arousals, gastro-oesophageal reflux, nocturnal panic, PTSD disturbance, dissociative states, and simulated parasomnias (PSG shows wakefulness). Diagnosis of parasomnias rests on the detailed episode narrative (onset-to-resolution sequence, timing, circumstances); home video is a superb tool; PSG only when clinical evaluation is inconclusive or another sleep disorder co-exists. Reassure: most primary childhood parasomnias remit within a few years; suspect psychological significance when a parasomnia is very frequent, unusually late in onset, persistent, or follows trauma; treat the minority with medication.

## 8. Diagnosis

**Screening (ask in every child):** difficulty getting to sleep or staying asleep? Excessive daytime sleepiness? Episodes of abnormal behaviour or experiences at night? Any positive → full sleep history.

**The sleep history (cornerstone):** current problems and evolution; past treatments and effects; **the 24-hour sleep–wake review**: evening (meal, activities, bedtime preparation and by whom, bedtime reluctance, fears/rituals, wants to sleep with someone, time to fall asleep), overnight (wakings; frequency, causes, return-to-sleep ability; episodic events: exact nature, timing, frequency; snoring, restlessness, enuresis; parents' reactions), waking (spontaneous or needs waking, final wake time, total duration, longest uninterrupted block, morning mood and refreshment), daytime (sleepiness, naps, lethargy, mood, overactivity, concentration and performance, unusual episodes); sleep environment and arrangements; development of sleep patterns; family history of sleep disorder; sleep questionnaire beforehand; **sleep diary for 2+ weeks** (corrects the fraught parent's distorted retrospective account). Plus developmental history, physical health and examination, behaviour and emotional state, family circumstances.

**Investigations:** PSG as in adults when indicated; **MSLT norms for school-age children: ~16–18 minutes to fall asleep is normal; less suggests significant sleepiness; sleep onset in ≥3 naps is a flag**, but normal MSLT in late childhood does not exclude a sleepiness disorder (naturally enhanced wakefulness); actigraphy for all ages (unobtrusive sleep–wake patterning); toxic screening where relevant.

## 9. Management: principles

- **Behavioural first, medication rarely:** the mainstay is altering parenting practices in ways each family can accept; sleep hygiene principles; explanation and reassurance for child and parents, sometimes effective alone.
- **Treat the parents to treat the child:** sometimes the programme cannot start until the parent is treated (maternal depression) or family problems are addressed; recognise hidden resistances (a child in the parental bed serving a marital purpose; fear of losing a disability allowance if the sleep problem improves).
- **Chronobiology for phase problems:** DSPS phase-resetting (gradual advance or round-the-clock delay), morning bright light, agreed schedule maintenance, with parental involvement throughout.
- **Physical treatment for physical disorders:** adenotonsillectomy for childhood OSA (CPAP less often); narcolepsy: medication plus support, advice on education, career and psychosocial matters; Kleine–Levin: supportive differentiation from psychiatric disorder.
- **Parasomnia management:** reassurance plus safety measures; avoid waking the child in arousal disorders; treat the underlying disorder in secondary parasomnias; medication only for a minority.
- **Optimism, calibrated:** children's sleep is more changeable than adults', but treatment must be chosen carefully, implemented properly, and supported by parents whose confidence and ability determine success.

## 10. Indian practice

- **Co-sleeping is culture, not pathology.** The chapter's Western "teach independent settling" advice must be reframed: the Indian family bed is normal and often adaptive; the clinical targets are timing, routine consistency and limits, not eviction from the parental bed. Co-sleeping after parental alcohol or sedatives remains a genuine safety caution to raise.
- **The adolescent sleep-debt factory.** Coaching classes, night-before-exam binge studying, late dinner in joint families, early school buses and long commutes, a structural sleep-deprivation machine. The counselling package: fixed wake time, daytime study planning, caffeine audit (the chai-and-cola timeline), weekend lie-in limits; frame it as performance engineering, not discipline.
- **Bedtime under family noise.** Shared rooms, late family TV: negotiate a household "quiet hour", not a solitary bedroom the family cannot provide.
- **The OSA tonsillectomy route.** Snoring children with daytime over-activity should reach an ENT assessment; in India the operation is available and effective, but the behavioural referral is often missed because nobody links hyperactivity to snoring. Ask the three screening questions at every paediatric OPD visit.
- **Enuresis and the faith-healing detour.** Before expensive or shaming "treatments", the behavioural programme is cheap, effective and parent-deliverable; school and family education removes blame.
- **Costs (approx 2026).** The core treatments (routines, diaries, graded withdrawal, scheduled waking) cost nothing; actigraphy and paediatric PSG exist only in major centres, so the clinical-history pathway is the standard of care; adenotonsillectomy is covered under most schemes including PM-JAY where indicated.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: The hyperactive snorer.** A 5-year-old is referred by his school for "ADHD-like behaviour": restless, aggressive, falling behind. His mother adds, when asked the three screening questions, that he snores "like his grandfather", sleeps restlessly, sweats at night, wets the bed, and wakes with morning headaches and a foul mood. He is thin, mouth-breathing with adenoidal facies; tonsils are large. Assessment: paediatric OSA (behavioural presentation). ENT referral → adenotonsillectomy; three months later the teacher reports a different child, and the bedwetting has largely resolved. Teaching points: the paediatric OSA triad (not obese, tonsils/adenoids, hyperactive presentation); "some ADHD is a sleep disorder"; PSG where available to grade severity, but clinical assessment is the cornerstone.

**Case 2: The boy who cannot wake for school.** A 15-year-old is brought with two years of "laziness and defiance": he cannot fall asleep before 2 a.m., cannot wake for his 7 a.m. school bus, sleeps through weekends until noon, and his father has begun punishing him for "attitude". Examination of the 24-hour pattern: once asleep he sleeps soundly and uninterrupted; morning function is poor, evening alertness is normal; the pattern is maintained by weekend catch-ups. Formulation: delayed sleep phase syndrome, misread as behaviour. Programme: explanation to the family (this is physiology, not defiance; the punishment stops); a negotiated round-the-clock phase reset during the vacation; fixed wake time thereafter with morning bright light (morning walk/outdoor study), weekend lie-ins capped at one hour, caffeine after 4 p.m. banned; school informed of the treatment phase. Teaching points: the DSPS quartet (late onset + sound sleep + morning dysfunction + evening alertness); chronotherapy replaces recrimination; check for the depression that travels with it.

## 13. FAQ

**"Is co-sleeping bad for my child?"**: In most Indian families it is normal and harmless. The goals are a predictable bedtime routine and enough total sleep, wherever the child sleeps. Only co-sleeping after a parent has drunk alcohol or taken sedatives is unsafe (breathing risk for the infant).
**"He fights bedtime, is he being defiant?"**: Usually not: separation anxiety, a stimulating bedroom, no firm limits, or a bedtime set before his body is sleepy. Check whether he is being put to bed in his evening "forbidden zone" of peak wakefulness; the schedule, not the child, is the problem.
**"She's hyperactive in the day, could she actually be sleepy?"**: Yes, genuinely: young children show sleepiness as over-activity, irritability and poor concentration. Ask about snoring, restless sleep, morning mood; some "ADHD" improves completely after the sleep disorder is treated.
**"Why can't my teenager just go to bed earlier?"**: Because puberty biologically delays the sleep phase: her body clock is set later, and the school bell is set early. The fix is a scheduled phase reset with morning light and fixed wake times, not punishment for "laziness".
**"Is sleepwalking dangerous? Should I wake him?"**: He is asleep and unaware; waking him tends to make the episode worse and more distressing. Guide him back to bed, protect him from hazards, and expect the episodes to fade with age. Frequent, late-onset or persistent episodes deserve a fuller assessment.
**"Will nightmares mean something psychological?"**: Occasional nightmares are normal at every age. Frequent nightmares plus intense bedtime fears suggest anxiety, and the nightmare content can point to the cause (including trauma or abuse).
**"Can poor sleep really affect his growth?"**: Yes: growth hormone is released in slow-wave sleep, and severe early sleep disruption (typically untreated OSA) is a recognised cause of failure to thrive; one more reason snoring in a small child is not cute.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **The headline pair**: 20–30% significant sleep problems; children are not miniature adults (presentation, cause, treatment all differ).
- **The hyperactivity masquerade**, the single most examinable idea: sleepiness → over-activity in children; OSA/PLMS/circadian disorder → "ADHD" that improves with sleep treatment.
- **Duration table (one-liner)**: 17/14/13/12/10/9+ hours from term to adolescence.
- **Arousal disorders from SWS**: why sleepwalking and terrors cluster in early childhood; child stays asleep; do not wake.
- **DSPS quartet** (late sleep onset, sound sleep, morning dysfunction, evening alertness) and treatment (15-minute daily advance ≤3h delay; round-the-clock for severe).
- **Paediatric OSA five contrasts**: not obese / tonsils-adenoids / equal sexes / partial obstruction-hypoventilation / hyperactive daytime picture, adenotonsillectomy as usual treatment.
- **Narcolepsy in childhood**: begins as prolonged sleep; hypocretin in CSF diagnostic; onset by 15 years in ≥one-third; misdiagnosed as laziness/depression/conversion.
- **High-risk groups for sleep problems**: psychiatric disorder, learning disability, homelessness, maternal affective illness.
- **Indian exam corner**: co-sleeping as normative; adolescent coaching-culture sleep debt; snoring-child-to-ENT referral; behavioural enuresis programme before anything expensive.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**Q1.** A 4-year-old with daytime over-activity, nightly snoring, restless sleep, nocturnal sweating and morning headaches is most likely to have:
- (a) Childhood ADHD requiring stimulants
- (b) Obstructive sleep apnoea from enlarged tonsils and adenoids
- (c) Narcolepsy
- (d) Delayed sleep phase syndrome

**Answer: (b).** The paediatric OSA picture, and the correct first move is ENT assessment for adenotonsillectomy, since the daytime behaviour often resolves with treatment.

**Q2.** The diagnostic quartet of delayed sleep phase syndrome includes:
- (a) Early sleep onset, fragmented sleep, evening sleepiness
- (b) Severe difficulty initiating sleep, sound uninterrupted sleep, great difficulty morning waking, evening alertness
- (c) Sudden sleep attacks with cataplexy
- (d) Snoring with paradoxical breathing movements

**Answer: (b).** The phase is physiologically shifted; the child sleeps well, at the wrong clock time, and functions poorly in the morning.

**Q3.** Confusional arousals and sleep terrors in a young child arise from:
- (a) REM sleep and dream content
- (b) Slow-wave sleep; the child remains asleep and unaware
- (c) Nocturnal epilepsy by definition
- (d) Conditioning to parental attention

**Answer: (b).** Partial-arousal disorders of deep sleep; waking the child is discouraged because it increases distress.

**Q4.** Average sleep requirement for a 1-year-old, per the chapter's table, is closest to:
- (a) 17 hours
- (b) 14 hours
- (c) 10 hours
- (d) 8 hours

**Answer: (b).** 17h at term, 14h at 1 year, 13h at 2, 12h at 4, 10h at 10, 9h+ in adolescence; many adolescents get far less.

**Q5.** In school-aged children, an MSLT mean sleep latency suggesting significant daytime sleepiness is roughly:
- (a) Below 16–18 minutes
- (b) Below 40 minutes
- (c) Any latency under 60 minutes
- (d) Latency cannot be interpreted in children at all

**Answer: (a).** 16–18 minutes is normal in school-age children; shorter latencies (and sleep onset in three or more naps) flag sleepiness, though normal results in late childhood can mask a true disorder because of naturally high alertness.

**Q6.** The first-line treatment for sleeplessness of a 3-year-old with repeated night waking and demands to enter the parental bed is:
- (a) Nightly sedative syrup
- (b) Behavioural programme addressing separation anxiety, associations, limit-setting and self-soothing
- (c) Co-sleeping prescription for life
- (d) Polysomnography before any intervention

**Answer: (b).** Behavioural methods are very effective even in severe, long-standing cases, including children with developmental disorders, when properly implemented; medication plays a very limited part.
