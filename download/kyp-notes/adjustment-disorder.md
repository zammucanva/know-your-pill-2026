# Adjustment Disorders

## 1. In one line + overview

**An adjustment disorder is a disproportionately intense emotional or behavioural reaction to an identifiable life change (a job loss, a migration, a diagnosis of cancer, a broken engagement) that begins within three months of the stressor, causes real distress or impairment, but never grows into a full depressive, anxiety or psychotic episode.**

Between ordinary everyday coping ("I'm stressed") and full mental illness ("major depression") lies a huge middle territory, and adjustment disorder is its clinical name. The trigger is knowable, visible, datable: the transfer order, the board-exam failure, the new dialysis schedule, the move to a strange city. The response is more than the situation deserves in intensity or duration by ordinary standards (tearful collapse, insomnia, inability to attend the office, quarrels, withdrawal) yet falls short of a full syndromal picture when you probe properly. It is one of the most commonly diagnosed psychiatric categories in the world precisely because life keeps happening to people, and in Indian practice it labels the anxious employee, the homesick student, the newly diagnosed patient, the retired officer. The essence of management is time-limited support, problem-solving and restoring function, not long-term medication. But it deserves clinical respect: suicide risk in adjustment disorder is elevated compared with the general population, and a missed evolving depression hiding under the label is the main trap.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- State the two timing rules (onset within 3 months; resolution within ~6 months of stressor termination, or persisting if stressor persists)
- Distinguish adjustment disorder from major depression, GAD, PTSD, and normal stress
- Use the DSM-5 specifiers and the ICD-11 subtype architecture
- Recognise why adjustment disorder dominates consultation-liaison, oncology and workplace practice
- Build a treatment plan around problem-solving therapy and support rather than prescriptions
- Identify when an adjustment label must be revised (emerging full syndromes, persistent symptoms)
- Take a suicide-risk pass through every adjustment-disorder patient
- Apply the Indian contexts: student migration, arranged-marriage ruptures, new medical diagnoses, retirement

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Adjustment disorder is among the most frequently used diagnoses in psychiatry; in hospital consultation-liaison settings it is often the single commonest psychiatric label (5–20% of CL referrals, and up to a third in oncology and palliative populations). Point prevalence in community samples ~0.5–1%, but in high-stress populations (medical patients, unemployed, caregivers, new migrants) it multiplies. It is most common between adolescence and middle age, with women diagnosed somewhat more often, partly help-seeking patterns. A sobering figure worth keeping: suicide risk in people diagnosed with adjustment disorder is several-fold higher than in the general population, comparable in relative terms to other diagnosed mental disorders in some register studies; the label "mild and temporary" must never mean "safe to ignore".

**India.** No dedicated national prevalence data (NMHS 2015–16 measured common mental disorders broadly). Indian clinical experience gives adjustment disorder a distinctive topography: migration for work and education (young men in hostels in Bengaluru/Pune/Surat; northeast and Ladakhi students in Delhi), new chronic-disease diagnoses in family medicine, workplace transfer orders, and, a modern growth area, retirement transitions of armed-forces and PSU personnel. Indian families present the patient as "broken since the transfer" and often want a certificate more than a conversation; the doctor's task is converting the certificate visit into a treatment visit.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**The stressor (definitional).** Any life change, negative usually (loss, illness, conflict, displacement, financial collapse) but sometimes positive-with-burden (promotion, new baby, migration abroad). Severity is judged subjectively: what matters is the meaning to the person, not the objective scale.

**Who over-reacts (vulnerability):**
- Biological: prior psychiatric history, anxious/dependent temperament, no prior experience of comparable stress (the sheltered achiever meeting first failure)
- Psychological: poor problem-solving repertoire, catastrophising style, weak emotional vocabulary (somatic instead of psychological expression, the Indian classic)
- Social: thin support network (the migrant alone in a new city), ongoing family conflict, economic precarity
- Cultural: role expectations that make failure unbearable (public exam as single life-gate; family honour riding on a girl's marriage)

**Indian context.** The single-stressor topography of Indian adjustment disorders: first-generation migration with loss of the joint-family shock-absorber; exam-result culture; arranged-marriage breakage (both sides); transfer culture in government and banking jobs; new diabetes/dialysis/cancer diagnoses delivered bluntly; and retirement identity collapse in men whose office was the self.

## 6. What happens in the mind/brain

**Story 1: the bridge under a load it was never rated for.** Ordinary coping is a bridge rated for ordinary traffic. A major life change drives a heavily loaded truck across it: most bridges flex and hold (normal stress response). In adjustment disorder, the flex turns to visible strain (the beams sing, the deck cracks) but the bridge does not fall (that would be a full syndrome: major depression, PTSD). Traffic management and time-limited reinforcement (support, problem-solving, restored sleep) let the bridge hold and re-rate itself. This is why recovery is the rule.

**Story 2: the coping budget runs out.** People cope with a finite budget of attention, energy and flexibility. A new stressor draws heavily on the budget; when the budget is exhausted, symptoms surface; insomnia is the rent unpaid first, then irritability, then withdrawal. The recovery plan is a budget plan: stop non-essential expenditures (extra shifts, social performance), import credit (support), and renegotiate the debt (problem-solving around the stressor itself).

**Story 3: the unfiled meaning.** An adjustment reaction is often grief's cousin: the loss of a future self. The transferred officer mourns the officer he was at his old posting; the dialysis patient mourns the eater and traveller he was. The "excessive" reaction makes sense as unprocessed loss of identity, which is why simply "treating symptoms" underperforms and meaning-centred conversations outperform.

## 7. Symptoms

**The core picture, three recognisable subtypes:**
- **With depressed mood**: low mood, tearfulness, hopelessness-flavoured statements, but never the full five-symptom, two-week, function-collapse of major depression; sleep and appetite wobble rather than derail
- **With anxiety**: nervousness, worry about the future, somatic anxiety (palpitations, tremor, "gas", tension headaches), short of the six-month uncontrollable-worry engine of GAD
- **With mixed anxiety and depressed mood**: the commonest presentation in Indian clinics
- **With disturbance of conduct** (DSM-5 lists it, ICD-11 subtypes it): acting-out, quarrels, rule-breaking, reckless spending or driving, drinking, a model student caught cheating; in adolescents this is the default language
- **With mixed disturbance of emotions and conduct**: tears plus fights

**The timing signature:** symptoms began close to the stressor (within 3 months by definition), track its course (worse around the court date, lighter after the transfer was cancelled), and are understood by the patient as connected to it (insight typically preserved, a distinguishing feature from most major illnesses).

**Functioning:** impaired in a real way (office attendance, studies, household roles) but not global collapse.

**What you should NOT find:** full syndromal depression, full PTSD clusters, psychosis, prominent suicidal intent (if present; you are looking at something more serious; re-assess), or symptoms that persist and generalise after the stressor is long gone.

## 8. Diagnosis

**The logic.** A stressor is identifiable and recent. The reaction began within 3 months. Distress is out of proportion to what the stressor would usually provoke, or causes marked impairment. The picture does not meet criteria for another disorder (the "sub-syndromal" clause; the diagnostic heart of the category) and is not merely an exacerbation of a pre-existing disorder. Bereavement has its own frame (see Bereavement note). Once the stressor has ended, symptoms are expected to resolve within about 6 months; if the stressor is chronic (a permanent colostomy, an unhappy marriage, a job that cannot be left), the disorder may correspondingly persist, the ICD-11 wording handles this explicitly.

**The assessment craft:**
- Map the stressor's timeline ON PAPER with the patient (onset, peaks, course) and match symptoms against it
- Take a deliberate suicide-risk pass (the safety clause above), direct questions, every time
- Probe for the syndromes you must exclude: sleep/appetite/weight/libido/concentration and duration questions for depression; worry-engine questions for GAD; trauma-screen for PTSD (the differential stressor question: was it loss-and-change, or death-threat-and-horror?)
- Ask about substances: the self-treatment layer
- Instruments: no dedicated validated scale is standard for this category in general practice; distress/impairment can be tracked with PHQ-9 or GAD-7 scores used as measures, not as diagnoses (their full-range scores would describe the syndromes you are excluding)

### Differential table

| Looks like | Clue pointing elsewhere |
|---|---|
| Normal stress reaction | Reaction proportional, transient, functioning preserved, person still solvent |
| Major depression | ≥ 5 symptoms, ≥ 2 weeks, anhedonia, neurovegetative change, often stressor-independent course |
| GAD | Worry generalised across domains, ≥ 6 months, uncontrollable worry engine |
| PTSD/ASD | Stressor was trauma-level (threat to life/sexual violence); intrusion-avoidance-arousal clusters |
| Bereavement | Death of attachment figure; grief waves, not disorder (see Bereavement note) |
| Exacerbation of pre-existing disorder | Pattern matches the old illness, not the new stressor |
| Substance-induced states | Onset locked to use |
| Personality-driven reactions | Lifelong repetitive pattern to minor stressors: formulation, not new diagnosis |

## 9. Management

**The architecture: short, active, functional.**
1. **Name and normalise**: "your mind is reacting to a real change; this has a name, a course, and an exit"; psychoeducation is itself a treatment here
2. **Problem-solving therapy (PST)**, the best-fit brief therapy: define the stressor as a problem set, brainstorm, choose, act, review; 4–8 sessions; the evidence base in adjustment disorders specifically and common mental disorders generally is solid
3. **Brief CBT**: cognitive work on catastrophic readings of the change; behavioural activation toward the avoided roles
4. **Restore the infrastructure**: sleep first, then routine, then role re-entry (office half-days, one subject restarted)
5. **Support mobilisation**, one trusted confidant activated (in India usually a sibling, a friend from the hometown, a colleague); family psychoeducation so "the reaction" is not read as weakness or insult
6. **Medication, optional, targeted, short:** a brief hypnotic for sleep (3–7 nights) or an SSRI at low dose for 2–3 months when the anxiety/depressive flavour is intense and impairing. The default answer to "should I start an antidepressant?" in pure adjustment disorder is: only if function fails to return as the plan runs.
7. **Follow-up with an exit plan**: reviews at 2–4 weeks and ~6 months; a persistent case at 6 months post-stressor is re-diagnosed, not shrugged at
8. **Safety**: suicide-risk assessment at every contact until clearly settling

*India note: the Indian treatment reality is the counselling room at the college, the medical officer, the district psychologist, or tele-MANAS (14416), free, multilingual, well-suited to exactly this disorder's intensity band. Private CBT/PST ≈ ₹500–1,500/session (approx 2026); district and medical-college services nominal. Workplace and college certificates ("advised rest") are an Indian ritual: write them honestly, dated and short, open-ended medical certificates convert adjustment disorders into prolonged sickness roles.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **The migrating student.** Hostel presentations (homesickness + academic shock) peak in the first semester; colleges with counsellors catch them, others see the failure/dropout version. Practical script: routine (sleep, mess timings, one fixed study slot), one human anchor (batchmate/senior from hometown), scheduled home call rather than endless homesick scrolling, and a review date.
- **Transfer orders and the family.** Government/banking transfer culture splits families annually. The reaction often belongs to the spouse (moved, uprooted, support-less in the new city) rather than the officer. Enlist both in the plan; a children's-school continuity plan is mental-health work here.
- **The new-diagnosis consultation.** Indian practice still often delivers cancer/dialysis/HIV news bluntly or by concealment (family-first disclosure). A large share of "adjustment disorder with depressed mood in relatives" is manufactured at the moment of diagnosis. SPIKES-style structured breaking of bad news (setting, perception, invitation, knowledge, empathy, strategy) belongs in every physician's skill set; prevention beats treatment.
- **Retirement identity collapse.** Men whose office was the self decompensate in the first year out: irritability, somatic complaints, "uselessness". Plan a pre-retirement consultation (roles, structure, one purpose project) as preventively as a cardiac review.
- **Arranged-marriage ruptures.** Both the jilted party and the family system react; conduct-flavoured reactions (confrontations, revenge spending) are common in young men. Safety assessment is non-negotiable in this group.
- **Certificates and courts.** Litigation-linked reactions invite certificate-seeking; write facts and function, not opinions about courts. Insurance/disability claims do not ride on this diagnosis in India; do not let the tail wag the treatment.

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: the transferred accountant.** A 38-year-old bank officer posted from Coimbatore to Shillong presented with two months of sleeplessness, "gas", weeping spells, and office errors after a transfer separated him from his family. No neurovegetative collapse, no worthlessness, no suicidal ideation; symptoms tracked the posting date. Diagnosis: adjustment disorder with mixed anxiety and depressed mood. Management: problem-solving structure (family relocation feasibility, wife's school-transfer case, 6-month plan), sleep bridge for one week, weekly telephonic reviews for a month, and a college-senior-style anchor (a Coimbatore-origin officer in Shillong, found through the staff club). At 8 weeks: functioning full, sleep natural; the family relocation case succeeded at month 5. Teaching points: (1) the stressor is treatable as a problem, not only the symptoms; (2) the "anchor person" prescription is often the active ingredient in migration cases; (3) timeline-mapping confirmed the diagnosis at the first visit.

**Case 2: the colostomy and the cook.** A 55-year-old school cook in Nagpur developed an adjustment disorder with depressed mood after a colostomy for rectal cancer: she refused to return to the school kitchen ("the smell will shame me"), stopped meeting neighbours, and cried through outpatient oncology reviews, without full depression syndromes, appetite intact, engaging in conversation about the surgery. Management: brief CBT around the shame forecast (test: did anyone in the village ever comment on anyone's colostomy? — nobody had even seen one), stoma-nurse practical coaching, a graded return plan (pack lunch with a co-cook for 2 weeks, then full duty), and one session with her daughter on how to answer neighbour questions. Full function by month 3. Teaching points: (1) consultation-liaison psychiatry should be scheduled into oncology, not merely requested; (2) the cognitive work is about the predicted social meaning, not the bowel; (3) graded role-return beats rest certificates.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way patients and families ask it

**"Is this depression, doctor?"** Not as we use the word clinically. Depression is an illness that takes over the whole system, sleep, appetite, energy, worth. What you have is a strong reaction to a real change: your mood and sleep wobble because the situation loads them. It usually settles as the situation is worked on, and it rarely needs the long-term medicine that depression does.

**"Everyone gets transferred. Why did only I break?"** People arrive at stressors with different loads and different shock-absorbers; support, temperament, experience, and how much identity is tied to what changed. "Breaking" under a real load is not weakness; it is a signal that the load exceeded your current rating, and ratings can be rebuilt.

**"Do I need tablets?"** Usually not. The first prescriptions here are sleep, structure, one anchor person and a problem plan. A short medicine course (some nights of sleep help, or a low-dose antidepressant for a few weeks) is reserved for when function is not returning. Long-term medication is rarely part of this condition.

**"How long will this last?"** As a rule, weeks to a few months once the stressor settles or is resolved, and the expectation itself is treatment. If months pass after things improved and you are still the same, come back: we then look again at what is really going on.

**"My son has started fighting and missing college since the results. Is this adjustment problem?"** That pattern fits the conduct-flavoured version; acting-out is the male-adolescent dialect of distress. It needs the same respect: a real reaction, a structured plan, one review, and a direct check about darker thoughts, because this group's surface anger can hide real despair.

**"Should he take rest at home for a month?"** Usually the opposite. Extended rest removes the routine and roles that rebuild the rating. The better certificate is graduated: half-days, lighter duty, one restarted subject, activity as prescription, with the workplace/college informed factually.

**"We are arranging his marriage to 'give him stability'. Is that advisable now?"** Adding a major life change on top of an unresolved reaction usually doubles the load. Sequence matters: settle the current stressor, restore function, then make big decisions; the marriage will be a better marriage in six months.

**"The counsellor just talks. Can talking really fix this?"** For this particular diagnosis, structured talking IS the specific treatment; problem-solving, cognitive and behavioural work are not merely comfort; they change outcomes. The tablet is the supplement here, not the therapy.

**"He says nothing is wrong but his behaviour changed completely after the diagnosis."** Adjustment reactions often wear a conduct or somatic coat in men; the changed behaviour IS the statement. Engage the behaviour, name the change gently ("since the surgery, everything shifted; that is common"), and offer the plan without requiring him to say "I am distressed" first.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**The four gates to memorise:** identifiable stressor; onset within 3 months; out-of-proportion distress or marked impairment; sub-syndromal (no other disorder better explains it). Plus the two course rules: resolution within ~6 months of stressor termination; persistence permitted only with a chronic stressor.

**High-yield facts:**
- Most common psychiatric diagnosis in consultation-liaison and oncology settings
- Suicide risk elevated versus general population, a register-based, examinable fact; always assess
- Problem-solving therapy and brief CBT = first-line; medication optional/short
- Conduct-disturbance presentations dominate in adolescents
- ICD-11 architecture: depressed-mood / anxiety / mixed / conduct-disturbance / mixed-emotions-and-conduct subtypes; DSM-5: with depressed mood, with anxiety, with mixed, with conduct, with mixed disturbance, unspecified

**Classic traps:**
1. Using "adjustment disorder" as a soft exit when full depression criteria are actually met, the commonest real-world misuse
2. Skipping the suicide screen because "it is only adjustment"
3. Writing open-ended rest certificates: prolonging the disorder
4. Forgetting the 6-month re-review: persistent symptoms mean re-diagnosis
5. Mistaking PTSD for adjustment disorder when the stressor was trauma-level
6. Missing the substance self-treatment layer under the presenting tears

**Indian exam corner:** " differentiate adjustment disorder from major depression and PTSD" (the evergreen trio), "subtypes of adjustment disorder", "adjustment disorder in medical illness (consultation-liaison role", "problem-solving therapy) principles". The Indian-context viva bonus: migrant-student and transfer-order presentations, and the family-first disclosure pattern in oncology that manufactures these reactions.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. By definition, symptoms of adjustment disorder begin within ___ of the stressor:**
A. 1 week
B. 1 month
C. 3 months
D. 1 year
**Answer: C.** Three months is the DSM-5 onset gate.

**2. The "sub-syndromal" clause means:**
A. Symptoms must be mild
B. Criteria for another mental disorder are not met
C. The stressor must be objectively minor
D. Functioning must be intact
**Answer: B.** The diagnosis exists only in the space not occupied by full syndromes, while distress/impairment remains real.

**3. First-line treatment for a typical adjustment disorder is:**
A. Long-term SSRI
B. Antipsychotic
C. Brief psychotherapies (problem-solving/CBT) plus support and routine
D. Admission
**Answer: C.** Talking-structured interventions are the specific treatment; medicines are optional, short and targeted.

**4. A patient with "adjustment disorder" at review 8 months after the stressor fully resolved, still symptomatic. Correct action:**
A. Continue the same diagnosis indefinitely
B. Re-diagnose: persistent symptoms demand a new look (evolving mood disorder etc.)
C. Double the hypnotic
D. Discharge with reassurance
**Answer: B.** Persistence beyond ~6 months after stressor resolution violates the construct, re-examine.

**5. The safety clause: patients with adjustment disorder, compared with the general population, show:**
A. No elevation in suicide risk
B. Elevated suicide risk, every contact includes a suicide screen
C. Lower risk
D. Risk equal only in the elderly
**Answer: B.** Register studies show several-fold elevation, the most examinable and most clinically urgent fact in this category.

**6. An adolescent becomes combative and truants after his parents' divorce, with no syndromal mood or anxiety picture. Best fit:**
A. Conduct disorder
B. Adjustment disorder with disturbance of conduct
C. PTSD
D. Bipolar disorder
**Answer: B.** The behaviour tracks an identifiable recent stressor, without the pervasive pattern of conduct disorder across contexts and time.

**7. The Indian clinical ritual most likely to prolong an adjustment disorder:**
A. Early problem-solving sessions
B. Open-ended "rest" certificates
C. Sleep hygiene advice
D. Family psychoeducation
**Answer: B.** Long rest certificates convert a time-limited reaction into a prolonged sickness role, write dated, specific, graded certificates instead.
