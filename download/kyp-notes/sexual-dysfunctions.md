# Sexual Dysfunctions — Desire, Arousal, Orgasm and Pain Problems in Men and Women

## 1. In one line + overview

**Sexual problems become dysfunctions when they are persistent, distressing, and not simply an understandable response to the person's circumstances.**

The Oxford chapter teaches a calm, unembarrassed way to think about sex gone wrong. Sexual response runs on a chain (desire, arousal, orgasm, resolution) and any link can fail, in men or women, for reasons ranging from resentment in a marriage to diabetes, antidepressants, or a pituitary tumour. The chapter's two great teaching gifts are the **Dual Control Model** (sexual response as an accelerator plus brakes, with people differing in brake-sensitivity) and the **three windows approach** to assessment (the current situation, the person's long-term vulnerability, and health/medication effects). Its central management message is ahead of its time: treat the **couple**, not just the individual, and combine psychological and medical treatments rather than choosing between them.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Describe the sexual response chain (DEOR) and explain why "desire" in women is often receptive rather than spontaneous.
- State the Dual Control Model and what high vs low inhibition propensity predicts.
- Distinguish a sexual *problem* (common, often transient) from a *dysfunction* (persistent, distressing), using the duration data from NATSAL.
- List the main presentations in men (erectile difficulty, low desire, premature and delayed ejaculation) and in women (low desire/arousal overlap, orgasm difficulty, dyspareunia and vaginismus, PGAD).
- Work through the three windows: current situation, individual vulnerability, health and medication.
- Name the drug classes that commonly impair sexual function (SSRIs, antipsychotics, beta-blockers, clonidine) and the two antidepressants that spare it (bupropion, nefazodone).
- Explain why nitrate use absolutely contraindicates PDE-5 inhibitors.
- Structure sex therapy (the three-part behavioural programme, typical 12 sessions over 4–5 months) and combine it rationally with pharmacotherapy.
- Manage confidentiality and cultural-value issues in an Indian clinic.

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Rates depend on definitions: a lesson the chapter teaches through a famous contrast. A US national survey (Laumann) reported "sexual dysfunction" in 43% of women and 31% of men, based on problems lasting "several months or more" in the past year. When the same questions were asked in the British NATSAL survey but with explicit durations, 53.8% of women and 34.8% of men reported at least one problem lasting **at least 1 month** in the past year, yet only **15.6% of women and 6.2% of men** had problems lasting **at least 6 months**. Transient problems are nearly universal; persistent ones are the clinical minority. In men, age works predictably against erections: complete erectile failure was reported by 5% of 40-year-olds and 25% of 70-year-olds (Massachusetts Male Aging Study); absent desire rises from 2% (ages 45–59) to 18.2% (75+). Premature ejaculation, contrary to popular belief, does **not** increase with age. In women, interest declines with age but older women are less likely to consider it a problem, and partner availability shapes women's sexuality more than men's.

**India.** No comparable national probability survey exists; clinic-based experience says the same problems arrive through different doors. Consummation failure and vaginismus commonly reach gynaecologists and infertility clinics rather than psychiatry; erectile difficulty reaches urologists and unlicensed "sex clinics" (a sector notorious for exploitation); desire problems after childbirth or during depression arrive in psychiatry disguised as "weakness" or marital conflict. Semen-loss anxiety ("dhat"), nightfall worries and performance fears are culturally salient presentations documented in Indian literature. The practical epidemiology a psychiatrist actually needs: SSRI- and antipsychotic-induced sexual dysfunction is likely the most common *iatrogenic* sexual dysfunction seen in Indian OPDs, and it is a leading hidden cause of self-stopping medication.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**Window 1: the current situation.**
- Relationship problems, especially resentment and insecurity ("letting go" needs safety).
- Poor communication about sexual feelings and needs.
- Misunderstandings and lack of information.
- Unsuitable circumstances: fatigue, lack of privacy, work pressure.
- Concerns about pregnancy or sexually transmitted disease.
- Low self-esteem and poor body image.

**Window 2: individual vulnerability.**
- Long-standing negative attitudes to sex ("sex is bad"), usually from childhood.
- A general need to maintain self-control; difficulty letting go with another person present.
- History of sexual abuse or trauma (mechanisms unclear but relevance established).
- Propensity for sexual **inhibition**: measurable, roughly normally distributed, partly heritable in men; high inhibition scores relate to erectile problems in men and to women's sexual problems (especially "arousal contingency"; arousal that collapses when circumstances are not "just right" or attention drifts).

**Window 3: health and medication.**
- **Depression** reduces interest and arousability (impaired nocturnal penile tumescence shows the excitation system itself is damped in endogenous depression). A paradoxical minority report *increased* interest when low, in men, 23.3% of depressed versus 6.9% of non-depressed; in women 8.8% versus 1.7%, which can drive sexual risk-taking or sex-as-mood-regulator.
- **Anxiety**: loss of interest in generalized anxiety disorder (Zurich cohort); sexual aversion in panic disorder; premature ejaculation notably common in men with social phobia.
- **Schizophrenia**: sexual thoughts and activity may increase, typically autoerotic or compulsive, without object-relational quality; antipsychotics then add drug effects on top.
- **Neural damage**: autonomic and peripheral neuropathy, multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury, prostatectomy, hysterectomy → erectile difficulty, impaired vaginal response, loss of emission, retrograde ejaculation.
- **Vascular disease**: peripheral vascular disease, ischaemic heart disease.
- **Endocrine**: low testosterone (loss of interest, impaired erection, loss of emission if severe); menopausal oestrogen lack (impaired lubrication); hyperprolactinaemia from pituitary adenomas (loss of interest, erectile difficulty, more likely in men).
- **Metabolic**: hepatic and renal disease.
- **Diabetes**, the multi-channel model: small-vessel disease + autonomic/peripheral neuropathy + associated hypogonadism; erectile dysfunction more common in Type 1 than Type 2.
- **Drugs**: SSRIs (orgasm/ejaculation inhibition, the most predictable effect); tricyclics; antipsychotics (roughly 60% of men, 30–90% of women affected); beta-blockers such as propranolol (erection problems); clonidine (erectile problems in ~25%); guanethidine; steroidal contraceptives reduce free testosterone but reduce interest only in a minority ("testosterone-dependent" women).

## 6. What happens in the body: three short stories

**The accelerator and the brakes.** The Dual Control Model pictures sexual response as an accelerator (excitation) plus brakes (inhibition), both running in the brain. "Normal" braking is adaptive; it shuts sexuality down when sex is unwise. People differ in brake-sensitivity from birth onwards (men's inhibition propensity shows heritability). Heavy brakes produce the person whose desire evaporates the moment anything is wrong; light brakes produce the person whose sexuality ignores danger. The same model explains why anxiety, resentment and lack of privacy present clinically as sexual dysfunction.

**The plumbing story (why a tablet can fix an erection).** The flaccid penis is kept flaccid by sympathetic (noradrenergic) tone squeezing its small vessels. Sexual arousal releases nitric oxide and VIP, which relax cavernosal smooth muscle through the second messenger cGMP; blood fills the chambers against the non-stretchable tunica albuginea, veins are squeezed shut, and the organ becomes rigid. Phosphodiesterase 5 (PDE-5) is the enzyme that destroys cGMP, so PDE-5 inhibitors let the signal last longer, restoring the *response to stimulation* (they do not create arousal by themselves; desire must still be present). The same nitrate-and-cGMP chemistry is why giving a PDE-5 inhibitor to a man on nitrates can precipitate catastrophic hypotension.

**The desire story is not the same story in women.** In men, desire and erectile responsiveness travel together. In women, the mapping is looser: many women do not separate "desire" from "arousal"; desire is frequently **receptive** (triggered by intimacy, arriving after arousal begins rather than before: Basson's model); vaginal lubrication can occur as an "automatic" genital response to stimuli a woman does not subjectively find appealing; and dryness does not necessarily mean absence of arousal. The clitoris (glans, shaft, paired crura, vestibular bulbs, a triplanar complex) engorges without true rigidity. This is why the chapter is skeptical of women's-dysfunction categories built by copying the male template.

## 7. Symptoms

**In men.**
- **Erectile difficulty**: trouble getting, or keeping, an erection; severity ranges from occasional to complete failure; the man's and partner's reactions often determine how disabling it becomes.
- **Low desire**: usually linked to fewer spontaneous erections; can exist with intact erections needing firmer stimulation; often tangled with erectile difficulty, and which came first is not always clear.
- **Premature ejaculation (PE)**: inability to delay orgasm/ejaculation as desired. Primary (lifelong) vs secondary (acquired); secondary PE is confounded by erectile difficulty; a man who takes longer to become erect may reach ejaculation threshold earlier in the arc. In severe cases emission precedes entry, with weak spasms and semen seeping rather than ejaculating.
- **Delayed or absent ejaculation**: much less common; may occur only with a partner, or only during penetration, or even during masturbation. The classic iatrogenic cause is SSRIs (the primary drug effect appears to be on orgasm triggering, since women on SSRIs are anorgasmic too).
- **Pain**: testicular ache after prolonged unrelieved arousal; urethral pain at ejaculation, both uncommon.

**In women.**
- **Low desire / arousal problems** (the most common presentation; categories overlap heavily in practice).
- **Orgasm difficulty**: often situational (orgasm with masturbation but not with the partner); some women need more specific or intense stimulation; an estimated 10–15% never experience orgasm in their lifetime. Before calling it an orgasm problem, confirm adequate arousal first.
- **Dyspareunia** (pain on entry or intercourse) and **vaginismus**, traditionally defined as involuntary spasm of the outer-third vaginal muscles blocking penetration, though the chapter notes the evidence for actual spasm is poor. **Vulvar vestibulitis**: pain on touching the labia or introitus. Whether these are "sexual" or pain disorders is actively debated.
- **Persistent genital arousal disorder (PGAD)**: genital/breast vasocongestion persisting hours to days, only briefly relieved by orgasm, experienced as intrusive and unwanted, without desire, with no male equivalent, probably because of men's longer post-orgasmic refractory period.
- **Sexual aversion**: extreme avoidance of all sexual contact; infrequent, occurs in both sexes.

## 8. Diagnosis

**Classification logic (with the chapter's critique).** DSM-IV defined sexual dysfunction as disturbance of desire and the psychophysiological changes of the response cycle causing **marked distress and interpersonal difficulty**; ICD-10 is far briefer, gives almost no operational criteria, and does not require distress. Both organised women's categories by copying the male response cycle, which the chapter criticises: heavy comorbidity between categories, genital focus, neglect of relationship and context. *(Modern update, flagged as post-Oxford: DSM-5 merged female desire and arousal problems into female sexual interest/arousal disorder; merged dyspareunia and vaginismus into genito-pelvic pain/penetration disorder; and defined PE around ejaculation within about 1 minute of penetration, per the International Society for Sexual Medicine. The Oxford chapter's scepticism about the old categories is exactly the reasoning that produced these merges.)*

**The crucial distinction: problem vs dysfunction.** Ask two things: duration (persistent, not a single bad month) and context (is the loss of interest an *understandable or even adaptive* response to current circumstances, the Dual Control Model's "adaptive inhibition"?). A woman with low desire in a resentful marriage does not have a malfunctioning sexual system; she has a marriage problem presenting through sex.

**Assessment structure: the three windows.** See both partners wherever possible: conjoint interview first, then each separately. Cover the nature of the problem, each partner's interest and response, sexual history, current relationship, contraceptive and reproductive history, alcohol and recreational drugs, and each partner's mental state. Assess motivation and commitment: couple therapy fails when one partner attends under protest. End the first interview with a preliminary formulation and plan.

**Medical workup where indicated.** Physical examination, glucose (diabetes), testosterone and prolactin (low desire; the treatable causes: hypogonadism, pituitary adenoma), lipids and vascular assessment for erectile difficulty; a clinical baseline before any pharmacological treatment.

**Diagnostic sequencing: the chapter's key move.** If window-1/window-2 factors are evident (inhibition-producing issues), do **not** start drugs. Either assess further or begin sex therapy: its first two stages (non-genital, then genital touching, with intercourse banned) are themselves diagnostic, they surface the resentment, trust problems, negative attitudes or trauma material that medication cannot touch. After three or four sessions, re-appraise and decide whether to add a drug. If no such factors are evident, a trial of pharmacological treatment after physical examination and baseline tests is reasonable. A worked rule: a couple's first two therapy stages remove "performance pressure", making the decision about a PDE-5 inhibitor cleaner, not murkier.

## 9. Management

**Sex therapy: the three-part behavioural programme (Masters & Johnson core, adapted).**
- **Part 1: non-genital touch.** Intercourse and genital stimulation banned; partners take turns initiating non-genital contact. Surfaces relationship issues: distrust, resentment, stereotyped assumptions ("once aroused, a man cannot be expected to stop").
- **Part 2: genital + non-genital touch, intercourse still banned.** Surfaces intrapersonal material: long-held negative sexual attitudes, sequelae of earlier abuse.
- **Part 3: gradual approach to penetration.** Surfaces performance anxiety and fear of pain.
- Weekly sessions initially, extending intervals as major issues resolve; typically **12 sessions over 4–5 months**, with the last two or three spaced out to consolidate and handle setbacks. Agree a set number of sessions at the outset, with a planned review; open-ended therapy is discouraged.
- The **goals are comfort with sexuality, trust, emotional security, and intimacy, not direct symptom reversal**; assuming intact physiology, function returns when these are achieved. Exceptions with specific behavioural techniques: premature ejaculation and vaginismus. Cognitive-behavioural and psychodynamic techniques are woven in as the tasks expose conflicts.

**Pharmacological treatment: men.**
- **PDE-5 inhibitors** are first-line for erectile difficulty: sildenafil 25–100 mg about an hour before activity (effect a few hours), tadalafil (≥30 min before; long half-life, effect up to 24–36 h), vardenafil 5–20 mg. Overall effectiveness around **75%**. Dose-related side effects: headache, flushing, dyspepsia. **Absolute contraindication: nitrates** (ischaemic heart disease); the interaction can cause severe hypotension. Honest expectation-setting: a multinational study found only **16%** of identified men maintained PDE-5 inhibitor use, drop-out reflects poor information, side-effect fear, partner concerns and distrust, not just drug failure. Tell couples the drug *assists* response but does not "cure"; the problem usually returns when it stops.
- **Premature ejaculation**: SSRIs (paroxetine, sertraline, fluoxetine) exploit the orgasm-delaying effect; continued dosing needed, benefit may lag the first week, and avoid abrupt withdrawal. *(Modern update: short-acting dapoxetine is now the on-demand SSRI developed specifically for PE, flagged as post-Oxford.)* PDE-5 inhibitors have unclear benefit, possibly confined to secondary PE.
- **Delayed ejaculation**: no accepted pharmacological treatment, a sobering exam fact.
- **Low desire**: treat the treatable; testosterone replacement for confirmed hypogonadism (transdermal routes give steadier, more physiological levels than injections); dopamine agonists (e.g. bromocriptine) for hyperprolactinaemia.

**Pharmacological treatment: women.** Genuinely limited (an honesty the chapter states plainly). Testosterone shows the best evidence in **ovariectomized** women, but treated levels often become supraphysiological, long-term risks are unknown, and regulatory reluctance persists for women with intact ovaries. PDE-5 inhibitors have been unsuccessful overall, though subgroups may benefit; apomorphine, phentolamine and bupropion remain exploratory. *(Modern update: flibanserin (2015) and bremelanotide (2019) were later approved in the USA for premenopausal low desire, modest effect sizes, flagged as post-Oxford.)* The realistic core of women's treatment remains the couple/psychosexual approach plus treating depression and reviewing medication.

**Combining the two.** Studies combining psychotherapy or counselling with sildenafil, intracavernosal injections or vacuum devices consistently beat medical treatment alone. The practical algorithm: sex therapy first (diagnostic + therapeutic), add medication where indicated *within* the continuing therapy programme, then attempt gradual withdrawal or intermittent use of the drug as the relationship improves.

**Drug-induced dysfunction: the psychiatrist's daily version.** For SSRI-induced dysfunction: wait (tolerance is possible), dose-reduce, switch (bupropion, nefazodone carry fewer sexual effects), schedule doses before "drug holidays" only with careful risk-benefit thought, or add an antidote strategy used in practice, remembering the chapter's caution that on-demand short-acting SSRIs exist for PE precisely because of this effect. For antipsychotic-induced dysfunction: check prolactin (hyperprolactinaemia from dopamine blockade), consider prolactin-sparing options, and never let the patient silently stop the antipsychotic. *India note: ask about it explicitly, patients almost never volunteer sexual side effects, and silent non-adherence is the price of not asking.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **The presenting doors differ.** Consummation failure and vaginismus arrive via gynaecology and infertility clinics; premature ejaculation via urology, unlicensed "sex clinics" and over-the-counter pharmacy requests; desire problems via marital discord, "weakness" and postpartum complaints; drug-induced dysfunction via psychiatry. Asking one respectful, routine sexual question in every relevant consultation opens the door wider than any poster.
- **Language and permission.** Patients respond to plain, matter-of-fact vocabulary and permission-giving ("many couples face this; it is common and treatable") far better than to anatomical lectures. Withhold the smile, the embarrassment and the moral commentary; the consultation's tone is the intervention.
- **Confidentiality carries extra weight.** Couples may not have discussed sex with each other, let alone a doctor; families accompany patients to OPD and may ask questions. Use a private moment for the sexual history; agree explicitly what goes into notes and referral letters. The chapter's advice: separate secure files for sexual-problem clinics; translates into Indian practice as: keep sexual-detail notes out of the general file relatives may handle.
- **Cultural negotiation, not cultural imposition.** Much of sex therapy "gives permission" for interaction patterns drawn from Western middle-class values (self-assertion, mutual initiation). With Indian couples (arranged or love marriages, joint-family households with thin privacy, religious frameworks governing sex) the chapter's instruction applies literally: surface and negotiate differing values; do not impose the therapist's.
- **Practical resource limits.** Formal sex therapists are scarce; the psychiatrist who is comfortable asking sexual questions, works psychotherapeutically with couples and knows CBT can deliver this programme; with the advantage of being able to prescribe. Referral to urology/gynaecology for physical disease, or to the few experienced therapists, otherwise.
- **Costs (approx 2026).** Sildenafil is generic and inexpensive in India (rupee-range per tablet for standard doses, brand-dependent); tadalafil similarly. Testosterone replacement and prolactin assays are available in most cities. The "sex-clinic" underground economy (unregulated injections, tonics and hormonal cocktails) is the expensive and dangerous default when the formal system stays silent.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: The SSRIs saved her mood and emptied her marriage.** A 31-year-old teacher on sertraline for panic disorder returns "for a stronger tablet"; the referral is actually her husband's, via the local doctor, for "no interest". Separate interviews reveal: her panic is fully controlled; she has had almost no sexual interest since dose escalation a year ago; orgasm has become rare; she has told no one, and their physical relationship has quietly stopped. Husband, seen alone, believes she "no longer loves him" and is considering an affair. Management: explain the SSRI-sexual effect openly to both (diagnosis alone repairs the marriage attribution), reduce the sertraline where panic control allows, consider switching to bupropion-based management, book the couple for four sex-therapy sessions re-establishing non-genital intimacy first, and review. Teaching points: sexual side effects are invisible unless asked; the couple heard "she doesn't love me" until physiology was named; never let the drug silently take the blame for the marriage or the marriage for the drug.

**Case 2: Consummation failure at the infertility clinic.** A 26-year-old woman and 29-year-old man, married ten months, referred from an infertility clinic: "no children". Private history reveals intercourse has never been achieved; she experiences burning pain and "closing up" at any attempt; examination shows marked pelvic-floor guarding with an intact hymenal ring; he has developed erectile difficulty that is clearly situational (normal morning erections). This is genito-pelvic pain/penetration disorder (previously vaginismus/dyspareunia) with his secondary erectile difficulty layered on top. Management: gynaecological examination to exclude vestibulitis and local pathology, then the graduated programme (non-genital touch, then genital touch, then gradual insertion with her controlling pace) combined with CBT for catastrophised pain and a ban on "scheduled performance attempts" at home. Teaching points: normal morning erections prove the vasculature and locate his problem as situational; the couple, not the woman, is the unit of treatment; in Indian practice this presentation hides behind infertility referrals and gynaecological repeats.

## 13. FAQ

**"How common is this, doctor, are we the only ones?"** Very common. More than half of women and a third of men report at least one problem in the past year; most are brief. Persistent, distressing problems, the ones worth treating, affect roughly 15% of women and 6% of men. You are not the only ones by a very long way.

**"Is my problem physical or psychological?"** Usually the question is wrong; it is both, interacting. The better questions: is desire present (if yes, the wiring usually is)? Are morning erections normal (if yes, the vasculature is)? Is it situational (with one partner or circumstance but not others)? What else is happening in the body, the relationship, and the medicine cabinet?

**"Will a tablet fix it?"** For erectile difficulty, PDE-5 inhibitors work in about three-quarters of men, but only assist an existing response; desire and the relationship remain yours to repair. For desire and orgasm problems in women there is still no reliable tablet. Tablets work best *combined* with therapy, and evidence shows the drug can later be tapered.

**"Is it my hormones?"** Sometimes: checking testosterone and prolactin is worthwhile for low desire, and diabetes for erectile difficulty. But most low desire is not hormonal, and hormonal treatment in women remains uncertain territory.

**"My antidepressant caused this, should I stop it?"** No, not without a plan. Options include dose adjustment, switching to bupropion or another sexuality-sparing drug, or treating the couple problem the side effect created. Silently stopping is how relapse happens.

**"We have never been able to have intercourse since marriage. Is there hope?"** Yes: this pattern (pain/tightening plus his situational erection difficulty) responds well to a graded programme plus pain-focused CBT; most couples achieve intercourse within months of proper treatment.

**"Is asking about sex awkward for you, doctor?"** It is a medical question like any other. The consultation's matter-of-factness is itself part of the treatment.

**"Can this be discussed without my family knowing?"** Yes. What goes in the notes, and what is written back to any referring doctor, will be agreed with you first, and each partner controls what is shared in joint sessions.

**"Does masturbation count as cheating / harm?"** Masturbation is a normal behaviour and a diagnostic clue (orgasm with masturbation but not with a partner points to situational, not bodily, causes). Moral frameworks belong to the couple to negotiate; the clinic's job is accurate information without judgement.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **The duration pair (high-yield):** 1-month problems ≈ 54% women / 35% men; 6-month problems ≈ 15.6% / 6.2%: "common problem, uncommon dysfunction".
- **Age facts:** complete ED 5% at 40 → 25% at 70; absent desire 2% (45–59) → 18.2% (75+); PE does *not* increase with age, a classic trap.
- **Dual Control Model:** inhibition propensity is heritable (in men); high inhibition → dysfunction; low → risk behaviour; "arousal contingency" in women.
- **The three windows:** situation / vulnerability / health-medication, reproduce the aetiology answer from them rather than memorising lists.
- **Drug effects:** SSRIs → orgasm/ejaculation delay (both sexes; exploited as PE treatment); antipsychotics → ~60% of men, 30–90% of women; beta-blockers and clonidine → erectile problems; bupropion and nefazodone → relatively spared.
- **PDE-5 essentials:** sildenafil ~1 h before (hours of effect), tadalafil up to 24–36 h; efficacy ~75%; nitrate contraindication is absolute; only 16% maintain long-term use.
- **Delayed ejaculation has no accepted pharmacological treatment**, a favourite one-liner in exams.
- **PGAD**, genital arousal without desire, no male equivalent (refractory period), recent, distinctive, exam-worthy.
- **Classification updates:** DSM-5's FSIAD and genito-pelvic pain/penetration disorder merges; PE ≈ 1 minute (ISSM): cite as modern updates to the 2009 text.
- **Indian exam corner:** Dhat syndrome; consummation-failure-via-infertility-clinic route; ask explicitly about SSRI sexual effects (silent non-adherence); prolactin check in antipsychotic-treated low desire.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**Q1.** A 29-year-old man on paroxetine for panic disorder reports gradual loss of ability to reach orgasm over two months, with preserved desire and erections. The most likely mechanism is:
- (a) Testosterone suppression by the SSRI
- (b) Serotonergic inhibition of orgasm triggering
- (c) Vascular impairment from panic-related hypertension
- (d) Prolactin elevation from the SSRI

**Answer: (b).** SSRIs predictably inhibit orgasm and ejaculation in both sexes; the effect exploited to treat premature ejaculation.

**Q2.** Which prevalence statement best reflects the NATSAL (Mercer 2003) duration findings?
- (a) About 55% of women and 35% of men report a sexual problem lasting at least 1 month in the past year; 6-month problems fall to ~15.6% and ~6.2%
- (b) Sexual dysfunction is rare, affecting under 5% of both sexes
- (c) Problems lasting 1 month and 6 months have near-identical prevalence
- (d) Men report more persistent problems than women

**Answer: (a).** Transient problems are extremely common; the clinically meaningful "dysfunction" population is the persistent minority.

**Q3.** A 55-year-old man with angina on isosorbide mononitrate asks for sildenafil for erectile difficulty. The correct response is:
- (a) Prescribe sildenafil 100 mg with nitrate instructions
- (b) Refuse PDE-5 inhibitors: nitrate co-use is an absolute contraindication
- (c) Substitute yohimbine at full dose
- (d) Advise intracavernosal papaverine as equally safe with nitrates

**Answer: (b).** PDE-5 inhibition plus nitrates produces dangerous hypotension through the shared cGMP pathway; the combination is strongly contraindicated.

**Q4.** During the three-part behavioural programme of sex therapy, long-standing negative sexual attitudes and sequelae of earlier sexual trauma most characteristically surface during:
- (a) Part 1: non-genital touching
- (b) Part 2: combined genital and non-genital touching with intercourse banned
- (c) Part 3: gradual approach to penetration
- (d) The termination sessions only

**Answer: (b).** Part 1 surfaces relationship material (trust, resentment); Part 2 intrapersonal material; Part 3 performance anxiety and fear of pain.

**Q5.** In women, distress about sexuality is best predicted by:
- (a) Vaginal lubrication speed
- (b) Frequency of orgasm
- (c) Mental health and the quality of the emotional relationship with the partner
- (d) Serum testosterone level

**Answer: (c).** Bancroft's survey found physical response markers were poor predictors of women's sexual distress; mood and emotional relationship quality dominated.

**Q6.** Which cause of low sexual desire in a man is both clearly treatable and specifically endocrine?
- (a) Resentment in the marriage
- (b) Hyperprolactinaemia from a pituitary adenoma
- (c) SSRI-induced dopamine blockade
- (d) Performance anxiety

**Answer: (b).** Prolactin-secreting adenomas cause loss of interest and erectile difficulty and respond to dopamine agonists; prolactin is the assay not to miss.
