# Depressive disorders

## 1. In one line + overview

**Tagline:** Depression is a common, treatable medical illness of mood and energy; far more than sadness, it changes sleep, appetite, thinking and hope for weeks at a time.

**Overview, in plain words:** Around **3–4% of the world's adults** live with depression at any time (about 280 million people), and in India roughly **1 in 40 adults in a year** by survey estimates, with the true number probably higher because most people never reach a psychiatrist. Untreated, an episode typically drags on for 6–12 months and returns in over half of cases; treated properly, most people recover, and the right maintenance plan keeps them well. Depression is a leading cause of disability worldwide precisely because it hits working-age adults and is so often missed. In India it frequently wears a disguise (body aches, "weakness", burning sensations, poor appetite) so the mood question never gets asked. The most important safety fact: depression is the strongest individual risk factor for suicide, and risk peaks both before treatment and early in recovery, when energy returns faster than hope.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:

1. Define depression as an illness, not a weakness, and say it to a patient in one convincing sentence.
2. Recognise the **Indian somatic presentation** and use a two-question screen that changes your hit rate.
3. State the DSM-5 logic (two weeks, five symptoms, one core) and how melancholic and atypical features change treatment choices.
4. Explain why you **must screen for past mania before starting an antidepressant**.
5. Use PHQ-9 bands for severity and tracking (it is public domain).
6. Choose a first-line SSRI, dose it, set expectations (2–4 weeks to benefit, 6–12 months minimum duration), and manage the common side effects.
7. List the situations where ECT becomes the best, and fastest, option.

## 4. Epidemiology

### Global

- Twelve-month prevalence about **3–5%**; lifetime risk **10–15%**, roughly **twice as common in women** as men.
- The WHO ranks depression among the **leading causes of disability worldwide**; over 700,000 people die by suicide each year, and depression is implicated in a large share.
- Peak onset in the **20s–40s**: exactly the working, child-rearing, earning years.

### India

- The National Mental Health Survey 2015–16 estimated about **2.7% of adults** with a current depressive episode, with nearly **85% of common mental disorders receiving no care**; the treatment gap is the defining Indian statistic.
- Women are affected 2–3 times more than men; risk clusters in perimenopausal women, the elderly living alone or in nuclear families, new mothers (postpartum depression affects roughly **1 in 6 to 1 in 8** Indian mothers) and people with chronic illness or heavy caregiving loads.
- Somatic presentation is the rule rather than the exception in primary care: fatigue, body ache, burning sensations, "gas", poor appetite; patients report the body, not the mood.
- Suicide is a leading cause of death in young Indians; the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 **decriminalised attempted suicide** and presumes severe stress; a fact every clinician should know by heart.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

Depression is the final common pathway of many roads, genes load the gun, stress pulls the trigger, and body states tune the sensitivity.

**Genetic (tag: genetic):**
- Heritability about **35–40%**; first-degree relatives carry 2–3× the risk. Polygenic, like most psychiatric conditions.
- The famous gene-environment interaction (serotonin-transporter gene × childhood stress) is now known to be weaker than first reported; teach it as a lesson in scientific humility.

**Biological (tag: biological):**
- **Check-and-treat mimics: hypothyroidism, B12 and vitamin D deficiency, anaemia, diabetes, hypogonadism.** In India, B12 deficiency in vegetarians is the classic reversibility miss.
- Stroke, Parkinson's disease, hypothyroidism, cancer, CKD and chronic pain all raise depression rates several-fold.
- Medicines and substances: steroids, isotretinoin, interferon, some anti-tuberculosis regimens (cycloserine), and alcohol (both a cause and a self-treatment attempt, always ask).

**Psychological (tag: psychological):**
- Beck's **negative cognitive triad**: views of self, world and future all negative; deep schemas ("I am not good enough") drive automatic negative thoughts.
- **Rumination** (chewing the same dark thoughts) predicts longer episodes; **learned helplessness** explains the "nothing I do matters" state.
- Adverse childhood experiences show a dose-response with adult depression; bullying, loss of a parent early, harsh criticism.

**Social (tag: social):**
- Unemployment and debt, social isolation, intimate-partner violence, caregiving overload, exam and workplace pressure.
- In Indian youth: academic pressure with few alternatives; in women: role overload plus low autonomy; in the elderly: migration of children to nuclear arrangements abroad or to metros.

**Environmental (tag: environmental):**
- Chronic sleep loss and shift work; chronic pain; post-viral states; seasonal light variation (mild relevance in India compared with high latitudes).

**Indian context (tag: indian-context):**
- Somatic idiom of distress → misdiagnosis cascade: months of tonics, vitamins and "liver" pills before the mood question is asked.
- Alcohol in men frequently masks and mirrors depression; screen both together.
- Postpartum depression hides behind "she is just adjusting" and the short window of "baby blues"; screen at the 6-week immunisation visit.
- The stigma script ("log kya kahenge"; what will people say) delays help-seeking by years; the first consultation is often a family negotiation.

## 6. What happens in the brain

Three stories, honestly told.

**Story 1: the chemistry story (partly true, beautifully simple).** Medicines that raised serotonin or noradrenaline were found (by accident, in the 1950s–60s) to lift mood, hence "chemical imbalance". But transmitters rise within *hours* of the first tablet while mood takes *weeks*: something downstream must matter. That something is the second story.

**Story 2: the plasticity story (the current one).** Chronic stress floods the brain with cortisol, shrinks the hippocampus (memory region) and blunts connections in the prefrontal cortex. Antidepressants, therapy, exercise and sleep all **re-trigger neuroplasticity**; the brain relearns that the world responds to action. Recovery takes weeks because rewiring takes weeks. This is also why "just think positive" fails but behaviour change works.

**Story 3: the body story (inflammation + HPA axis).** Inflammation and stress hormones produce a "sickness state" (low energy, low appetite, social withdrawal) that looks and feels exactly like depression. It explains the physical symptoms Indian patients present with, and why exercise, sleep and treating physical illness are genuine antidepressant interventions, not lifestyle garnish.

**Clinical translation:** melancholic features (see next section) signal the deeper "shut-down" state that responds preferentially to biological treatment; the milder, reactive pattern responds beautifully to behavioural and psychological treatment. Both stories are true; match the treatment to the pattern.

## 7. Symptoms

### The two core symptoms (one must be present)

1. **Low mood** most of the day, nearly every day, in Indian clinics often described as "mann nahi lagta" (the heart is not in anything) or heaviness rather than "sadness".
2. **Anhedonia**: loss of interest or pleasure in things previously enjoyed.

### The supporting symptoms: the SIGECAPS way students learn them

**S**leep changes (early waking, or oversleeping) · **I**nterest lost · **G**uilt or worthlessness · **E**nergy low · **C**oncentration poor ("gashti chal gayi"; the mind wanders) · **A**ppetite/weight change · **P**sychomotor slowing or agitation · **S**uicidal thoughts.

Cognitive symptoms deserve respect: guilt, worthlessness, memory complaints (in the elderly, depression can mimic dementia, "pseudodementia", and it reverses with treatment). Always ask the suicide question directly and early: "Do you ever feel life is not worth living? Do you have thoughts of ending it?"

### Special patterns that change treatment

| Pattern | Recognise it by | Why it matters |
|---------|-----------------|----------------|
| **Melancholic** | profound anhedonia, early-morning waking, worse mood in the morning, weight loss, severe slowing or agitation, disproportionate guilt | responds preferentially to antidepressants and ECT |
| **Atypical** | oversleeping, overeating, leaden-heavy limbs, intense rejection sensitivity | common in younger onset; responds well to medication |
| **Psychotic** | nihilistic delusions ("my insides have rotted"), guilt-laden or poverty delusions, occasionally smell hallucinations | ECT or antidepressant + antipsychotic; never SSRI alone |
| **Peripartum** | onset in pregnancy or within weeks after delivery; intrusive harm thoughts about the baby | treat actively; sertraline is the usual first choice in breastfeeding |
| **Anxious distress** | tension, restlessness, racing worry | predicts poorer outcomes; treat the anxiety component too |

### Course, untreated

A typical untreated episode lasts **6–13 months**; after one episode, about 50% have another; after three, the risk of further episodes exceeds 70%; the arithmetic behind "treat the episode, then protect the person."

## 8. Diagnosis

**The screen that changes practice (2 minutes, any clinic):** *Over the last 2 weeks, have you often felt down or hopeless? Have you lost interest or pleasure in things?* Two "yes" answers make depression a live possibility; then the fuller picture follows.

### DSM-5 logic (paraphrased, not quoted)

At least **five symptoms** from the list above (the two-question screen plus SIGECAPS set), present nearly every day for **at least two weeks**, with at least one being low mood or anhedonia, plus real distress or functional impairment, and not better explained by another condition. Dysthymia's modern name, **persistent depressive disorder**, needs the gloom present more days than not for **two years**.

### ICD logic

ICD-10 counted symptoms and graded mild/moderate/severe (that vocabulary still rules Indian exams); ICD-11 keeps the grading but aligns more with DSM-5. Same illness, different bookkeeping.

### Severity: measuring it

**PHQ-9** is public domain, translated and validated in Indian languages, and earns its place: nine quick questions, scored 0–27, with bands **5 / 10 / 15 / 20** for mild / moderate / moderately-severe / severe. Repeat it at each review: it turns "I think she's better" into data. (Named and described, not reproduced: download the official version for clinical use.) For new mothers, the **Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS)** is the standard choice.

### Before you commit to "depression"

- **Screen every patient for past mania**, even one distinct period of elevated/irritable mood lasting 4+ days with increased energy changes the whole plan (see the bipolar note). The classic litigation nightmare is an SSRI unmasking mania.
- **Bloods:** CBC, TSH, B12, vitamin D, fasting glucose. In women with irregular cycles, consider pregnancy and thyroid.
- **Substances:** alcohol, cannabis, and current medicines (steroids, isotretinoin, anti-TB drugs).
- **Collateral from family**: patients routinely minimise.
- Elderly first episode: cognitive screen, vascular history, and a low threshold to investigate; late-life depression can be the first chapter of a neurological illness.

### Differential diagnosis: the five that bite

| Mimic | The separating clue |
|-------|--------------------|
| **Bipolar depression** | any past hypomania/mania; family history of bipolar; early onset; atypical/melancholic mix |
| Hypothyroidism / B12 deficiency / anaemia | fatigue-dominant, physical signs, bloods confirm; both can also co-exist |
| Grief / adjustment | tied to a loss; waves of pain with preserved self-worth; guilt is about the dead, not about being worthless |
| Substance-induced (esp. alcohol) | intoxication/withdrawal pattern; mood tracks use |
| Early dementia (elderly) | progressive cognitive decline without prominent mood language; depression tests improve when treated, then re-test over time |

## 9. Management

### The stepped plan in one box

**Mild:** guided behavioural activation or self-help + exercise + review in 2 weeks (medicines are not the first step). **Moderate:** psychotherapy **and/or** an SSRI. **Severe / psychotic / melancholic with weight loss:** antidepressant (± antipsychotic), consider **ECT** early rather than late. Every tier: treat the mimics (thyroid, B12), treat sleep, remove alcohol, and answer the suicide question with a plan.

### Lifestyle (real medicine in this illness)

- **Exercise: 30–45 minutes of brisk activity, most days**; in mild-to-moderate depression the effect size is comparable to antidepressants. Prescribe it like a tablet: type, dose, timing.
- Regular sleep and wake times (sleep is both symptom and fuel); morning sunlight; **behavioural activation**: the counter-intuitive rule "action comes before motivation", schedule small doable activities and let mood follow.
- Alcohol out (it is a depressant and a sleep destroyer); caffeine after noon out.

> *India note (lifestyle):* walking is free and universal; yoga has supportive Indian trial evidence as an add-on; group morning-walk circuits double as social activation, an underused prescription in Indian housing societies.

### Psychotherapy

- **CBT**: restructures the automatic negative thoughts; 8–16 sessions; maintenance CBT cuts relapse after recovery.
- **Behavioural activation (BA)**: simpler, cheaper, deliverable by trained non-specialists; trials show it matches full CBT. For a country with a therapy shortage, this is the most important sentence in the section.
- **IPT** (interpersonal therapy): targets role transitions and conflicts; strong evidence for peripartum and young-adult depression.
- **Problem-solving therapy**: the primary-care workhorse; 4–6 sessions.

> *India note (therapy):* India's flagship contribution here is **task-sharing**: the Goa-based trials (Patel and colleagues) showed trained lay counsellors delivering BA and problem-solving in primary care produced real recovery, evidence the WHO built mhGAP on. Tele-MANAS (14416) offers counselling nationally; well-trained private therapists remain metro-concentrated (₹500–2,000/session).

### Pharmacotherapy

**First-line SSRIs and how to choose among them:**

| Drug | Typical start → target | Personality of the drug |
|------|------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Sertraline** | 25–50 mg → 100–200 mg | the default: cheap, well-tolerated, few interactions, best overall balance in the largest network meta-analysis |
| **Escitalopram** | 5–10 mg → 10–20 mg | best-tolerated SSRI; costlier generics |
| **Fluoxetine** | 20 mg → 20–60 mg | activating; week-long half-life makes missed doses forgiving; approved down to age 8 |
| Paroxetine | 20 mg → 20–40 mg | most sedating and anticholinergic; worst discontinuation syndrome: avoid in elderly |
| Fluvoxamine | 50 → 100–300 mg | mostly an OCD drug; multiple interactions |

**Other classes, briefly:** SNRIs (venlafaxine XR, duloxetine, helpful when chronic pain co-exists); **mirtazapine** (sedating and appetite-boosting: the choice for the thin, sleepless, elderly patient); bupropion (energising, no sexual side effects, but lowers seizure threshold); **TCAs** (amitriptyline, imipramine: effective and the cheapest tablets in India, but cardiotoxic in overdose: never the first choice where overdose risk exists, and ECG in the elderly).

**The rules that matter:**
1. **Start low, go slow, expect 2–4 weeks** (sleep and appetite improve first, mood lags); judge the trial only after **6–8 weeks at a therapeutic dose**.
2. **Continue 6–12 months after full recovery** (first episode), 2–3 years after the second, consider indefinite after three-plus episodes.
3. Under age 25: closer monitoring in the first weeks for activation and new suicidal thoughts; the illness itself carries far more suicide risk than the medicine, so monitor, do not withhold.
4. Know the practical side-effect set: **sexual dysfunction** (up to a third; warn upfront, it is the main hidden reason people stop), nausea in week one, headache, early **hyponatraemia in the elderly** (new confusion/lethargy/falls → check sodium), bleeding risk with NSAIDs/anticoagulants, and citalopram dose limits for QT prolongation.
5. **Discontinuation syndrome** (dizziness, "electric-zap" sensations, nausea, insomnia; the FINISH set) after abrupt stop, worst with paroxetine, mildest with fluoxetine: educate patients so they do not mistake it for relapse or "addiction".
6. **Serotonin syndrome** (agitation, sweating, clonus, fever, usually from combinations like SSRI + tramadol or + linezolid): stop the drug, cool the patient, consider cyproheptadine; severe cases are an ICU event.
7. Non-response: verify adherence and duration → optimise dose → switch (within-class or cross-class, both defensible) → **augment** (lithium, an atypical antipsychotic such as aripiprazole, or thyroid hormone, the classic trio).

> *India note (medicines):* generic sertraline and escitalopram cost **₹60–200/month**, fluoxetine less; amitriptyline is the cheapest option in every PHC formulary. Pharmacies sell these without enforced prescriptions, so the counselling about *duration* (minimum 6–9 months after recovery) matters more in India than in regulated systems. Detailed costs in section 10.

### Brain stimulation

- **ECT**: the fastest and most effective option we have for psychotic depression, melancholic depression with refusal of food, high imminent suicide risk, severe depression in pregnancy, and failed drug trials. A course is 6–12 sessions; in India it is cheap, available in district hospitals, and still feared because of cinema images. Consent under MHA 2017; anaesthesia and muscle relaxation always.
- **rTMS**: daily sessions over 4–6 weeks; useful for resistant or medicine-intolerant depression; metro centres only.
- **Esketamine** (rapid-acting, for resistant depression) exists but is expensive and scarce in India; know its name, do not expect it in a district hospital.

### Other

Lithium augmentation (monitor levels, thyroid, renal function); light therapy for the seasonal pattern; mindfulness-based cognitive therapy for relapse prevention; treating the inseparables: anxiety, alcohol, chronic pain, and the medical illnesses (diabetes, heart disease) that depression both follows and worsens.

## 10. Indian practice

### Which guidelines are actually followed?

No India-specific national guideline. Urban specialists follow **NICE NG222 (2022)** and APA guidance; primary care follows the **WHO mhGAP** depression module, which is beautifully suited to Indian conditions: psychological first-aid plus behavioural activation, add an SSRI only when moderate-severe, review in two weeks. NIMHANS digital resources support the same logic.

### The government pathway

ASHA/ANM-level screening during home visits and village outreach → PHC medical officer (two-question screen, start treatment for moderate-severe cases, activate families) → district hospital psychiatry OPD under **DMHP** (medicines free or near-free; referral hub) → medical college and tertiary centres (rTMS, ECT, treatment resistance). **Tele-MANAS (14416)** provides national counselling; the **Kiran helpline (1800-599-0019)** is the crisis line. Postpartum screening rides on the 6-week immunisation contact, the single most reachable mental-health touchpoint for Indian mothers.

### Private practice reality

Consultations ₹300–1,000; therapy ₹500–2,000 per session in metros (and a genuine scarcity of CBT-trained therapists outside them). Insurance: since **IRDAI's 2024 directive**, mental illness must be covered at parity with physical illness; again, check outpatient pharmacy cover, which is where the actual cost of depression sits.

### Primary care: the make-or-break tier

The PHC doctor's depression kit is five items: the **two-question screen**, a **suicide question**, the **PHQ-9** for tracking, a **starter prescription** (sertraline 25–50 mg; half-dose start in the anxious and the elderly), and a **two-week review**. Behavioural activation delivered by a counsellor or even a trained ASHA completes the package. Red flags for referral: psychotic features, active suicidal intent, suspected bipolarity, two failed adequate trials, pregnancy, or a confusing medical picture.

### Patient and family education: the real medicine

Four sentences every family should hear: **(1)** depression is an illness, like thyroid disease, not a character defect, and it is treatable. **(2)** The tablets are not addictive, and they take 2–4 weeks to start working. **(3)** Even after feeling normal, the medicine continues for at least 6–9 months; that is what prevents the next episode. **(4)** Alcohol and irregular sleep are quietly feeding the illness; walk daily, with someone, in daylight.

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

### Case 1: postpartum depression hiding behind "weakness"

Meena (name changed), 26, second delivery six months ago, joint family in Madhya Pradesh. Brought to the PHC for "weakness" and "no interest in food". On examination: thin, slow-speaking; the baby's immunisations were overdue. Asked directly, she wept: sleep was broken even when the baby slept, she had stopped cooking, and she believed her milk was "poisoned and will harm the baby". PHQ-9 score 21 (severe). The husband's phone call confirmed three months of this. Diagnosis: major depression with peripartum onset and intrusive harm thoughts (not commands). Plan: same-week review at district psychiatry OPD; started sertraline 50 mg (chosen for breastfeeding safety), titrated to 100 mg; mother-in-law counselled in one session to share night care; behavioural activation plan of two scheduled activities daily. By week 8 she was sleeping, cooking and had completed the immunisation schedule.

**Teaching points:** postpartum depression is not "baby blues" (which is brief and self-limiting); intrusive harm thoughts are common and respond to treatment, assess the risk calmly, not with panic; and counselling one family member to share the night shift is as therapeutic as it is underrated.

### Case 2: "dementia" that was depression

Krishnan (name changed), 68, retired schoolteacher in Kerala, brought by his son for "memory loss; he keeps repeating questions and cannot manage his medicines". Six months of declining function: stopped morning walks, 5 kg weight loss, waking at 4 a.m., and voiced guilt about "having cheated students 40 years ago". MMSE-type screening was poor, but answers were "don't know" rather than wrong; effort was poor, not knowledge. Bloods: B12 borderline-low, TSH mildly raised, corrected both. PHQ-9 score 23. Diagnosis: severe depression with melancholic and psychotic-adjacent features presenting as pseudodementia. Started sertraline titrated to 150 mg with mirtazapine 15 mg for sleep and appetite; family coached on warm, low-pressure interaction. Cognitive testing normalised at 3 months; walks resumed.

**Teaching points:** in elderly "dementia", depression is the great mimic; "I don't know" answers, morning-worse pattern, and prominent guilt argue for mood illness; treat and re-test cognition later (late-life depression also modestly raises later dementia risk; be honest about follow-up); and the B12/thyroid panel is mandatory, not optional, in India.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way patients ask it

**Am I just weak? Everyone has stress.**
Stress and sadness pass; depression stays for weeks and takes sleep, appetite, energy and hope with it. It is a medical condition with measurable brain and body changes, and like any illness, it deserves treatment, not blame.

**Is it a "chemical imbalance" that my tablet will fix?**
The old slogan is an oversimplification. Stress chemistry, inflammation and stalled brain-plasticity all contribute. Medicines restart the brain's ability to adapt, which is why they take weeks, and why therapy and exercise work alongside them.

**How long will I need the tablets?**
At least 6–9 months after you feel completely normal (first episode). Stopping earlier quietly invites the next episode. Longer after repeated episodes: decided with your doctor, never abruptly on your own.

**Are antidepressants addictive?**
No. No craving, no tolerance, no "high". Stopping suddenly can cause dizziness and flu-like withdrawal sensations (that is the medicine leaving, not addiction) which is why tapering is planned.

**Will it affect my sexual life?**
Some antidepressants reduce desire or delay orgasm in up to a third of users. It is manageable (dose adjustment, timing changes or switching drugs) but only if you tell your doctor. Silently stopping the tablet is the commonest outcome of not saying it.

**I feel better after two weeks of yoga. Do I still need medicine?**
Mild depression often responds to exercise, routine and counselling alone, genuinely. Moderate-to-severe depression does better with medicines plus those measures. Get a proper severity assessment before deciding.

**Will my children develop depression?**
Risk to first-degree relatives is 2–3 times the average, which still means most relatives never develop it. Prevention works: sleep, exercise, treating early symptoms, and reducing chronic stress at home.

**Do I have to tell my employer or my in-laws?**
Disclosure is your decision, not your duty. What matters clinically is having one or two supportive people who know. A doctor's letter for leave or adjusted duties needs no diagnosis details.

**Is ECT used for depression? Is it for "mad" people only?**
ECT is a refined, anaesthetised medical procedure, and for severe, psychotic or life-threatening depression it is the *fastest and most effective* treatment we have. Memory wobbles are usually temporary. Cinema did to ECT what cinema once did to sharks.

**Can I take it during pregnancy or breastfeeding?**
Yes, when needed: untreated depression harms mother and baby too. Sertraline is the usual first choice with the best safety data in pregnancy and lactation. The decision balances severity, alternatives and close follow-up, never fear alone; discuss with your obstetrician and psychiatrist together.

**Will alcohol help me sleep?**
It sedates for two hours, then fragments sleep and deepens depression. It is the most popular antidepressant in India and the least effective.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**Mnemonics:**
- **SIGECAPS**: Sleep, Interest, Guilt, Energy, Concentration, Appetite, Psychomotor, Suicidality (the DSM symptom set).
- Melancholic features: skip the mnemonic, learn the examiners' favourites: *early-morning waking, mood worse in the morning, weight loss, profound anhedonia, disproportionate guilt, psychomotor change*.
- Discontinuation syndrome, **FINISH**: Flu-like symptoms, Insomnia, Nausea, Imbalance, Sensory "zaps", Hyperarousal.

**High-yield facts:**
- DSM-5: 5 of 9 symptoms, ≥2 weeks, one must be mood or anhedonia. Persistent depressive disorder = 2 years.
- **Always screen for mania before an SSRI**, bipolar depression treated with an antidepressant alone risks a switch; the viva favourite.
- PHQ-9 bands: 5 mild, 10 moderate, 15 moderately severe, 20 severe, public domain, free to use.
- Cipriani's Lancet 2016 network meta-analysis (21 drugs, 500+ trials): all antidepressants beat placebo; **sertraline and escitalopram offered the best overall balance**, the source of the sertraline default.
- STAR*D: remission ~1/3 on the first step, ~2/3 cumulative over stepped steps; counsel persistence at the first review.
- Tricyclic overdose: wide-QRS on ECG, treat with sodium bicarbonate, a perennial short-question.
- Serotonin syndrome = serotonergic drug + **clonus** + hyperthermia (Hunter logic); treatment: stop, cool, cyproheptadine.
- SSRI cautions: hyponatraemia in the elderly, bleeding with NSAIDs, citalopram QT limit, under-25 early monitoring.
- ECT indications exam list: severe suicidal, psychotic, refusal of food, pregnancy (severe), treatment resistance, plus "catatonia" for the schizophrenia/mood overlap question.
- Indian exam corner: MHA 2017 decriminalised attempted suicide (presumption of severe stress); tele-MANAS 14416; Kiran 1800-599-0019; NMHS 2015–16 treatment gap ~85% for common mental disorders.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1.** A 32-year-old woman reports two weeks of pervasive low mood after a job loss: sleep 4 hours, 3 kg weight loss, fatigue, self-blame "I ruined everything", occasional death wishes. Best first step per stepped care:
A. Start imipramine B. Guided activation/exercise + review in 2 weeks C. ECT D. Start lithium
**Answer: B.** Moderate-severity pattern of a first, trigger-linked episode: watch-and-activate is guideline-concordant (though therapy ± SSRI is equally defensible if symptoms persist; ECT/lithium are far beyond the picture). The exam point is that mild-to-moderate first episodes do not open with the heavy artillery.

**2.** Elderly man on sertraline for 3 weeks develops new confusion and lethargy; sodium 124 mmol/L. Diagnosis and next step:
A. Dementia: refer for CT B. SSRI-induced hyponatraemia: hold the drug and recheck sodium C. Depression worsening: increase dose D. Normal ageing: reassure
**Answer: B.** SSRI hyponatraemia in the elderly is a must-know; it emerges early, mimics worsening, and the wrong move is dose escalation.

**3.** Patient's relative mentions a past episode of "4 days of feeling fantastic, barely slept, spent savings". Before choosing an antidepressant you must now:
A. Add an anxiolytic B. Reconsider bipolar disorder and rethink the plan C. Double the SSRI D. Ignore: it was enthusiasm
**Answer: B.** Any hypomanic history converts this to bipolar-spectrum management; antidepressant monotherapy risks a switch. The single most-litigated error in psychopharmacology.

**4.** Severe postpartum depression with nihilistic delusions and refusal of feeds. The fastest effective treatment:
A. Fluoxetine 40 mg B. Wait 4 weeks for therapy C. ECT D. St John's Wort
**Answer: C.** Psychotic depression with inability to eat is a classic ECT indication; medicines are too slow alone, and herbs are irrelevant.

**5.** A patient stops paroxetine abruptly after 5 months, reporting dizziness, "electric shocks", nausea and insomnia, but mood remains good. Most likely:
A. Relapse of depression B. Discontinuation syndrome C. Serotonin syndrome D. Brain tumour
**Answer: B.** FINISH symptoms after abrupt paroxetine stop with preserved mood = discontinuation syndrome, worst of the SSRIs; manage by restarting and tapering, and explain it is not addiction.

**6.** Under mhGAP/DMHP, the correct primary-care depression package is:
A. Referral to a psychiatrist for all cases B. Two-question screen + activation counselling + SSRI if moderate-severe + review at 2 weeks C. Benzodiazepines for 6 months D. ECT at PHC level
**Answer: B.** mhGAP's stepped logic is built exactly for task-shared primary care in India; screen, activate, medicate when moderate-severe, review, and refer only the red-flag set.
