# Vascular dementia and vascular cognitive impairment

## 1. In one line + overview

**Tagline:** Vascular dementia is thinking loss caused by damaged blood supply to the brain, strokes big and small, producing a **stepwise or patchy** decline whose brightest message is that its main driver, vascular disease, is **largely preventable and treatable**.

**Overview, in plain words:** The brain is 2% of body weight but takes 20% of the blood supply; it is the most blood-hungry organ we have. When blood vessels harden, narrow, or burst, brain tissue loses oxygen and dies; when enough strategic tissue is lost, thinking slows, planning fails, walking changes, mood dips. This is vascular cognitive impairment; when it becomes severe enough to interfere with daily life it is called vascular dementia; the **second commonest cause of dementia worldwide**, and arguably the commonest in some East Asian and Indian stroke-heavy clinics. Its personality differs from Alzheimer's: decline comes in **steps** (each small stroke a sudden drop, then a plateau) rather than a smooth slide; **slowness and planning trouble appear early** while memory may hold for years; gait change, falls, urinary urgency and emotional lability are its signature flags. Because it rides on hypertension, diabetes, smoking and cholesterol, all treatable, vascular dementia is, in a real sense, the dementia you can *fight back against*.

## 2. Learning objectives

After working through this note, you should be able to:

1. Define vascular dementia and vascular cognitive impairment, and explain the stepwise-course concept.
2. Describe the three mechanisms: large-vessel infarcts, strategic infarcts, and small-vessel disease.
3. Contrast the cognitive *profile* (early executive slowing, patchy deficits) with Alzheimer's (early memory loss).
4. Recognise the neurological "extras": gait change, falls, urinary symptoms, pseudobulbar affect, parkinsonism.
5. Build the risk-factor list and the prevention prescription, the treatment's real core.
6. Apply the diagnostic logic (imaging plus a temporal link to a vascular event) and know why the history matters as much as the scan.
7. Manage it: risk-factor control, stroke prevention, cautious cognition medicines, walking and mood, family education.
8. Explain the concept of mixed dementia and why it changes almost every management plan.

## 4. Epidemiology

### Global

- Vascular dementia accounts for roughly **15–25% of dementia** as the "pure" form, but mixed vascular-Alzheimer brains are the most common finding when pathologists look, so its *contribution* is larger than its headcount.
- It is the most common dementia in people with **stroke**: about a quarter to a third of stroke survivors develop dementia within a year (the risk remains raised for years).
- East Asia and Eastern Europe report relatively higher proportions of vascular dementia; some Indian series mirror the stroke burden.
- Men predominate slightly (unlike Alzheimer's); age of onset overlaps with stroke age, often 60s–70s; survival after diagnosis tends to be shorter than Alzheimer's because the same vessels threaten the heart and kidneys too.

### India

- India is the world capital of young-onset stroke and insulin resistance: rising **hypertension** (awareness and control rates under a third), **diabetes** (among the world's largest populations), and smoking combine to feed vascular brain injury.
- The 10/66 Indian site studies and others suggest a high proportion of vascular contribution to late-life cognitive impairment in Indian elderly; in memory-clinic series from Indian metros, vascular and mixed cases are a leading subgroup.
- Under-detection is enormous: a person's first "mini-stroke" forgetfulness is often dismissed as weakness of age, and the CT that follows the stroke is rarely accompanied by any cognitive assessment at all. The single biggest Indian opportunity: **every stroke clinic should be doing a memory test at follow-up.**

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**The mechanisms (how blood supply actually damages thinking):**

- **Large-vessel disease:** carotid and middle cerebral artery territory infarcts knock out whole cognitive functions (speech, calculation, spatial skill) depending on the region. The dementia is what the strokes add up to.
- **Strategic infarct dementia:** one well-placed, even small, stroke can cause dementia on its own, classically in the **thalamus** (memory relay), **caudate** (frontal circuit), **angular gyrus** (language-number), or the **medial frontal/anterior communicating artery territory** (initiation and encoding). Sudden-onset dementia in a hypertensive patient: think strategic infarct.
- **Small-vessel disease:** the arterioles deep in the brain stiffen and cause (a) **lacunes**; tiny deep infarcts, and (b) diffuse **white-matter changes** (chronic under-perfusion of the wiring). This subcortical pattern produces the classic picture: slowing, apathy or depression, executive failure, small-stepped gait, falls, urinary urgency; Binswanger's disease is its historical extreme name.
- **Mixed mechanism:** most real elderly brains carry both amyloid and vascular pathology; the clinical picture is a blend, and risk factors feed both fires.

**Risk factors (where the prevention lives):**

- Vascular: **hypertension (the biggest player)**, diabetes, smoking, atrial fibrillation, high cholesterol, obesity, sleep apnoea, excessive salt, physical inactivity.
- Demographic/genetic: older age, male sex, family history of stroke, the rare inherited **CADASIL** (NOTCH3 mutation, migraine with aura in the 30s, recurrent small strokes in the 40s–50s, then subcortical dementia; ask about early strokes in relatives when a youngish patient presents).
- Event-related: any stroke, any transient ischaemic attack, coronary bypass or major vascular surgery (post-operative cognitive decline).
- Indian context: early-onset diabetes in the 40s, high smokeless-tobacco and smoking use, untreated atrial fibrillation, and very low blood-pressure-control rates make this the dementia with the largest *preventable* fraction in our population.

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: the pipes and the wiring.** Imagine the deep brain as a bungalow loft crossed by thin water pipes, with the wiring (white matter tracts) clipped alongside. Hypertension pounds the pipes for decades; they thicken, stiffen, and start to leak. Every time a pipe blocks, a small patch of wiring dies (a lacune); every time the whole loft is under-supplied, the wiring's insulation swells and misfires (white matter change). The loft connects the frontal "manager" office to the rest of the house, so the first complaints are about **speed and management**, not memory storage.

**Story 2: the stepwise staircase.** Alzheimer's is a ramp; vascular dementia is a staircase. Each infarct drops the person down a step abruptly (often noticed as a sudden "he became slow/odd/confused" week), followed by a plateau, sometimes even some recovery, until the next step. Families who draw this staircase for you in clinic have essentially handed you the diagnosis.

**Story 3: the shared soil.** The same vessel disease is quietly narrowing coronary and renal arteries. That is why vascular dementia patients die more of heart attacks, heart failure and kidney disease than of the dementia itself, and why the memory clinic prescription (pressure, sugar, statin, walking, smoking cessation) is also the heart clinic prescription. One prescription, two organs saved.

## 7. Symptoms

**Cognitive, the "subcortical" face:**

- **Slowness of thought (bradyphrenia):** answers arrive late; decisions take forever; mental gear changes are stiff.
- **Executive failure early:** cannot plan a puja or a family function that she managed for 30 years; gets stuck when routines change; poor judgement in money.
- **Patchy, uneven deficits:** some functions surprisingly intact, others clearly lost; unlike Alzheimer's even decline. Memory for recent events may remain usable for years, though retrieval is slow.
- Insight is often **relatively preserved** early, which is exactly why depression, frustration and embarrassment are so common.

**Neurological "extras" (the clues to vascular):**

- **Gait:** small steps, shuffling, unsteadiness, "gait apraxia", walking disorder appearing with or before the thinking disorder; **falls** follow.
- **Urinary:** urgency, frequency, incontinence, appears earlier than in Alzheimer's.
- **Pseudobulbar affect:** sudden uncontrolled laughter or crying out of proportion to feelings (the family says "he cries for nothing"), from subcortical disconnection.
- Focal signs: mild weakness, clumsy hand, asymmetrical reflexes, dysarthria (slurring), parkinsonism of the lower body.
- **Depression and apathy** are frequent, and under-treated.

**Course:** stepwise decline with plateaus; fluctuations *between* plateaus (better mornings, worse evenings are common); overnight worsening usually means a new infarct, a seizure, or a delirium trigger, investigate rather than blame the dementia.

## 8. Diagnosis

### The logic of DSM-5 / ICD-11 (paraphrased)

DSM-5 uses the same major-neurocognitive-disorder skeleton (decline from baseline, objective deficits, loss of independence, not delirium). **Vascular neurocognitive disorder** is declared when the decline relates temporally to a cerebrovascular event (stroke, haemorrhage) **or** there is imaging evidence of significant vascular injury plus a course of stepwise or abrupt decline consistent with it. ICD-11 similarly requires cerebrovascular disease to be judged the *cause*, not just a fellow-traveller. The classic older research criteria (NINDS-AIREN: named, not reproduced) formalised the same idea: dementia + cerebrovascular disease + a link between them.

### Workup

- **History is the half-diagnosis:** stepwise timeline, known stroke/TIA, transient monocular blindness, morning headaches in the hypertensive, smoking and diabetes duration, drug compliance (or the notorious pattern of stopping tablets once "pressure felt normal").
- **Examination:** blood pressure in both arms, cardiac rhythm (atrial fibrillation), carotid bruits, focal neurology, gait observation (get the patient to walk; the gait is often more eloquent than the memory test), fundus for hypertensive changes.
- **Cognitive testing:** brief tests (MoCA-type preferred, it weights executive function more than MMSE-type screens) plus fluency, trail-making-type tasks where available; the deficit *pattern* (executive/processing-speed out of proportion to amnesia) is the diagnostic gold.
- **Imaging (this is the one dementia where imaging is close to mandatory):** CT is acceptable for infarct demonstration; **MRI** shows lacunes, white-matter hyperintensities, microbleeds, strategic infarcts, and atrophy pattern. But caution: white-matter changes are common in normal ageing; *imaging must be linked to the clinical picture*, or you will label every scanned elder.
- **Vascular workup:** ECG (rhythm), echo where indicated, sugar/lipids/renal function, homocysteine in the young, and carotid imaging in large-vessel cases. Hachinski score (named) may help students weigh vascular vs degenerative features.
- **Rule-out bloods** as in any dementia (thyroid, B12; see the Alzheimer's note; one fact, one home).
- **Young patient with white-matter disease and migraines:** ask the family tree, CADASIL; genetic testing and skin biopsy exist, mostly tertiary-centre.

### Differential highlights

| Against | Clue |
|---------|------|
| Alzheimer's | Stepwise vs smooth; slowness/planning first vs memory first; focal signs present; MRI infarcts load |
| Normal-pressure hydrocephalus | Gait worse than cognition proportion, incontinence early, large ventricles without shrinkage of cortex |
| Depression | Weeks-onset, effort-dependent testing, mood before gait change |
| Progressive supranuclear palsy / parkinsonian dementias | Eye-movement palsy, axial rigidity, symmetrical parkinsonism |
| Delirium (again!) | The step of a *new* stroke vs a fluctuating toxic-metabolic picture, image when in doubt |

## 9. Management

### (a) Vascular risk control: the heart of the treatment

- **Blood pressure:** treat to guideline targets, but gently in the frail elderly; sudden over-treatment in stiff arteries can worsen perfusion and even cognition. Aim for steady control over years, not dramatic drops.
- **Antithrombotics:** antiplatelet therapy (aspirin/clopidogrel per physician) after non-cardioembolic stroke; **anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation** after stroke (DOAC or warfarin as appropriate); one of the most evidence-backed dementia-preventing moves there is; caution: microbleeds on MRI temper enthusiasm for over-aggressive antithrombotics in advanced small-vessel disease.
- **Diabetes and lipids:** control to guideline; statin after stroke as per cardiology/neurology advice.
- **Lifestyle:** walking 30 minutes most days (falls permitting), salt reduction, smoking cessation, the cheapest and best-tolerated "memory medicines" in this disease.
- **Sleep apnoea:** ask about snoring and day sleepiness; treatable and under-diagnosed cause of cognitive fluctuation in the vascular patient.

*India note: amlodipine, metformin, a statin and aspirin together cost under a couple of hundred rupees a month in generics (approx, 2026, varies). The actual challenge is compliance and follow-up, not cost; one family member should own the medicine box and the BP diary.*

### (b) Cognition medicines: modest, worth trying, honestly framed

- **Donepezil** and **memantine** have trials in vascular cognitive impairment showing small benefits on cognition and global impression; they are frequently used off-label-in-spirit for this indication. Same dosing as the Alzheimer's note.
- Do not promise what they cannot deliver: the disease-modifying treatment here is the vascular control, and that is not a tablet; it is a system of follow-up.

### (c) The neurological symptoms

- **Gait and falls:** physiotherapy, home rails, remove loose rugs, night lighting, footwear; a single fall with hip fracture converts a walking vascular dementia into a bed-bound one.
- **Urinary urgency:** timed toileting, treat prostatic issues in men, avoid anticholinergic bladder drugs if possible (they blunt cognition; see the delirium and Alzheimer's notes).
- **Pseudobulbar affect:** explain it first ("the wiring, not the heart"); SSRI or low-dose options can reduce episodes.
- **Depression:** treat actively; SSRIs, activation, problem-solving support; mood improvement alone can "give back" what looked like dementia.
- **Agitation/BPSD:** same trigger-first, drug-second ladder as in the Alzheimer's note.

### (d) Prevention: the family prescription

For the patient's siblings and children, who share the risk: the same blood-pressure, sugar, salt, tobacco and exercise message. Framed this way, the diagnosis of one elder becomes the prevention opportunity for a whole household.

*India note: family-level advice fits naturally, the "we all eat from the same kitchen" conversation (less salt, less fried, more walking for everyone) converts the patient's illness into the household's health plan. District-level stroke follow-up camps that add one memory question are the cheapest scalable screening we have.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **Guidelines:** the Indian Stroke Association and neurology practice guidance for secondary stroke prevention apply directly; psychiatric/geriatric practice follows DSM/ICD logic plus IPS general guidance.
- **Where patients land:** stroke units and neurology OPDs (the missed opportunity: no cognitive check at follow-up); memory clinics in metros; general medicine for "pressure and sugar control", rarely connecting the two; psychiatry usually enters late, for depression or BPSD.
- **PM-JAY (Ayushman Bharat)** covers stroke treatment and many hospital events; medicines for risk factors are cheap generics in Jan Aushadhi stores; the practical pathway for daily treatment is the local PHC + district follow-up.
- **Caregiving:** as in all dementias, home-based; but the physical burden is heavier; the vascular patient falls, needs toileting earlier, and may have hemiparesis alongside. Combine dementia-care teaching with stroke rehabilitation teaching (one home programme).
- **Common clinical traps in India:** (1) "weakness" clinics where cognitive symptoms never get asked; (2) CT-lesion-obsessed practice where white-matter change is either ignored or over-diagnosed; (3) stopping antihypertensives when "the pressure became normal"; the classic cause of the next step down the staircase; (4) attributing pseudobulbar crying to depression alone and missing the vascular cause.
- **Primary care role:** enormous. BP control over years, diabetes follow-up, arrhythmia detection (teach pulse-feeling in every elderly contact), one memory question at every stroke follow-up, and salt/tobacco advice to the household.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: the staircase nobody drew.** A 66-year-old bank manager with 15 years of hypertension (medicines stopped whenever he "felt fine") and diabetes, presented after his family documented three drops over two years: first, suddenly unable to balance the household accounts after a "giddiness week" (a lacunar stroke on MRI); eight months later, a fall in the bathroom and subsequent fear of walking alone; six months after that, crying episodes at odd moments and losing the thread while reciting prayers. Testing showed striking slowness and poor planning with relatively preserved memory for recent events. MRI: multiple lacunes and confluent white-matter changes. Diagnosis: subcortical vascular dementia. Restarted antihypertensives with a BP diary and a son in charge of the tablet box, diabetes tightened, physiotherapy for gait, SSRI for the affect episodes. At one year: no further steps down, walking with a stick, and the crying episodes halved.

*Teaching points: the stepwise history *is* the diagnosis; "stopped my tablets" is the Indian signature; control of the staircase matters more than any memory tablet; pseudobulbar affect responds to explanation plus SSRI.*

**Case 2: the overnight "dementia" that demanded a scan.** A 72-year-old hypertensive woman with known mild forgetfulness was brought with "she has become severely confused since yesterday evening." Speed of onset screamed delirium, but her gait had also acutely worsened and her smile was asymmetrical. Urgent imaging: fresh strategic infarct in the left thalamic region. Atrial fibrillation was newly detected on ECG. She was anticoagulated after the neurology team's assessment, blood pressure stabilised, and her cognition improved partially over weeks, a new baseline, lower than before.

*Teaching points: sudden cognitive worsening in a vascular patient = new stroke, seizure, or delirium-trigger, image, don't just assume; the thalamus is the classic strategic memory relay; AF found is AF treated; every step down deserves a cause hunt.*

## 13. FAQ

**"The doctor said it is not Alzheimer's but vascular, is that better news?"**
In one way, yes: the causes (blood pressure, sugar, cholesterol, rhythm problems) are partly treatable, so the staircase can be slowed. But it is still a serious dementia; it still changes life; it still needs planning.

**"Can the damage already done be repaired?"**
Lost brain tissue does not regrow. But plateaus are real, rehabilitation can recover some function after each step, and mood or hearing or pain problems can masquerade as worsening, so treat what is treatable before concluding it is a step.

**"He cried at the wedding for no reason. Is he depressed?"**
Possibly, but if laughter and crying burst out of proportion to feelings, it may be pseudobulbar affect, a wiring problem from small-vessel disease. It is explainable, often reducible with medicines, and it is not "madness".

**"Should I stop his blood pressure medicines now that he has dementia?"**
No: the opposite, unless his doctor has a specific reason. Good blood pressure control protects what remains. Only frail-patient over-treatment (falls, dizziness on standing) calls for loosening, and that is a clinical decision, not a family one.

**"Will I get it too?"**
Blood-pressure tendencies and diabetes do run in families and shared kitchens, which is why we ask the whole household to cut salt, walk, and get their numbers checked. That is the good news: it is actionable.

**"He walks so slowly. Is exercise safe?"**
Yes, adapted: walking within the house, then short outdoor walks with a stick and a companion; physiotherapy exercises for balance. Bed rest makes both gait and thinking worse.

**"What watchful signs mean 'go to hospital'?"**
A new sudden step down (more confusion, new weakness, slurred speech, one-sided change) or an overnight behavioural change. These can mean a fresh stroke; they are emergencies.

**"Do the memory medicines used for Alzheimer's work here?"**
Slightly, in some people, for some functions. Worth a monitored trial. But the disease's real treatment is the risk-factor system, and that is lifelong, not a course.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **Mnemonics to build:** risk factors *S-V-D-T-B* style lists are less useful than the *mechanism trio*: large vessel / strategic infarct / small vessel. Own that trio and everything else hangs on it.
- **Highest-yield single fact:** stepwise decline + focal signs + executive-predominant deficits + infarcts on imaging.
- **Classic traps:** (1) diagnosing Alzheimer's because "MRI shows atrophy too" while ignoring the lacunes; (2) diagnosing vascular dementia from white-matter spots alone with no clinical link; (3) missing atrial fibrillation because nobody felt the pulse; (4) blaming the dementia for an overnight decline that was actually a new infarct or a sodium of 118; (5) over-treating blood pressure in the frail elderly and worsening cognition.
- **Viva favourites:** Hachinski ischaemic score (what it tries to measure, not the items); strategic infarct sites (thalamus, caudate, angular gyrus); Binswanger and lacunar state names; CADASIL (NOTCH3, migraine + early strokes + subcortical dementia, autosomal dominant); the one-year post-stroke dementia statistic; gait disorder plus incontinence differential (NPH vs small-vessel disease, ventricle size distinguishes).
- **Indian exam corner:** young-onset stroke burden, "stopped tablets when pressure felt normal" narrative; stroke-unit access inequity; PM-JAY coverage for stroke events; integrating one cognitive question into stroke follow-up as a screening policy suggestion (a favourite "how would you design a programme" answer).
- **Rehearse one line:** "In vascular dementia, the scan does not make the diagnosis; the link between the vessels and the mind does."

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. The cognitive profile that most suggests vascular rather than Alzheimer's dementia is:**
A. Early profound episodic memory loss
B. Early executive dysfunction and psychomotor slowing
C. Fluent empty speech
D. Loss of reading early

**Answer: B.** Subcortical-frontal circuits fail first in small-vessel disease; memory is relatively spared early.

**2. A 70-year-old has had three abrupt drops in function over two years with plateaus between. The imaging most likely shows:**
A. Hippocampal atrophy only
B. Multiple infarcts / lacunes and white-matter change
C. Normal study
D. A frontal tumour

**Answer: B.** Stepwise decline plus plateau is the multi-infarct / small-vessel signature.

**3. The single strategic lesion most classically able to cause sudden dementia is:**
A. Left thalamic infarct
B. Occipital pole infarct
C. Cerebellar infarct
D. Small internal capsule lacune

**Answer: A.** The thalamus is the memory and relay gateway; strategic infarcts there (also caudate, angular gyrus) can produce abrupt dementia.

**4. In a frail elderly patient with extensive white-matter disease, the blood pressure approach is:**
A. Drive it as low as possible immediately
B. Steady guideline control, avoiding abrupt over-treatment
C. Stop all antihypertensives
D. Treat only when symptomatic

**Answer: B.** Long-term control protects the brain; acute over-treatment of stiff cerebrovascular systems can worsen perfusion and cognition.

**5. Atrial fibrillation found after a stroke matters most because:**
A. It predicts epilepsy
B. Anticoagulation prevents further infarcts, and further cognitive steps
C. It requires a pacemaker always
D. It explains the crying episodes

**Answer: B.** Rhythm control of stroke recurrence is one of the best-evidenced dementia-prevention moves; pseudobulbar affect is unrelated.

**6. CADASIL is:**
A. A mitochondrial disorder
B. An autosomal dominant small-vessel disease (NOTCH3) with migraine and early strokes
C. A complication of diabetes only
D. A variant of Alzheimer's disease

**Answer: B.** NOTCH3 mutations; migraine with aura, recurrent subcortical strokes in the 40s–50s, then subcortical dementia; ask the family tree in young white-matter-disease patients.
