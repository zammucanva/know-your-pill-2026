# Amnesic syndromes (including Korsakoff syndrome)

## 1. In one line + overview

**Tagline:** The amnesic syndrome is a **punched-out hole in new memory** (attention, personality and old memories intact, fresh learning gone) most often caused by **thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency in alcohol misuse**, and the tragedy is that the hole is largely **preventable with a 10-rupee injection given in time**.

**Overview, in plain words:** In the classic amnesic syndrome, a strange thing happens: the person can talk, reason, joke and recognise everyone, but cannot lay down new memories for more than a few minutes. Yesterday's visit vanishes overnight; the same question returns thirty times; the gaps are filled, sometimes magnificently, with **confabulation**; invented stories produced by a mind trying to be helpful. The cause is damage to a memory-filing circuit that runs through the **thalamus and mammillary bodies** deep in the brain. The commonest route there is **thiamine deficiency from chronic, malnourished drinking**: untreated, it first causes the acute crisis of confusion and eye-movement paralysis (**Wernicke encephalopathy**), and then settles into the permanent gap of **Korsakoff syndrome**. Two other doors exist: blockage of memory structures (thalamic or temporal stroke) and severe oxygen or glucose starvation of the brain. Psychiatry's jobs are four: **give thiamine early and generously** (before any glucose load); **recognise the syndrome** behind the "confabulating alcoholic"; **separate it from dementia and from ordinary drunkenness**; and **build the family system that lets a memory-less person live safely and with dignity.**

## 2. Learning objectives

1. Define the amnesic syndrome by its four features (new-memory failure; relative sparing of immediate memory, personality and old memories; confabulation; anosognosia variable).
2. Draw the memory circuit (hippocampal formation → fornix → mammillary bodies → thalamus) and name which lesions hit it.
3. Recite the Wernicke triad and the treatment rule, thiamine **before** glucose, with parenteral dosing.
4. Explain confabulation and distinguish it from lying.
5. Differentiate the amnesic syndrome from dementia, delirium and transient global amnesia.
6. Manage established Korsakoff syndrome: environment, memory aids, family rules, safety, alcohol treatment.
7. Address the Indian realities: malnourished drinking, hunger and hyperemesis as non-alcoholic routes, and the missed-injection audit point.

## 4. Epidemiology

### Global
- Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome is overwhelmingly associated with alcohol misuse; estimates of Wernicke's frequency among people with severe alcohol problems run in the double figures at autopsy, far above bedside recognition; it is **missed in most cases that have it**.
- Non-alcoholic routes matter wherever malnutrition and vomiting exist: starvation, hunger strikes, anorexia, hyperemesis of pregnancy, bariatric surgery, prolonged vomiting, dialysis, cancer, AIDS.
- Survival with established Korsakoff syndrome is variable: many patients stabilise and live years in supported settings; a substantial minority (around a quarter to a fifth in classic series) improve meaningfully; complete recovery is rare.

### India
- Indian reports of Wernicke–Korsakoff come from two streams: alcohol-dependent patients with heavy *and* malnourished drinking (arrack/country liquor populations, where the diet is nearly carbohydrate-only), and **non-alcoholic thiamine crises in women with hyperemesis gravidarum, famine/starvation, tuberculosis and severe dietary restriction**; the latter group is the classic Indian exam vignette and the clinical blind spot.
- The intervention gap is the story: the syndrome is preventable at the cost of a few ampoules of thiamine, yet the injection is routinely forgotten at first glucose infusion; the audit point every training programme should drill.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**Thiamine-deficiency route (commonest):**
- Chronic heavy drinking with poor diet (thiamine absorption and storage both impaired by alcohol).
- Non-alcoholic: hyperemesis of pregnancy; anorexia nervosa; starvation and hunger; bariatric surgery; prolonged vomiting/jejunal feeding; refeeding after severe malnutrition (carbohydrate load on empty thiamine stores, the "refeeding syndrome" logic); dialysis; severe infection; heart failure; AIDS; thyrotoxicosis.

**Direct-lesion route:**
- Thalamic stroke (anterior or medial thalamic, arterial territory, sudden amnesia in a hypertensive patient).
- Temporal/hippocampal damage: herpes simplex encephalitis, surgery, anoxia, carbon monoxide, severe hypoglycaemia, head trauma.
- Third-ventricle and fornix-area tumours/surgery.

**Risk factors to act on:** anything combining carbohydrate load with empty thiamine stores (glucose drip in a starving or drinking patient, carb-heavy binges); repeated Wernicke episodes before the first treated one; prior alcohol-related seizures.

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: the filing room is intact; the porter is dead.** New memory formation works like a library: the reading room (working memory) holds a book for seconds; a porter (the hippocampal-thalamic circuit) must carry it to the stacks (long-term storage). In the amnesic syndrome, the porter's corridor, mammillary bodies and thalamus, is damaged. The book can be read, discussed, enjoyed... and never shelved. Tomorrow, the person denies ever seeing it, because in *his* library, he never did.

**Story 2: the helpful inventor.** Asked what he did this morning, a brain with a memory hole does not tolerate silence: it offers material that feels like memory, a plausible mixture of old habits, fragments and guesses. That is **confabulation**, not lying (there is no intent to deceive) but **honest retrieval of material that was never filed.** It fades with time in most Korsakoff patients; vivid, rich confabulation is more prominent early.

**Story 3: the ten-rupee clock.** Thiamine is the spark plug for brain glucose metabolism. The thalamus and mammillary bodies have high metabolic demands and thin reserves: when thiamine runs out, they fail within days. Give thiamine **before** the glucose load and they usually recover; give glucose first (glucose metabolism *consumes* thiamine) and you push them over the edge; the lesion becomes permanent. The whole prevention message of this note is a sequence rule, not an expensive drug: **B1 before D5.**

## 7. Symptoms

**The pure syndrome:**
- **Immediate memory intact**: can repeat a phone number or a sentence right away, can hold a normal conversation, do mental arithmetic, play cards.
- **New memory fails within minutes**: cannot recall the morning's visit, the meal just eaten, the names introduced an hour ago.
- **Remote memory relatively preserved**: childhood, schooling, old songs; events just before the illness may have a border blur.
- **Personality and social skills preserved**: warmth, manners, humour survive; this is what separates Korsakoff patients socially from dementia patients.
- **Confabulation**: plausible invented answers, more vivid early, usually fading to "I don't remember" over months.
- **Anosognosia variable**; some know and joke about the hole; many are indifferent (indifference from frontal/thalamic involvement is classic).

**Wernicke encephalopathy: the emergency that precedes it (recognise on the spot):**
- **Confusion/apathy**, **eye-movement paralysis or double vision/nystagmus**, **ataxia (wide-based unsteady gait)**; fever, tachycardia and hypotension may accompany. The full triad is the exception, **any one sign in a malnourished drinker or vomiting patient demands parenteral thiamine now.**
- Untreated: progression to stupor, coma and death, or into Korsakoff syndrome.

**Other-route variants:** sudden-onset (thalamic stroke) amnesia with other stroke signs; fever-and-seizure onset (herpes encephalitis); post-anoxic amnesia after resuscitation; **transient global amnesia** (a middle-aged/elderly person with a pure hours-long memory gap, often after exertion or immersion in cold water, repeatedly asking the same questions, then complete recovery) frightening, benign, recurs rarely.

## 8. Diagnosis

### The clinical logic
The syndrome is defined by its **architecture** (as above), not by a test. Examination pattern to build at the bedside: name-and-address registration → immediate recall intact → 5-minute recall gone → remote memory (school, marriage) intact → working conversation seamless. Then hunt the cause: drinking/nutrition history, vomiting, weight loss, pregnancy, gastric surgery; eye movements and gait now (Wernicke signs may still be active; treat first, investigate alongside); vascular risk for thalamic stroke; fever and temporal signs for encephalitis.

### Workup
- **Bloods:** thiamine where available (rarely needed practically; clinical suspicion treats), magnesium (deficiency perpetuates the picture and must be replaced), full counts, sugar, renal, liver, B12/folate, and where relevant pregnancy test.
- **MRI brain:** mammillary body and medial-thalamic signal change in Wernicke–Korsakoff; thalamic infarct; temporal herpes changes; atrophy patterns of the mimics.
- **Memory assessment** (fuller where available): the dissociation between intact immediate and destroyed delayed recall is the formal fingerprint.
- **EEG** if seizures or encephalitis are in question; **CSF** when herpes is (temporal presentation, fever, confusion out of proportion).

### Differentials
| Against | Clue |
|---------|------|
| Delirium | Immediate memory and attention are *impaired*, clouded consciousness, fluctuation, versus preserved immediate in pure amnesia |
| Alzheimer's dementia | Gradual, remote memory eventually lost, other cognitive domains fall too |
| Depression-related "poor memory" | Encoding fails from effort, but new learning recovers with cueing and treatment |
| Psychogenic/dissociative amnesia | Patchy, memory for identity/trauma periods, inconsistency across examinations, the diagnosis of exclusion after careful exclusion |
| Transient global amnesia | Self-limited hours; full return; no cause found; typically 50–70 |
| Alcohol intoxication/withdrawal | Clears with detoxification; eye-movement and gait signs distinguish Wernicke's |

## 9. Management

### (a) The emergency rule: before anything else
- **Parenteral thiamine, generously** (e.g. high-dose intravenous thiamine, continuing for several days in suspected Wernicke's, doses per local protocol; the principle: oral thiamine absorbs poorly in these states, so early parenteral replacement is the evidence-based move), **always before any glucose-containing fluid**; magnesium replacement alongside; then nutritional rehabilitation.
- Every malnourished/alcohol-dependent/vomiting patient presenting to casualty or clinic gets this *by default*: making the injection automatic is the syndrome's real prevention programme.

### (b) Established Korsakoff syndrome: building a life around the porter
- **Stability and labelling:** fixed routines, same rooms, same faces; clocks, calendars, labels and photographs everywhere; the family uses the same sentences ("after lunch we sit on the veranda") so life becomes a predictable rail.
- **Memory aids to body memory:** procedures survive even when facts don't, habits, songs, routes; teach the family to build life as habit rather than as instruction.
- **One-encounter honesty:** visitors introduce themselves each time; the family stops correcting ("you DO remember him!"): correction exhausts both; gentle orientation replaces it.
- **Safety:** stove and money locks, wandering plan, ID card, bathing supervision (sequencing of steps often frays).
- **Alcohol treatment** where relevant: motivational conversation, detoxification if withdrawal risk, family contract, relapse-care plan, the memory handicap demands *family-led* adherence designs, since the patient cannot self-track appointments or doses; a medicine box with alarms belongs to the family's phone, not his.
- **Pharmacology:** no drug restores the filed gap; trials (cholinesterase inhibitors, SSRIs, others) have been disappointing-to-modest. What to treat: depression, insomnia, agitation, the usual careful, low-dose way; and any remaining Wernicke activity (thiamine continued through the months of recovery).
- **Prognosis honesty:** improvement happens in a minority and takes months; the family should be told that some capacity returns, that the disability itself usually persists, and that supported life, not cure, is the goal.

*India note: thiamine ampoules are among the cheapest medicines in the hospital (approx ₹10–30 per ampoule, 2026); the Korsakoff problem in India is therefore never cost; it is the forgotten sequence rule (B1 before D5) and the missing follow-up structure. Institutional options are scarce; the realistic home package is the routine-and-labels system above, taught in one family session.*

### (c) Non-alcoholic routes
- Hyperemesis of pregnancy: treat the vomiting, replace thiamine and magnesium, feed back slowly, and obstetric teams should carry the same sequence rule.
- Refeeding after starvation: start calories low, thiamine and electrolytes first, the refeeding-syndrome discipline.
- Stroke and post-anoxic cases: memory rehabilitation as in the TBI note; recovery has the same "treat the load, then see the floor" logic.

## 10. Indian practice

- **Where it is missed:** the malnourished drinker in casualty receiving a dextrose drip for "weakness"; the hyperemesis mother admitted for IV fluids without vitamins for days; the starved elderly in famine or post-institutional neglect. Every one of these is a prevention opportunity that costs less than the IV set.
- **Teaching-level fix:** make "no dextrose before thiamine in the malnourished" a house-rule in every emergency department and maternity ward; audit it monthly.
- **The confabulating alcoholic in psychiatry OPD:** the referral usually says "dementia vs psychosis", the preserved personality and immediate memory with pure new-learning failure gives the diagnosis at the bedside in three minutes.
- **Family realities:** the spouse manages money, medicines and appointments; the patient attends happily and forgets the visit; the family must leave each consultation with one written instruction, not five spoken ones.
- **Follow-up:** three-monthly reviews for memory status, alcohol plan, nutrition and magnesium; annual checks for other alcohol-related complications (liver, neuropathy, cerebellar).

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: the dextrose drip that cost a library.** A 46-year-old arrack-dependent labourer, eating one meal a day, was brought "weak and confused" to a rural hospital and treated with an intravenous glucose drip overnight; the next morning he had double vision and a wide-based gait, which were attributed to "drink". Thiamine started on day three: eye movements recovered, gait improved, but he never again laid down memories. Three months later he was pleasant, talkative, played cards, denied ever meeting the examiner, and described at length a trip to his village that morning (he had not left the ward). The family was taught the routine-and-labels system; his wife took over money and medicines; he remained at home for years.

*Teaching points: glucose-before-thiamine is the classic iatrogenic amplifier; the full triad rarely presents; eye-movement signs in a malnourished drinker = parenteral thiamine now; the card-playing, confabulating, preserved-personality picture is the Korsakoff fingerprint; the family becomes the memory system.*

**Case 2: the mother who kept vomiting.** A 26-year-old in the 12th week of her second pregnancy had vomited for weeks and could keep down only glucose water; she became confused, then apathetic and drowsy. Admission glucose drip, then, on review, intravenous thiamine with magnesium and slow refeeding; she improved over days. Her memory at six weeks remained patchy but her children's names, her work life and her personality returned; only the weeks around the illness stayed dark.

*Teaching points: hyperemesis is the classic non-alcoholic Indian route; the same sequence rule applies in maternity; partial recovery is the good outcome to expect with early treatment.*

## 13. FAQ

**"He remembers his school days perfectly. How is this a memory disease?"**
The filing of old memories is complete and safe on the shelves; the corridor that carries new memories to the stacks is damaged. Old is safe, new is lost; that is exactly this disease.

**"He invents things about this morning. Is he lying to us?"**
No. Confabulation is the brain filling silence with material that feels like memory. There is no intention to deceive; the story is as real to him as your morning is to you.

**"Will the memory come back?"**
Some recovery occurs in a minority of patients over months, mostly in those treated early. The realistic goal is a safe, structured, dignified life where the family and the routine become his memory.

**"What is the injection for, if the damage is already done?"**
First, active injury may still be continuing; thiamine stops the fire even if the burnt part stays burnt. Second, without it, the hole gets bigger.

**"Can he stay at home?"**
Usually, yes, with the system: fixed routines, labels, money and medicines in one family member's hands, safety locks, and a written (not spoken) daily plan.

**"Can he take his own medicines?"**
Not reliably: he cannot remember taking them, so he either misses doses or repeats them. The family member with alarms owns this job permanently.

**"Does he have dementia? Is it the same thing?"**
No: dementia affects many thinking domains and worsens progressively. This is a specific hole in one function, with personality and intelligence intact. It does not spread the way dementia does.

**"The same thing happened to my aunt for a few hours and then she was fine."**
That sounds like transient global amnesia: a frightening but benign, self-limiting cousin, typically in the 50s–70s, that rarely recurs. It is not the start of this disease.

**"What should the hospital have done differently?"**
Thiamine before the glucose drip, in anyone starved, vomiting or drinking heavily. That one sequence, costing a few rupees, is the difference between this syndrome and no syndrome.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **Mnemonics:** the Wernicke trigger as **"one sign is enough"** (confusion OR eye-movement palsy OR ataxia in the malnourished); the sequence rule as **"B1 before D5"**; the syndrome architecture as **"immediate intact, recent ruined, remote retained"**.
- **Highest-yield facts:** thiamine-before-glucose; mammillary body/thalamic lesions; confabulation fades over months; procedural learning survives; TGA is benign and self-limited; hyperemesis/starvation as Indian routes.
- **Classic traps:** (1) labelling a Korsakoff patient as "dementia" on memory-weighted screens; (2) giving oral thiamine alone in active Wernicke's (absorbs poorly); (3) treating the confabulation as malingering; (4) forgetting magnesium; (5) diagnosing psychogenic amnesia without excluding the structural circuit.
- **Viva favourites:** Papez-circuit anatomy; Korsakoff vs alcoholic dementia (they differ; the former is the punched-out hole); the eye-movement signs of Wernicke's; prognosis percentages (minority improve, most stabilise); refeeding syndrome link; TGA natural history.
- **Indian exam corner:** the hyperemesis vignette; the glucose-drip catastrophe as a preventable-iatrogenesis short note; malnourished country-liquor populations; the audit idea "no dextrose before thiamine" as a quality-improvement answer.
- **Rehearse one line:** "In the confused malnourished patient, the diagnosis is made in the IV room: thiamine before glucose, every time."

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. In the pure amnesic syndrome, which memory is characteristically intact?**
A. New long-term memory
B. Immediate (working) memory
C. Confabulated memory
D. Prospective memory

**Answer: B.** The architecture: immediate intact, recent ruined, remote retained.

**2. A malnourished dependent drinker presents confused with double vision. The immediate treatment is:**
A. Intravenous glucose
B. Parenteral thiamine before any glucose
C. Haloperidol
D. CT scan first

**Answer: B.** One Wernicke sign in the malnourished is enough; glucose first worsens the injury; the scan accompanies, not precedes, treatment.

**3. Confabulation differs from lying because:**
A. It is always grandiose
B. It lacks intent to deceive: the brain offers material that feels like memory
C. It is always about childhood
D. It can be stopped by confrontation

**Answer: B.** No deception intent; the material is sincerely offered; confrontation only exhausts.

**4. The classic anatomy of Korsakoff pathology involves:**
A. Hippocampal plaques
B. Mammillary bodies and medial thalamus
C. Frontal poles bilaterally
D. Occipital cortex

**Answer: B.** The filing corridor (mammillary bodies, medial thalamic nuclei) is the lesion set.

**5. A 64-year-old repeatedly asks the same questions for 6 hours after gardening, then fully recovers. Most likely:**
A. Korsakoff syndrome
B. Transient global amnesia
C. Psychogenic amnesia
D. Wernicke encephalopathy

**Answer: B.** TGA: hours-long, self-limited, repetitive questioning, no cause found, benign course.

**6. Which survives in Korsakoff syndrome and should be exploited in care?**
A. Episodic fact learning
B. Procedural (habit) learning
C. Confabulation as therapy
D. REM sleep memory

**Answer: B.** Procedural/implicit learning persists; routines, habits and routes become the patient's functional memory system.
