# Bipolar Disorders (Manic-Depressive Illness)

## 1. In one line + overview

**Bipolar disorder = a lifelong condition of the mood-regulation system in which episodes of mania (pathological "highs") and depression alternate with normal stretches in between, treatable, often very well, but requiring long-term thinking from day one.**

Everyone's mood goes up and down; bipolar disorder is different in three ways. First, the swings are *episodic*, weeks to months of illness, not hours of bad temper. Second, they are *pathological*; the "high" is not happiness but a revved, dangerous state of overconfidence and sleeplessness; the "low" is not sadness but a body slowed to a stop. Third, the biology is *recurrent*; without mood-stabilising treatment, episodes tend to come more frequently, and each untreated episode may make the next come faster. Bipolar I involves full mania, usually needing hospital care at least once. Bipolar II involves milder hypomanias that relatives often mistake for "finally being productive", paired with crushing depressions that bring the patient to the clinic, which is why the diagnosis is missed for years and mistreated as ordinary depression. About one person in a hundred has bipolar I, and as many again have bipolar II; the suicide risk is among the highest in medicine, which is why getting the label right early is not academic; it is life-saving.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Distinguish mania, hypomania and mixed features using duration and impairment gates
- Apply the bipolar I vs II vs cyclothymia logic confidently
- Screen for past hypomania in every "depressed" patient using the right questions
- Explain the kindling/episode-sensitisation model to a patient in plain words
- Run lithium properly: doses, levels, monitoring, toxicity signs, thyroid/renal/teratogenic issues
- Choose between valproate, lamotrigine, carbamazepine and atypical antipsychotics by pole and patient
- Avoid the two lethal errors: antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar depression, and abrupt stoppage of lithium
- Manage bipolar pregnancy, bipolar in youth, and rapid cycling
- Work the Indian realities: cost of monitoring, marriage disclosure, family-based adherence, prescription rules

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Bipolar I lifetime prevalence ~0.6–1%; bipolar II ~0.4–1% (higher in some surveys); the whole bipolar spectrum including soft forms may reach 2–5%. Equal sex ratio for bipolar I; bipolar II and rapid cycling skew female. Mean onset 18–22 for bipolar I, slightly later for II; onset after 50 is unusual and should prompt a medical hunt (thyroid, steroids, frontal lesions). Mean delay from first symptoms to correct diagnosis is 5–10 years; most patients are mislabelled unipolar-depressive first and exposed to antidepressants. Suicide: around a third attempt, and completed-suicide risk is 10–15-fold elevated; the years immediately after diagnosis and mixed states are highest-risk windows.

**India.** NMHS 2015–16 found ~0.3% for "bipolar affective disorder" (0.4% male, 0.3% female, urban slightly higher) with a huge treatment gap of roughly 70%. Clinical experience adds: substantial under-recognition of hypomania (families celebrate the energetic phase and report only depression); heavier use of valproate than lithium for practical monitoring reasons; and presentations complicated by steroid use, hypothyroidism and substance use (especially cannabis and alcohol) which both mimic and worsen the course. Indian families are powerful assets for adherence and early-warning detection; provided they are briefed, and provided the illness is not hidden from them.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**Genetic (the strongest single story).**
- Heritability ~70–85%, among the highest in psychiatry.
- One bipolar parent: risk to a child roughly 5–10%; two affected parents: considerably higher (often quoted 20–40%). Siblings of patients carry similar risks.
- No single gene; polygenic risk overlapping schizophrenia and depression.

**Biological.**
- Circadian-clock fragility: shift work, transmeridian flights, and sleep loss can precipitate mania, and mania destroys sleep in a vicious cycle.
- HPA-axis and thyroid interactions; autoimmune thyroiditis commoner in bipolar women (and lithium itself affects the thyroid).
- MRI: subtle white-matter and prefrontal-limbic differences, research tools, not diagnostic tests.

**Psychological.**
- Personality traits of high goal-drive and reward-sensitivity interact with life events (goal-attainment life events precede mania more than losses do; losses precede depression).
- Childhood adversity raises earlier-onset, mixed, treatment-resistant courses.

**Social / environmental.**
- Life events trigger first episodes more than later ones (biological autonomy develops).
- Substance use: cannabis and stimulants accelerate onset and worsen course; alcohol destabilises sleep and adherence.

**Indian context.**
- Arranged-marriage concealment: families suppress history of mania to secure matches, then the marriage coincides with relapse, medication stoppage, or in-law conflict, a recurring iatrogenic-social disaster.
- Steroid courses (prescribed liberally for allergies/skin), SLE/thyroid illness, and postpartum states are common local mania mimics/trigger-factors to screen.
- Male alcohol use frequently masks mixed states until a proper family timeline is drawn.

## 6. What happens in the brain

**Story 1: the broken thermostat.** The brain holds mood in a band, the way a thermostat holds room temperature. In bipolar disorder the thermostat is a designer original with two broken settings: the heater can jam full-on (mania, confidence, energy, sexuality, speech, everything at maximum, with the safety sensors that would normally say "too much" burnt out), and the cooler can jam full-on (depression, everything at minimum, including hope). Normal stretches (euthymia) are the thermostat working between breakdowns. Mood stabilisers do not remove the thermostat; they narrow the swings.

**Story 2: the kindling fire.** Epilepsy taught psychiatry an old idea: each seizure makes the next come easier. Bipolar episodes behave similarly. The first episode usually needs a big life event to start; the second needs less; by the fifth, episodes can arrive without any trigger; the brain has *learned* the pathway. This is the honest reason treatment continues after the first episode even when the person feels completely well: each prevented episode protects the future thermostat. Stopping lithium abruptly after years is like leaving dry wood next to the stove.

**Story 3: the clock and the sun.** Bipolar biology is stitched to the body clock. A late-night exam run, a night-shift job, a red-eye flight, or a family wedding that runs three nights; each can flick the mania switch in a vulnerable person. And the earliest reliable warning of an approaching high is not mood at all: it is *decreasing need for sleep with rising energy*. Families who know this one fact can catch episodes at day two instead of week three, the difference between a tablet adjustment and an admission.

## 7. Symptoms

### Mania (the full high): bipolar I
- Elevated or irritable mood for ≥ 1 week (or any duration if hospitalisation needed), plus at least 3–4 of:
- Inflated self-esteem / grandiosity ("I have divine insight; I can fix the national economy")
- Decreased need for sleep (sleeps 2–3 hours, wakes furious with energy; note: not insomnia, *no need* to sleep)
- Pressured speech: fast, loud, uninterruptible, joke-jumping
- Flight of ideas: thoughts racing link to link
- Distractibility
- Goal-driven hyperactivity: starting many projects, religious devotion, business schemes, renovation marathons
- Hedonistic disinhibition: overspending, sexual risk, reckless driving, EMI splurges, impulsive gold/property decisions
- Psychotic features in severe mania: grandiose delusions, paranoid themes, religious mission status
- Lack of insight is near-total; the ill person feels at their *best*

**Danger signals during mania:** physical exhaustion with refusal to rest, dehydration, police involvement, unlimited giving away of money, sexual indiscretions with lasting consequences (in India, elopement or recorded material that later fuels blackmail), suicidal acts when the high crashes into mixed misery.

### Hypomania: bipolar II
- Same flavour for ≥ 4 days, observable by others, but *no marked impairment, no psychosis, no hospitalisation*
- Often experienced as the "good period": unusually productive, witty, social, less sleep, big plans
- The tell: after it passes, the projects are unfinished and the money is gone; the person is slightly embarrassed by what they did

### Bipolar depression
- Looks like severe depression (see Depressive Disorders note) BUT with atypical features more often: oversleeping, overeating, leaden heaviness of limbs, rejection sensitivity, young onset, many brief episodes, postpartum onset, family history of bipolar
- Psychotic guilt-laden features can occur
- **Mixed features** (DSM-5): ≥ 3 opposite-pole symptoms during the depression, racing thoughts, agitation, talkativeness, insomnia-despite-fatigue, risk-taking. The person feels "tired but wired", the worst mental state in psychiatry and the most dangerous for suicide.

### Cyclothymia
- At least 2 years of low-grade ups and downs (never meeting full episode criteria), never more than 2 months of stability, misdiagnosed as "moody personality", and a genuine risk factor for later full episodes.

### Course patterns worth naming
- **Rapid cycling:** ≥ 4 episodes/year, often alcohol-related, thyroid-related, or antidepressant-induced; valproate preferred over lithium
- **Seasonal patterns, postpartum-onset episodes, and late-adolescent first presentations** all change management emphasis

## 8. Diagnosis

**The logic in plain words.** Bipolar I: a manic episode, ever, makes the diagnosis, regardless of depression history (depression almost always eventually appears). Bipolar II: hypomania + major depression, with no full mania ever. Cyclothymia: chronic mild seesaw without full episodes. Duration gates: mania 7 days (or immediate hospitalisation), hypomania 4 days, depression 2 weeks. The clinician must actively rule out medical and substance highs: steroids, thyroid disease, stimulants, and frontal/temporal brain lesions, especially in first episodes, atypical ages, or focal signs.

**The screening craft, the questions that actually work:**
- Not "have you ever been too happy?" but: "Has there ever been a period of at least four days when you needed much less sleep, felt full of energy, and people said you were talking too much or making big plans?"
- Ask about credit-card/loan splurges, letters written to officials/deities/celebrities, and any week in the person's life others called "too much".
- Always get collateral: spouses and parents detect hypomania that patients will never volunteer.

**Instruments:** mood-rating scales (YMRS for mania, PHQ-9/HAM-D for depression, MDQ as a screening questionnaire, all named, not reproduced); a life-chart (episodes on a timeline) is the single most useful tool in the whole field; draw it WITH the family in the first two visits.

**Workup at diagnosis / before lithium:** TSH, creatinine/eGFR, electrolytes + calcium, LFT, fasting glucose/lipids, pregnancy test, ECG if >40 or cardiac history; baseline weight. During treatment: the monitoring schedule in Section 9.

### Differential table

| Looks like | Clue pointing away from bipolar | Clue pointing toward bipolar |
|---|---|---|
| Unipolar depression | First episodes > 30, no highs ever, classic insomnia | First episode < 25, atypical features, postpartum onset, family bipolar history |
| ADHD | Lifelong, continuous since childhood, no discrete episodes | Clear episodicity with euthymic gaps |
| Borderline personality | Hours-long reactivity to interpersonal triggers | Week-long sustained euphoric episodes with euthymic gaps; life-chart shows episodic architecture |
| Schizoaffective / schizophrenia | Psychosis persists outside mood episodes | Psychosis only within episodes; grandiosity-laden |
| Substance-induced mania | High confined to intoxication/withdrawal weeks | Recurrent highs when clean |
| Steroid / thyroid / frontal-lobe mania | Onset locks to medical cause | No medical correlate; classic early onset |
| ADHD+anxiety+temper outbursts in teens | — | Youth bipolar presents as explosive, mixed, ultra-rapid pictures, needs specialist differentiation |

## 9. Management

**The architecture: treat the episode, then defend the peace.** Acute-phase treatments (weeks) and maintenance-phase treatments (years) are different decisions. Write both plans explicitly at the first consultation.

### Acute mania / mixed states
- Stop any antidepressant.
- **First-line:** lithium (start 800–1,200 mg/day, target level 0.8–1.2 mmol/L for acute mania), valproate (loading 20–30 mg/kg/day, e.g. 1,000–1,500 mg/day; fast, easy, no levels needed in most Indian practice), quetiapine (400–800 mg/day), olanzapine (10–20 mg), risperidone (2–6 mg), or asenapine; combinations (lithium or valproate + an antipsychotic) for severe cases.
- Benzodiazepines (lorazepam/clonazepam) bridge agitation and sleep for the first week.
- **Hospitalise** when: danger to self/others, exhaustion/dehydration, psychotic, no supervision, pregnancy with severe symptoms.
- **ECT** for mania unresponsive to drugs, or where speed matters most, highly effective and safe, including in pregnancy.

### Acute bipolar depression
- **First-line:** quetiapine 300 mg/day (the best single-agent evidence for bipolar II too), lurasidone 20–120 mg (with food), lithium, or lamotrigine (titrated slowly, 25 mg × 2 weeks, 50 mg, then 100–200 mg; slow titration protects against rash).
- Olanzapine-fluoxetine combination is an approved option.
- **Antidepressant monotherapy is forbidden.** If an antidepressant is used at all, only as an add-on to a mood stabiliser, never in patients with mixed or rapid-cycling features, with an explicit stop-plan. This one rule prevents a large share of treatment-induced manias.
- Bupropion and SSRIs are the safer add-on choices if needed; venlafaxine has a poorer switch record.

### Maintenance (the phase that decides the life)
- Continue the medicine that worked acutely; first-episode patients after a classic manic episode should be maintained at least 2–5 years, and most guidelines favour indefinite treatment after 2+ episodes, frame it to patients as "blood pressure medicine for the mood system".
- **Lithium** remains gold: anti-manic, anti-depressant, anti-suicide (the only psychiatric drug with consistent suicide-mortality reduction evidence, say this to patients; it matters to them).
- **Valproate:** strong anti-manic/mixed cover; easier monitoring; teratogenic (see pregnancy).
- **Lamotrigine:** mainly anti-depressive pole; gentle, weight-neutral; slow titration.
- **Carbamazepine:** useful, many interactions (OCPs, warfarin, other psychotropics); watch for hyponatraemia and rash.
- **Quetiapine, olanzapine (with metformin watch), aripiprazole, lamotrigine-adjunct regimens**, modern outpatient workhorses.
- **Long-acting injectable antipsychotics** for adherence-fragile patients.

### Lithium running-rules (learn these cold)
- Levels: 0.8–1.2 mmol/L acute mania; 0.6–0.8 maintenance (12-hour post-dose trough)
- Check at 5–7 days after dose change, then monthly × 3, then quarterly; TSH and creatinine every 6–12 months; calcium yearly; weight each visit
- **Toxicity ladder** (know as emergency): coarse tremor, nausea, diarrhoea, slurring → confusion, ataxia → seizures, coma. Triggers: dehydration, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, thiazides, low-salt diets (Indian summers!), fevers. Blood level, hold drug, hydrate: dialysis in severe cases.
- Long-term: hypothyroidism (replace thyroxine; do NOT stop lithium), renal concentrating defect (thirst), mild cognitive dulling, weight gain, acne, psoriasis, PCOS interactions; parathyroid/calcium check.
- **Never stop abruptly**, taper over weeks to months; abrupt stoppage multiplies relapse risk in the following months.

### Special situations
- **Pregnancy:** plan it, never stumble into it. Valproate is the highest-risk major teratogen in psychiatry; avoid entirely in women who may conceive (neural tube and neurodevelopmental risks); lithium carry-forward is possible with specialised monitoring and level checks around delivery; lamotrigine and quetiapine carry the more reassuring registers. Folic acid 5 mg pre-conception. Severe episodes in pregnancy: ECT is a first-line, safe, fast option.
- **Postpartum:** childbirth is the single strongest episode trigger in bipolar illness; plan prophylaxis (restart/raise the stabiliser before or immediately after delivery, monitor closely for 6 weeks; see Acute Transient Psychosis note for puerperal psychosis).
- **Youth:** explosive mixed presentations; prefer mood-stabiliser/second-generation antipsychotic combinations; guard against antidepressant-induced switching.
- **Rapid cycling:** fix the driver, alcohol, thyroid, antidepressants, poor adherence; valproate-leaning regimens; honesty that it is hard.
- **Comorbidity:** alcohol/cannabis treatment is bipolar treatment; relapse prevention fails while substances flow.

*India note: the cost-and-monitoring ledger (approx 2026): lithium carbonate ≈ ₹60–150/month BUT needs ₹300–600 level checks and lab access; valproate ≈ ₹150–350/month with no routine levels; lamotrigine ≈ ₹150–400/month (rash education in the local language is mandatory); quetiapine 300 mg ≈ ₹350–700/month; olanzapine 10 mg ≈ ₹120–250/month. District hospital psychiatry supplies many of these free or near-free; ECT in medical-college hospitals. The real Indian cost is not the tablet; it is the travel day lost to a level check, and the wage loss of an untreated manic month. Also: pharmacist lithium availability outside metros can be patchy; know your town's supply reality before prescribing it.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **The hidden hypomania.** Indian families report the depressed member but omit the "energetic month"; either because it was the only time the person earned well, or because it was classified as religious fervour. Ask separately: "his best period, what was he like?" and "how much sleep did he need in his best period?"
- **Marriage, disclosure, and relapse.** The arranged-marriage context creates a predictable three-act tragedy: concealment → marriage with hidden diagnosis and medicines stopped secretly → relapse discovered by the spouse. The honest route (stabilised, disclosed to at least one senior family member on the other side, framed as "treatable like thyroid/epilepsy") is hard to sell but survives audits. Psychiatrists are frequently asked to write "fitness certificates", decline anything beyond a factual fitness-for-marriage-with-treatment-status letter.
- **Family as the early-warning system.** Give the family a written list of the person's three earliest signs (typically: sleep cut, morning energy rise, phone-calling spurt) and a standing instruction: call the OPD or raise the review; do not wait for the full costume. This single intervention reduces admissions more reliably than any prescription change.
- **Lithium in the heat.** Indian summers, gastroenteritis season, and Ramadan-timed fasting all cause salt/water depletion, the classic local lithium-toxicity windows. Teach the patient: "li + low salt + loose motions = come the same day."
- **Heritability counselling.** Siblings ask "will my child get it?", honest numbers: general population ~1%, one affected parent raises it to roughly 5–10%; not a prohibition, a planning conversation. Avoid both alarm and false reassurance.
- **Faith pathways.** Mania with religious content draws families to temples and dargahs first; collaterally beneficial when it brings peace, harmful when it replaces medicine. Engage respectfully, keep the medical door open.
- **Government schemes:** disability certification (40% benchmark for mental illness under RPwD 2016) applies to severely persistent cases; PM-JAY covers hospitalisation; NIMHANS/medical-college OPDs anchor low-cost maintenance.

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: the "model student" crash.** A 19-year-old engineering student in Vellore was brought by his father after staying awake five nights "finishing the semester early", spending ₹1.2 lakh of fee money on a "startup to deliver temple prasadam by drone", and messaging the college director "direct instructions received". He had been treated for "depression" a year earlier with an SSRI. Timeline taken with the mother revealed a 10-day "super-study period" then too (4 hours' sleep, notes in three colours, torrential talking) that the family had enjoyed. Diagnosis: bipolar I, manic episode with psychotic features, prior antidepressant exposure. Olanzapine 15 mg + valproate 1,250 mg; lorazepam for four nights; discharge at day 12 when sleep normalised. Maintenance continued through graduation; the family was taught the sleep-sign protocol; the drone project was quietly closed with his consent after recovery. Teaching points: (1) antidepressant-triggered mania in a misdiagnosed teen; (2) hypomania hides in "his best period", always ask about the best, not only the worst; (3) sleep is both the alarm and the treatment target.

**Case 2: the wife who checked the ledger.** A 34-year-old bank employee in Bhubaneswar with 8 years of "recurrent depression" had failed three antidepressant trials; episodes were increasingly short, with oversleeping and heaviness. On structured screening she admitted a recurring "four-to-five day sparkle": cleaning the whole house at 3 a.m., calling relatives she had fought with, buying sarees on EMI, needing 4 hours' sleep, never reported because "those were my good days, doctor". Her husband confirmed it. Diagnosis: bipolar II disorder, currently depressed, with lamotrigine begun (slow titration to 150 mg) + quetiapine 50 mg at night, antidepressant stopped. Recovery over 8 weeks; at 1 year, one mild hypomanic blip caught by her husband at day 3 via a phone call to the OPD, dose adjustment only, no admission. Teaching points: (1) failed antidepressant trials in "depression" should always trigger hypomania screening; (2) collateral history is diagnostic here; (3) the couple who can catch day-3 signs converts admissions into phone calls; that is the entire maintenance game.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way patients ask it

**"Am I mad? My relatives say mania means madness."** No. You have a mood-regulation condition that comes in episodes, like epilepsy comes in seizures. Between episodes most people with bipolar disorder are completely themselves, and with maintenance treatment the episodes can be pushed years apart or stopped altogether.

**"The doctor says I must take lithium for years though I feel completely fine."** The paradox is real: the medicine's job is precisely to keep you feeling like this. Stopping when well is like removing the umbrella the moment the rain stops. If you want to try stopping, do it planned and slow, with your doctor, with a relapse drill in place, never abruptly.

**"Is lithium poison? I read about toxicity."** Lithium is one of psychiatry's oldest and best-proven medicines, including for preventing suicide. Its window between dose and toxicity is narrower than most drugs, so we monitor blood levels and guard the salt/water balance, especially in Indian summers and stomach infections. Respected, it is safe; ignored, it bites.

**"Why can't I just take an antidepressant for the low phases?"** In this condition antidepressants alone can flip you into a high or set off frequent cycling; it is the classic mistreatment of bipolar disorder. The lows are treated with medicines that also protect the highs (quetiapine, lithium, lamotrigine), so both ends stay guarded.

**"Will my children get this?"** The honest numbers: about 5–10% if one parent is affected, higher if both. Most children of patients never develop it. This is a conversation for planning, not panic, and the child who does develop it will have something you never had: a family that recognises day one.

**"Can I marry? Should I tell the other family?"** Many people with bipolar disorder marry and parent well. Telling at least one senior member of the prospective family, after stabilisation, is the course we recommend; concealment plus stopped medicines is the most common route to a marriage-crisis relapse. We can help you plan that conversation.

**"The person is better now, can we stop everything before the wedding/auspicious date?"** Stopping medicine for an auspicious date is like cancelling the antibiotics because the fever looks better. If a dose-timing change is needed for rituals, we can adjust schedules; stopping outright multiplies relapse risk in the following months; usually on schedule to ruin the very occasion it was meant to bless.

**"Is ECT for mania dangerous?"** Modern ECT under anaesthesia is among the safest treatments we have and is sometimes the fastest way out of a severe episode, including in pregnancy. Memory blurring around the course is real and usually clears over weeks.

**"He becomes very religious during highs. Is it spiritual or illness?"** During mania, deep conviction, mission and divine feelings are common symptoms; genuine spirituality is not the question. Our test is the ledger, not the liturgy: when "devotion" arrives with no sleep and vanished savings, treat the state first; the person's real beliefs will still be there afterwards.

**"Will I lose my job/license because of this diagnosis?"** The condition itself need not be disclosed in most employment contexts, and lakhs of people with bipolar disorder work professionally on maintenance treatment. Safety-critical roles (transport, armed forces, aviation) have their own medical rules; plan those with the treating doctor rather than being ambushed by them.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**Duration gates (the exam backbone):** mania ≥ 7 days (or hospitalisation); hypomania ≥ 4 days; depression ≥ 2 weeks; cyclothymia ≥ 2 years; rapid cycling ≥ 4 episodes/year.

**Mania mnemonic: "DIGFAST"** (Distractibility, Insomnia (decreased need), Grandiosity, Flight of ideas, Activity increase, Speech (pressured), Thoughtlessness (risky behaviour)). Know it, then be able to describe each in patient words.

**Lithium mnemonic for toxicity, "the 3 C's ladder":** Coarse tremor, Confusion, Convulsions/Coma. (Simpler): GI signs → tremor → ataxia/confusion → seizures/coma. Interactions: NSAIDs, ACE-I, thiazides, dehydration.

**High-yield facts:**
- Heritability 70–85%: highest in psychiatry with autism.
- Lithium = the only psychotropic with replicated anti-suicide mortality data.
- Valproate = the strongest teratogen in common psychiatric use (avoid in women of childbearing potential).
- Lamotrigine = depressive pole; SJS risk demands slow titration and rash-education.
- Postpartum period = strongest single trigger; plan prophylaxis antenatally.
- Mixed features = highest suicide-risk state + antidepressant danger zone.
- Antidepressant-induced switching risk + early age of onset = the classic "youth sent down the wrong road" scenario.

**Classic traps:**
1. Treating the presenting depression without hypomania screening, the most common real-world error in the whole field.
2. Prescribing valproate to a woman of childbearing age without a pregnancy-conversation and folate plan.
3. Confusing hypomania with the spontaneous recovery of a withdrawn person (chart: was the sleep need *decreased*, or just normal?).
4. Missing steroid-induced or thyrotoxic mania in a first presentation.
5. Letting the patient stop lithium abruptly at the "I feel fine now" self-discharge.
6. Calling week-long euphoria with psychotic grandiosity "schizophrenia", psychosis sits INSIDE episodes in bipolar.

**Indian exam corner:** perennial questions include "lithium, pharmacology, monitoring, toxicity" (write the levels, the interaction list, the summer/dehydration context), " differentiate bipolar I from II", "management of bipolar disorder in a pregnant woman", "electroconvulsive therapy indications in mania", and the viva classic "why avoid antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar depression". NMHS 2015–16 bipolar prevalence (0.3%) and the treatment gap are quotable Indian-context marks.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. A 24-year-old woman has had 5 days of 3-hour-sleep nights, boundless energy, pressured speech, heavy online spending, and feels "invincible". She continues to attend work, though poorly. Best characterisation:**
A. Manic episode: bipolar I
B. Hypomanic episode: bipolar II
C. Mixed features with depression
D. Cyclothymia
**Answer: B.** Four-plus days of hypomanic symptoms without marked impairment/hospitalisation/psychosis = hypomania; full bipolar II picture if a major depression occurs before or after. (Watch the impairment boundary: her functioning is clearly already affected; if "marked", it upgrades toward mania.)

**2. Which single drug has replicated evidence for reducing SUICIDE MORTALITY in bipolar disorder?**
A. Valproate
B. Lithium
C. Quetiapine
D. Lamotrigine
**Answer: B.** Lithium's anti-suicide mortality effect is unique among mood treatments.

**3. A patient on lithium develops coarse tremor, vomiting, and slurred speech during a gastroenteritis week. The correct immediate steps:**
A. Double lithium to counter agitation
B. Hold lithium, check level, rehydrate urgently
C. Add an antipsychotic
D. Reassure and continue
**Answer: B.** Dehydration + lithium = toxicity ladder; hold, level, hydrate are the ABCs.

**4. First-line options for acute BIPOLAR DEPRESSION include all EXCEPT:**
A. Quetiapine monotherapy
B. Lurasidone
C. Lamotrigine (titrated slowly)
D. Venlafaxine monotherapy
**Answer: D.** Antidepressant monotherapy is contraindicated; venlafaxine carries higher switch risk than other antidepressants even when added.

**5. A woman with bipolar I on valproate plans pregnancy. The correct course is:**
A. Continue valproate at same dose, benefits outweigh risks
B. Stop valproate, substitute a lower-teratogenic strategy (e.g., quetiapine/lamotrigine or lithium with specialised monitoring), folate 5 mg, plan relapse-prevention
C. Terminate pregnancy planning permanently
D. Switch to paroxetine
**Answer: B.** Valproate is the strongest teratogen in common psychiatric use; plan the switch pre-conception with a maintenance strategy and relapse plan (ECT available for severe episodes).

**6. The strongest single trigger for a new bipolar episode is:**
A. Job promotion
B. Childbirth (postpartum period)
C. Winter
D. Change of residence
**Answer: B.** The postpartum weeks carry the highest episode risk in bipolar illness; plan prophylaxis antenatally. (Note: goal-attainment events like promotion rank high for mania, but childbirth is the strongest overall trigger.)
