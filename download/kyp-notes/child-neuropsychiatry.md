# Neuropsychiatric Disorders of Childhood — When the Brain Itself Shapes the Mind

## 1. In one line + overview

**The psychiatric consequences of a developing brain shaped by genes, toxins, hormones, injury and seizures; a domain where behavioural patterns can point back to biology, and treating the parent is part of treating the child.**

This note is the Oxford chapter on developmental neuropsychiatry: the discipline that addresses the neurobiological basis of behaviour in infants, children and adolescents with neurodevelopmental disorders or brain damage acquired during development. Its organising ideas: development is **transactional** (the child acts on the environment which acts back on the child; parental response is a critical treatment variable, not background noise); specific genetic syndromes carry **behavioural phenotypes**, characteristic personalities that hint at the underlying neuroanatomy; prenatal insults produce **timing- and dose-dependent** damage, of which foetal alcohol spectrum disorder is the most common preventable example; and childhood epilepsy and traumatic brain injury generate psychiatric pictures that routinely masquerade as "pure" behaviour problems. The chapter's quiet thesis: in child psychiatry, brain and behaviour are not competing explanations but one continuous story.

## 4. Epidemiology

**Foetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD)**: worldwide incidence of full foetal alcohol syndrome ~1.9 per 1,000 live births; FAS plus alcohol-related neurodevelopmental disorder together ~9.1 per 1,000 in one US study, and the syndrome is under-recognised because physicians do not systematically ask about alcohol. **Epilepsy**: incidence 0.7–1.1% of the general population; ~50% of all epilepsy begins in childhood; ~5% of children have recurrent seizures without extracerebral cause; ~3% have febrile convulsions, of whom ~98% never develop epilepsy; the British national survey found 0.7% of 5–15-year-olds with epilepsy and increased emotional, behavioural and peer-relationship problems among them. **Traumatic brain injury**: ~185 per 100,000 children (infancy to 14 years); ~90% are mild. **Congenital hypothyroidism**: a preventable cause of intellectual disability where early diagnosis and levothyroxine prevent the syndrome, the argument for newborn screening.

**India.** The Indian picture amplifies every one of these numbers' public-health meanings. Epilepsy carries heavy stigma (marriage, schooling, employment discrimination) on top of its psychiatric load; TBI from road traffic accidents is a large and growing paediatric burden; congenital hypothyroidism screening is patchy outside major centres; lead exposure remains common in Indian environments; and FASD, though less prominent than in high-alcohol-consumption countries, is essentially unmeasured; the chapter's warning that "physicians may not systematically enquire about alcohol" applies with full force to Indian antenatal practice.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

- **Genetic (syndromic):** Down syndrome (trisomy 21, gene dosage), fragile X (triplet repeat amplification), Williams syndrome (microdeletion/contiguous gene deletion), Prader–Willi (imprinting), Rett (transcriptional derepression), Lesch–Nyhan (classic Mendelian X-linked recessive).
- **Teratogenic (prenatal):** alcohol (best studied), retinoids, anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, valproate, lithium), SSRIs; without gross malformation but neurotoxic: lead, heroin, methadone.
- **Timing:** embryogenesis (days 14–60), craniofacial, neural and organ malformations; foetal period: behavioural and cognitive effects without physical abnormality; effects are stage-specific and dose-dependent (the same alcohol dose that malforms during embryogenesis may "only" alter behaviour in the foetal period).
- **Shared developmental pathways:** HOX regulatory genes govern face, head, hindbrain, heart and thymus (all neural-crest derivatives); retinoids control HOX genes; ethanol competitively inhibits retinol metabolism (shared alcohol dehydrogenase); hence a "funny face" can genuinely signal an abnormal brain.
- **Postnatal environment:** impoverished rearing disproportionately harms methadone-exposed children; family instability perpetuates FASD behaviour problems; parents with ADHD or mood disorders may self-medicate with drugs.
- **Acquired:** traumatic brain injury (falls, road traffic accidents); epilepsy from mesial temporal sclerosis, tuberous sclerosis, migrational disorders, tumours.
- **Endocrine:** congenital hypothyroidism, intellectual disability preventable by neonatal diagnosis and levothyroxine.

## 6. What happens in the brain: three short stories

**The transactional story (why development is a two-way street).** The old passive model (the brain matures, behaviour appears) is dead. The active child shapes the responses of adults, which shape the child's brain further. A child with attention deficits elicits more correction and less warmth; that response amplifies disruptive tendencies; the amplified behaviour draws harsher handling; the spiral lands in conduct disorder. Treatment therefore aims at the *interaction*, not just the child: the parent's response, the chapter insists, is a critical element in outcome.

**The phenotype story (genes to personality).** Nyhan's 1972 insight: some genetic syndromes produce behaviour so characteristic it suggests the syndrome, behaviour as a visible readout of neuroanatomy. Fragile X boys avert gaze and act hyperactive with autistic-like patterns; Williams syndrome children are hypersocial and hyperverbal with striking visuospatial deficits; Lesch–Nyhan children compulsively self-injure; Prader–Willi children are hyperphagic with obsessive-compulsive features; Down syndrome shapes the pattern of language difficulty. Non-Mendelian inheritance (imprinting, triplet repeats, microdeletions) explains how single mutations yield rich behavioural syndromes, especially when the affected protein regulates many downstream genes.

**The excitability story (epilepsy's psychiatric chemistry).** Seizures arise from an imbalance between inhibition (GABA) and excitation (glutamate). The immature brain is *more* vulnerable (fewer high-affinity GABA-A receptors, larger excitatory postsynaptic potentials) which is why epilepsy's incidence peaks in childhood. Recurrent seizures then disturb the very functions psychiatrists assess: affect, memory, perception and behaviour. The psychiatric task is recognising when odd behaviour is electrical, not psychological, and when both are present, since children with epilepsy carry psychiatric diagnoses at high rates.

## 7. Symptoms: the clinical pictures

**Neurodevelopmental disorders as systems:** ADHD investigated as a prefrontal executive disorder; autism as a disorder of social cognition and communication, the disorders inform brain science as much as it informs them. Interrelations matter: Tourette's with ADHD carries the disruptive behaviour and social dysfunction ("TS alone" has a different, milder social-emotional profile); OCD symptoms may be part of "pure" Tourette's.

**Neurogenetic behavioural phenotypes:** gaze aversion, hyperkinesia and autistic-like behaviour (fragile X); sociability, hyperverbalness, visuospatial deficits (Williams); compulsive self-injury and aggression (Lesch–Nyhan); hyperphagia and compulsive behaviour (Prader–Willi); language profile (Down). Personality phenotypes (five-factor assessment) differ across syndromes and relate to parental behaviour and family context. Isolated special abilities (calculation, music) may themselves be phenotypes, suggesting modular brain organisation.

**Foetal alcohol spectrum disorder.** Full syndrome: prenatal and postnatal growth deficiency, microcephaly, infantile irritability, mild-to-moderate intellectual disability, characteristic facies; ~half have coordination problems, hypotonia and attention deficits; 20–50% have eye, ear and cardiac anomalies. The wider spectrum (alcohol-related neurodevelopmental disorder) without growth retardation or anomalies: attention problems, disruptive behaviour, slow processing, clumsiness, speech disorders, fine motor impairment, and maths-specific learning problems. Even average-IQ children show deficits in complex attention, verbal learning and executive functioning. Behavioural phenotype: poor abstraction, difficulty grasping cause-and-effect and generalising, impaired judgement, impulsivity, vulnerable to later oppositional and conduct diagnoses. Natural history: CNS effects persist for life, ~50% function as intellectually disabled; in non-disabled adults the commonest structured-interview diagnoses are alcohol/drug dependence, mood disorders and personality disorders (passive-aggressive, antisocial).

**Gestational substance exposure.** Opiates: neonatal withdrawal to variable long-term outcomes; methadone exposure raises vulnerability to poor parent-infant relationships; monitor the relationship. Cocaine: reduced gestational age, birth weight, head circumference; genitourinary, cardiac, CNS and limb-reduction anomalies (interrupted blood supply); at 4 years, IQ effects were not demonstrated but specific cognitive impairments were, and a better home environment equalled the scores of non-exposed children; irritability and impulsivity diminish with behavioural intervention. Child abuse tracks substance abuse closely.

**Epilepsy: the psychiatric shapes.** Complex partial seizures (temporal/frontal) are where neurology and psychiatry overlap most: automatisms, perceptual alterations, affect and memory changes, distorted thinking, hallucinations. Frontal-lobe epilepsy: brief sudden unresponsiveness with preserved consciousness understanding, facial/arm clonic phenomena, laughing/crying, pedalling, sexual automatisms; sexual disinhibition, pressured tangential speech, screaming, aggression, disorganised behaviour and nightmares in children; a normal EEG does not exclude it. Lennox–Gastaut: onset 1–7 years, intractable mixed seizures, slow spike-wave EEG, ~half intellectually disabled, marked language delay, overactivity, irritability; behaviour may improve with seizure control; prolonged minor status (weeks of social unresponsiveness, aggression, reduced articulation with minor twitching) must be distinguished from psychiatric regression. Landau–Kleffner: late-onset language disorder. Tuberous sclerosis: infantile-onset cognitive impairment and autistic regression linked to epilepsy.

**Traumatic brain injury.** Most common long-term outcomes: verbal memory impairment persisting up to 10 years; behavioural disinhibition after severe closed injury (Rutter's classical finding); new psychiatric disorder in a majority of severe-injury children *including over half of those without premorbid disorder*; frontal injuries predict the behavioural profile. Level of consciousness, somatic injury and length of post-traumatic amnesia index severity. The paradox: children under 7 fare *worse* than older children despite plasticity; the developing brain is not a regenerating one.

## 8. Diagnosis

**The behavioural-phenotype gateway.** Any child with intellectual disability plus a distinctive behavioural or personality signature deserves a genetics-informed look: dysmorphic features, the five phenotype syndromes, and the inheritance modes (family history pattern: X-linked in Lesch–Nyhan and fragile X; imprinting clues in Prader–Willi). Neurogenetic assessment plus behavioural/neuropsychological/brain-imaging correlation is the modern pathway from genes to cognition.

**FASD assessment.** Ask systematically about alcohol in every pregnancy history (the chapter's core prevention point: no agreed safe dose exists; binge patterns with high peaks are most deleterious; late-pregnancy use mainly causes prematurity and small-for-dates). Examine growth, head circumference, facies, heart; test cognition, attention, executive function and arithmetic specifically, the disabilities live at average IQs too.

**Epilepsy differentiation: the psychiatric skill set.** Epilepsy is a clinical, not laboratory, diagnosis; errors come from inadequate history. Sleep arousal disorders can mimic seizures. Frontal vs temporal complex partial seizures: frontal shows amnesia disproportionate to consciousness loss, tonic posturing and pedalling, partial awareness, contralateral head/eye deviation; temporal shows oroalimentary and hand automatisms with looking around. Sensory/gustatory/olfactory hallucinations in frontal epilepsy must be separated from schizophrenia and mania. **Pseudoseizure differentiation** (children with pseudoseizures commonly also have true seizures): occur only when observed; gradual onset; uncontrolled flailing rather than rhythmic flexion-extension; histrionics; painful stimuli avoided; sudden cessation with immediate alertness; no paroxysmal EEG discharge, and video-EEG (sometimes with depth electrodes) settles difficult frontal-lobe cases. Remember: emotional dysphoria can precipitate *true* seizures, so the distinction is not "real vs psychological" but electrical vs non-electrical.

**TBI workup.** Neuropsychological profiling beyond IQ (visuomotor integration, learning disability at low-average ability); document post-traumatic amnesia duration (PTA ends when new memories form); monitor for new psychiatric disorder after even "mild" injuries in the young.

## 9. Management

**FASD: prevention is the treatment.** No safe dose in pregnancy: women pregnant or planning pregnancy should abstain; educate women of childbearing age; refer identified children early for educational services. Management: parental acknowledgement of aetiology (with treatment of the parent's alcohol misuse as indicated); counselling on the physical and behavioural phenotype; special educational programmes; behavioural management; family therapy; treatment of comorbid ADHD, disruptive and mood disorders.

**Gestational substance exposure.** Attend to the postnatal rearing environment as much as the prenatal insult: treat parental substance use and psychiatric disorder (including self-medicating ADHD and mood disorders); early intervention, special schooling, behavioural programmes, structured day programmes, ongoing parent training.

**Epilepsy.** Seizure control is behavioural control in Lennox–Gastaut; psychosocial support of the whole family; behavioural and psychiatric comorbidity treated in its own right; for apparent psychiatric presentations of seizure disorders (minor status, frontal automatisms), video-EEG before psychotropic escalation. Modern neuroimaging (high-resolution MRI identifying mesial temporal sclerosis, tuberous sclerosis, migrational disorders, small tumours) and surgical options (callosotomy, hemisphere procedures for catastrophic seizures) belong in the referral network.

**TBI.** Early rehabilitation; family education about disinhibition and memory; school reintegration planning; long-horizon follow-up (effects measurable a decade later).

**The universal ingredient.** Across all six domains, the chapter repeats one management law: **the parent's response, adjustment and involvement in treatment is a critical element in outcome**; parent guidance is not an adjunct to treatment, it is treatment.

## 10. Indian practice

- **Epilepsy's double burden.** Beyond the psychiatric comorbidity documented in the British survey, Indian children with epilepsy face school expulsion, marriage-market discrimination and faith-healing circuits. The practical programme: diagnosis confirmation (the clinical-diagnosis rule), family psychoeducation, school liaison letter (child is not "possessed", not contagious, can study), and explicit comorbidity screening at each visit.
- **The FASD blind spot.** Indian antenatal booking rarely asks about alcohol systematically, and local, traditional alcoholic preparations go unmentioned. The prevention package is cheap: ask, advise abstinence, educate, with sensitivity where partner alcohol use complicates household dynamics.
- **Congenital hypothyroidism.** Where newborn screening is unavailable, the clinical fallback (prolonged jaundice, large fontanelle, macroglossia, umbilical hernia) must trigger TSH testing; every month of delay costs IQ points, and levothyroxine is on every essential-medicines list.
- **Lead.** Indian exposure routes (paint, batteries, contaminated water, traditional cosmetics like surma) justify a low threshold for lead levels in developmental delay with anaemia and irritability.
- **TBI rehabilitation.** Road-traffic-injury survivors need the school-and-family reintegration package; disability certification and the RPwD Act's educational entitlements (concessions, scribes) are practical Indian tools.
- **Costs (approx 2026).** Genetic testing (karyotype to microarray) ranges from a few thousand rupees in public institutions to far more privately; antiepileptics and levothyroxine are inexpensive; the scarce resources are developmental paediatrics, child neuropsychology and epilepsy monitoring beds; plan referrals realistically.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: The "behaviour problem" that was electrical.** A 7-year-old boy is referred for "sudden aggression and daydreaming" at school: brief episodes of unresponsiveness with lip-smacking and fumbling of the hands, post-episode confusion, plus worsening night fears. His teacher's letter requests "treatment for ADHD". Careful history: episodes last under a minute, begin abruptly, interrupt mid-sentence, and occurred twice during the interview, drowsy afterwards for several minutes. EEG: temporal discharges. Complex partial epilepsy: behaviour settles with carbamazepine, and the "ADHD" letter is withdrawn. Teaching points: abrupt-onset, brief-duration, post-episode drowsiness and automatisms point electrical; the misdiagnosis route (inattention → ADHD) is exactly the chapter's warning; treat the comorbid night fears explicitly rather than assuming they will vanish.

**Case 2: The maths problem and the missed questions.** A 9-year-old in a Ludhiana school fails repeatedly in arithmetic, is restless, disinhibited, and "doesn't learn from punishment". No dysmorphism recorded. Your pregnancy history (asked systematically, privately, with the mother alone) reveals binge alcohol exposure in the first trimester before the pregnancy was known. Examination: thin upper lip, small head circumference at the third centile, coordination difficulty. Formulation: foetal alcohol spectrum disorder (alcohol-related neurodevelopmental disorder); the arithmetic-specific learning difficulty, abstraction deficits and impulsivity are the phenotype, not "laziness". Management: parent counselling naming the cause without prosecution; educational assessment with maths-specific remediation; structured behavioural programme; ADHD comorbidity treated with medication if needed; sibling-relevant prevention counselling for future pregnancies. Teaching points: the disabilities live at normal IQ; the diagnosis hides behind unasked questions; "does not learn from consequences" is a neuropsychological sign in this group, not a character verdict.

## 13. FAQ

**"Was it something I did?"**: Parents ask this about every developmental disorder. The honest answer: genes, timing and biology shaped the wiring; your responses now are the strongest lever you control, and blaming yourself wastes the energy the child needs.
**"Can a genetic test explain his behaviour?"**: Sometimes specifically (fragile X, Prader–Willi), often partially, sometimes not yet. The behavioural phenotype itself (how he socialises, eats, worries) is diagnostic information even without a test.
**"I drank before I knew I was pregnant, is the baby harmed?"**: The exposure window and amount matter; risk rises with binge-pattern drinking in early pregnancy. There is no safe established dose, so the advice now is abstinence, and a developmental check with attention to arithmetic, attention and abstraction, not panic.
**"Is epilepsy a mental illness?"**: No: it is an electrical disorder of the brain with high rates of emotional and behavioural difficulties attached. Both deserve treatment; neither is shame.
**"He wasn't even knocked out, can a mild head injury matter?"**: Most recover fully, but children younger than 7 are paradoxically more vulnerable, and subtle problems (attention, memory, behaviour) can surface months later; watch, don't dismiss.
**"Will the seizures make her intellectually disabled?"**: Most childhood epilepsy does not; specific syndromes (Lennox–Gastaut, tuberous sclerosis-related) carry much higher risk, and early seizure control improves behaviour and development in several of them.
**"Why does the doctor keep treating me (the parent)?"**: Because your response and adjustment are the best-documented modifiers of outcome in every condition in this note.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **The definitions**: developmental neuropsychiatry scope (six domains); behavioural phenotype (Nyhan 1972: behaviour suggesting a neurogenetic syndrome).
- **Phenotype matching (short-answer favourite)**: fragile X = gaze aversion + hyperkinesia + autistic-like; Williams = hypersocial + hyperverbal + visuospatial deficits; Lesch–Nyhan = self-injury; Prader–Willi = hyperphagia + compulsions; Down = language profile.
- **Inheritance mechanisms**: triplet repeat (fragile X), microdeletion (Williams), imprinting (Prader–Willi), transcriptional derepression (Rett), gene dosage (Down), Mendelian (Lesch–Nyhan).
- **Teratology numbers**: embryogenesis days 14–60; FAS ~1.9/1,000, FASD-spectrum ~9.1/1,000; ~50% of FASD adults function as intellectually disabled.
- **Epilepsy numbers**: 0.7–1.1% prevalence; 50% begins in childhood; febrile convulsions 3% with 98% benign; 0.7% of UK 5–15-year-olds with excess emotional/behavioural problems.
- **Pseudoseizure discriminators (list-question classic)**: observed-only, gradual onset, flailing, histrionics, pain avoided, abrupt cessation with immediate alertness, no EEG discharge.
- **TBI**: 185/100,000 children, 90% mild, verbal memory deficits up to 10 years, under-7s worse.
- **Indian exam corner**: congenital hypothyroidism and newborn screening; lead in developmental delay; epilepsy stigma management; FASD screening questions in antenatal booking.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**Q1.** The term "behavioural phenotype" was introduced to describe patterns of unusual behaviour that:
- (a) Result purely from parenting style
- (b) Are so characteristic of a neurogenetic syndrome that they suggest the diagnosis
- (c) Appear only after traumatic brain injury
- (d) Are learned responses to seizures

**Answer: (b).** Nyhan's 1972 concept: stereotyped behaviour in sizeable numbers of affected individuals, likely reflecting structural CNS deficits, visible readouts of neuroanatomy.

**Q2.** A pregnant woman binge-drinks during days 20–40 of gestation. Compared with the same exposure in the third trimester, the embryogenesis exposure is more likely to produce:
- (a) Craniofacial and major organ malformations
- (b) Only prematurity and low birth weight
- (c) No effects at all
- (d) Purely behavioural abnormalities without physical changes

**Answer: (a).** The vulnerability window for malformations is embryogenesis (days 14–60); foetal-period exposure more often yields behavioural-cognitive effects without visible anomaly, and late pregnancy mainly prematurity and smallness.

**Q3.** Combined rates of foetal alcohol syndrome and alcohol-related neurodevelopmental disorder reported in a US study were approximately:
- (a) 0.5 per 1,000
- (b) 9.1 per 1,000
- (c) 50 per 1,000
- (d) 1 per 100

**Answer: (b).** FAS alone ~1.9 per 1,000 worldwide; the full spectrum approaches 1%; one of the most common preventable causes of intellectual disability.

**Q4.** Which feature most strongly suggests a frontal-lobe rather than temporal-lobe origin of complex partial seizures?
- (a) Oroalimentary automatisms and looking around
- (b) Amnesia disproportionate to the degree of consciousness loss, with tonic posturing and pedalling movements
- (c) Continuous spike-wave during sleep
- (d) Postictal migraine

**Answer: (b).** The frontal signature: prominent amnesia, bilateral motor phenomena, partial awareness; temporal seizures show oroalimentary and hand automatisms with environmental scanning.

**Q5.** After severe paediatric traumatic brain injury, Rutter's classical finding regarding new psychiatric disorder was:
- (a) Psychiatric disorder occurs only with premorbid disorder
- (b) New psychiatric disorder appears in a majority, including over half of children with no premorbid disorder
- (c) Behavioural disinhibition occurs only in adults
- (d) Outcomes are uniformly good in children

**Answer: (b).** Severe closed injury generates disorder even in previously well children, and those under 7 paradoxically fare worse despite assumed plasticity.

**Q6.** In managing a methadone-exposed infant, the chapter's central clinical instruction is to:
- (a) Focus exclusively on pharmacological treatment of the neonate
- (b) Monitor and support the parent-infant relationship carefully, as exposure increases vulnerability to its impairment
- (c) Arrange immediate adoption
- (d) Avoid all developmental follow-up

**Answer: (b).** Outcome is environment-dependent: the exposed child's vulnerability to poor relationships requires deliberate relationship monitoring and support.
