# Anorexia Nervosa

## 1. In one line + overview

**Anorexia nervosa is a disorder in which a person restricts food to a dangerous degree, driven by an intense fear of gaining weight and a body-image perception that reads even a skeletal frame as "too big", the psychiatric illness with one of the highest mortality rates in medicine.**

The illness presents a paradox that defeats common sense: food is available, hunger is present, and yet the person cannot eat, because eating has become fused with catastrophe ("weight gain = failure = the end of me") and the body's mirror has been re-wired to report "large" no matter what the scale and the ribs say. Anorexia typically begins as something praiseworthy (health-consciousness, exam-season discipline, a New Year diet) and crosses into illness when restriction becomes compulsory, weight becomes the day's scoreboard, and identity collapses into a number. Medicine must take the driver's seat in this disorder: starvation is not a mindset but a multi-organ medical emergency (slow heart, low pressure, fragile bones, electrolyte chaos), and the famous danger is the refeeding syndrome that strikes precisely when treatment begins. The counter-intuitive good news: for adolescents, the best-evidenced treatment is FAMILY-based; parents, blamed for decades by old theories, are the primary therapeutic instrument. In India, anorexia is rising quietly among urban adolescents (girls and, under-recognised, boys), usually camouflaged as "dieting", "sattvic discipline", or exam-fuelled appetite loss, and typically reaching clinics only at medical-complication stage.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Apply the DSM-5 gates: restriction, fear of weight gain, body-image disturbance, and note the deleted amenorrhoea criterion
- Grade severity by BMI bands and recognise atypical anorexia (normal-weight restriction)
- Run the medical assessment: bradycardia, orthostasis, electrolytes, ECG, bone density
- Understand refeeding syndrome: its biochemistry, its prevention, its timing
- Deliver family-based treatment (Maudsley/FBT) as the adolescent first-line
- Use olanzapine judiciously; state why SSRIs do NOT treat the core of anorexia
- Recognise Indian camouflage: sattvic/dieting/exam frames, male anorexia, marriage-market pressures
- Hold the mortality honestly: among the highest in psychiatry, from cardiac and refeeding events and suicide

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Lifetime prevalence ~1–4% in women, an order of magnitude less in men, but male anorexia is real, under-diagnosed, and rising (fitness/six-pack culture replacing thin-ideal for boys). Classic onset: adolescence (peak 14–18), with a second cluster in young adulthood. Mortality: among the highest in psychiatry, standardised mortality ratios several-fold elevated; roughly 5–6% per decade in older cohorts, from cardiac arrhythmia and refeeding catastrophes, medical complications, and a substantial suicide contribution. Comorbidity: depression, anxiety/OCD-spectrum perfectionism, and in the purging subtype, electrolyte-driven emergencies. Course: about half recover well with modern treatment; a minority develop chronic courses that consume decades.

**India.** No national data; clinical-series data from Indian centres document a genuine rise over the past two decades in urban adolescents and young women, alongside the classical under-detection. The Indian camouflage set is specific: (1) dieting-to-fitness frames (the gym-joined teenager who "eats clean"); (2) religious discipline frames (sattvic/fasting practices concealing restriction, read by families as piety); (3) exam-stress appetite loss that never self-corrects after results; (4) the arranged-marriage appearance economy ("reduce for the alliance"); and (5) male anorexia presenting as "gym obsession with fat-phobia" that families read as health. Indian presentations skew toward late medical detection: the adolescent reaching the endocrinologist for amenorrhoea, the gastroenterology referral for "food allergy" self-diagnosis, the fainting-at-school case.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**Biological.**
- Heritability 50–60%+ (twin studies), among the more heritable psychiatric disorders; family aggregation with OCD, anxiety, perfectionism
- Serotonin systems implicated (the anxiolytic effect of starvation itself, restriction as self-medication for an over-tense brain, a powerful explanatory model)
- Puberty's body changes as a biological trigger

**Psychological.**
- Perfectionism, harm-avoidance, obsessionality: the premorbid personality signature
- The starvation-maintenance loop: hunger sharpens food-preoccupation, rigidity and body distortion, the illness's own biology amplifies the psychology (the Minnesota starvation experiment's lesson: starvation produces eating-disorder psychology in healthy men)
- Body dissatisfaction internalised from the thin-ideal (and, for boys, the lean-ideal)

**Social / cultural.**
- Appearance-focused subcultures: gymnastics, dance, modelling, combat-weight sports, long-distance running
- Social-media economies of body content; pro-ana communities (harmful online material to ask about directly)
- Indian context: the fairness-and-thinness marriage market, page-3 culture in metros, "reduce before the wedding" seasons, and exam-culture fusion; the board-year adolescent whose restriction begins as time-saving and ends as illness

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: the weight thermostat set to starvation.** Deep brain systems defend body weight like a thermostat defends temperature. When weight drops, the body pulls every lever to restore it: hunger rises, thoughts fixate on food, metabolic rate falls. Anorexia hacks the thermostat's SETTING: the brain re-defines "normal" as starving, so weight-restoration feels like overheating; eating produces panic as surely as a furnace in July. Understanding this explains the treatment's logic: the re-setting cannot happen verbally; it happens calorically: the weight must be restored for the mind to have any chance of resetting, which is why "treat the weight first" is not superficiality but neuroscience.

**Story 2: the six-hundred-calorie brain.** The Minnesota experiment starved conscientious objectors to study famine; the men (healthy, non-eating-disordered) developed food obsession, ritualised eating, body preoccupation, depression and rigidity. Starvation ITSELF manufactures eating-disorder psychology. In anorexia, this loop is closed: restriction creates the mental state that justifies restriction. This is why psychological work done at low weight skates on ice; the first therapeutic target is nutritional, so that there is a nourished brain to do the therapy with.

**Story 3: the phosphate crash.** When a starved body finally receives food, insulin drives electrolytes INTO cells (phosphate, potassium, magnesium) and serum levels can collapse within 24–72 hours: the refeeding syndrome. The heart, already slowed and myopathic, meets an electrolyte storm at its weakest moment, the historical reason refeeding deaths occurred "as treatment began". Prevention is procedural: feed lower than instinct suggests at first (modest initial calories with careful increases), check phosphate/potassium/magnesium daily in week one, give thiamine before the first meals, monitor for oedema. Respect for this syndrome is the difference between treatment and iatrogenic catastrophe.

## 7. Symptoms

**Behavioural:**
- Restriction (skipping meals, tiny portions, sudden vegetarianism/fasting, "clean eating" rules, eating alone, food rituals, cutting into fragments, slow eating, arranging)
- Weight-checking or mirror-checking; hiding under baggy clothes
- Purging subtype: self-induced vomiting, laxative/diuretic misuse
- Excessive exercise with injury; compulsive stair-climbing ("I must burn it")
- Food shopping/cooking FOR OTHERS while eating nothing (the anorexic chef, a classic sign)

**Psychological:**
- Intense fear of weight gain, persisting under weight loss ("if the scale goes up, something terrible happens to me")
- Body-image distortion: feeling/declaring fatness at low weight; the body's report rejected
- Food preoccupation: recipes, cooking shows, calorie arithmetic
- Anhedonia, social withdrawal, depression (both cause and starvation-product)
- Rigid perfectionism; the "good child" history is almost stereotypical
- Denial of the illness's seriousness: the anosognosia of eating disorders (the illness defends itself)

**Physical (the medical exam findings to hunt):**
- BMI below thresholds; emaciation; lanugo (fine body hair); cold intolerance; dry skin; hair loss
- Bradycardia (heart rate below 50); hypotension with orthostatic drop (dizziness on standing); arrhythmias
- Amenorrhoea (no longer a criterion but still a clinical flag); loss of libido
- Constipation, delayed gastric emptying ("I feel full after two bites", real physiology)
- Osteopenia/osteoporosis: bone density decays within a year of amenorrhoea (the young woman with a stress fracture)
- Oedema during refeeding; Russell's sign (knuckle calluses in purging); dental erosion
- Electrolytes (hypokalaemia in purging), glucose instability, QT prolongation on ECG
- Muscle weakness, fainting (the school presentation)

## 8. Diagnosis

**The logic (DSM-5, paraphrased):** restriction relative to requirements leading to significantly low body weight for age/sex/developmental trajectory; intense fear of gaining weight or persistent behaviour preventing gain (even at low weight); disturbance in the way body weight/shape is experienced, undue influence on self-evaluation or persistent lack of recognition of the seriousness. Severity by BMI: mild ≥17, moderate 16–16.99, severe 15–15.99, extreme <15 kg/m². **Amenorrhoea was deleted as a criterion** (the modern exam fact); some women menstruate at low weight, some amenorrhoeic women are not anorexic. Atypical presentations (all criteria except low weight) sit in the OSFED category, and require the same treatment seriousness.

**The assessment craft:**
- Weigh privately, in gown, after voiding, same scale (the patient water-loads or hides weights in clothing; check for hidden objects)
- Take the weight HISTORY as a curve, not a point: the trajectory reveals the disorder's engine
- The three psychological questions in plain words: "what number would feel safe?", "what happens in your mind when you eat a normal meal?", "when you look in the mirror, what do you see?"
- Ask about purging, laxatives, exercise, and social-media/diet content directly
- The collateral interview (parents/spouse) uncovers the eaten-but-hidden, the exercised-in-secret, the discarded lunches
- SCOFF (the five-item screen: named, not reproduced) for quick screening in general practice

**Medical workup:** ECG (rate, QT), electrolytes including phosphate and magnesium, glucose, renal function, LFT, TFT (differential and refeeding prediction), FBC; DXA bone density when amenorrhoea persists; consider coeliac screen, inflammatory markers, and, when presentation is atypical, the organic-hunt (see differential).

### Differential table

| Looks like | The distinguishing feature |
|---|---|
| Bulimia nervosa | Normal-or-higher weight with binge-purge cycles; fear of weight with functioning weight |
| ARFID (avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder) | Restriction driven by sensory aversion/fear of consequences/low interest, NO body-image or weight-gain fear (the key discriminator, common in autism) |
| Depression with appetite loss | Low mood LEADS; no weight-phobia, no body-image investment; weight regained when mood lifts |
| Organic wasting (coeliac, Crohn's, hyperthyroidism, diabetes type 1 (insulin omission), malignancy, TB, HIV) | No weight-fear; appetite often preserved or increased; objective signs and investigations |
| Sattvic/religious fasting practice | No distress, no body-image fear, culturally sanctioned completion and festival return |
| Obsessive-compulsive disorder (food rules) | Rituals not weight-driven; other OCD domains present |
| Early psychosis's food refusal | Delusional content around food; broader psychotic architecture |

## 9. Management

**The order of operations: medical safety → weight restoration → psychological therapy → relapse prevention.**

**Step 1: the medical bed (criteria for admission):** BMI <15 (or rapid loss), heart rate <40 daytime, arrhythmia/QTc significant, potassium/phosphate disturbances, syncope, refusal to eat or drink, uncontrolled purging, depression with suicidality, failed outpatient. Medical admission handles the vitals, the electrolytes, and the refeeding start, paediatric or internal medicine, with psychiatric liaison; full refeeding stays a specialist job.

**Step 2: refeeding, safe and unhurried at the start:**
- Begin modest (roughly 1,000–1,400 kcal/day in high-risk patients, per protocol-based guidance), advance by 200–300 kcal every 1–2 days as tolerated and monitored
- Thiamine before first feeds; multivitamin; monitor phosphate, potassium, magnesium daily in week 1–2; fluid balance and oedema watch
- Cardiac monitoring for high-risk cases; monitor for refeeding syndrome's clinical signs (oedema, confusion, weakness, arrhythmia)
- Target weight-gain: ~0.5–1 kg/week inpatient, 0.3–0.5 outpatient; the weight curve is the treatment's scoreboard
- For the patient, explain the PLAN honestly: "we will start lower than your fears and higher than your comfort; the oedema you will see is water, not fat, and it will pass"

**Step 3, psychological treatment:**
- **Family-based treatment (FBT/Maudsley model), first-line for adolescents**: phase 1 parents take FULL charge of all eating (plating, supervising, preventing exercise) at home; phase 2 returns autonomy meal-by-meal; phase 3 addresses adolescent development. The evidence is strong, and the message to parents is redemptive: "you did not cause this, and you are the treatment"
- **Individual CBT-E (enhanced) / adult therapies** for older adolescents and adults: the over-evaluation of weight-and-shape is dismantled; regular eating restored; body-image work; perfectionism and mood modules
- Adolescent-focused therapy (AFT) as the alternative when family involvement is impossible
- Care coordination with school/college around graded return (half-days before full days; the re-entry often precedes full weight restoration)

**Step 4, pharmacotherapy, the honest position:**
- **No drug treats the core of anorexia.** SSRIs, well-studied here, fail at low weight (starved brains do not respond) and add little after restoration, the classic negative-evidence exam point
- **Olanzapine 2.5–10 mg** as adjunct: evidence of faster weight-gain and reduced ruminative food-fear; use with counselling about sedation/metabolic sides; the pragmatic Indian inpatient aid
- Treat comorbid depression/anxiety AFTER weight restoration begins (their "symptoms" are partly starvation artefacts, the Minnesota lesson applied clinically)
- Hormone replacement for bone protection: largely disappointing in restoring bone density (weight restoration beats oestrogen, another exam pearl); calcium/vitamin D as standard support

**Step 5, relapse prevention and the long arc:** outpatient follow-up with the weight curve; the written relapse drill (meal-skipping returning, the scale-checking surging, exercise creeping); family watch-brief; transitions to adult services; and honesty that recovery usually takes 1–3 years, with the majority of adolescent-onset cases achieving full recovery, a genuinely hopeful message delivered early.

*India note (approx 2026): paediatric/medical admission (shared-ward attendant model) at district level handles emergencies at low cash cost but refeeding expertise is thin outside medical colleges; the referral default for any BMI <15 case should be a medical-college paediatric/internal-medicine service with psychiatric liaison. FBT is family-time-intensive and free of drug cost; the highest-value therapy an Indian family can deliver themselves with monthly specialist supervision; the NIMHANS-style eating-disorder programmes, tele-supervision, and a couple of metro private ED programmes form the specialist tier. Olanzapine ≈ ₹120–250/month; phosphate/potassium monitoring at district labs ≈ ₹100–300 per set. The Indian barriers worth naming: paediatricians under-referring ("girls diet, send her home"), parents' food-culture guilt, and the exam-season social collusion ("she studies all day and eats little, good child").*

## 10. Indian practice

- **The dieting-to-danger boundary: teach families the checkpoints.** Diet becomes a disorder when: weight falls below a healthy trajectory, fear of eating specific "fear foods" appears, eating becomes solitary/ritualised, exercise becomes compensatory-compulsive, and the person cannot stop restriction even when they agree to. The one-page checklist with these five markers, handed to school counsellors and paediatricians, is the Indian detection system.
- **Sattvic and fasting camouflage.** Genuine religious practice is flexible, social, and distress-free; anorexia's fasts are rigid, solitary, anxious, and defended with the same intensity whatever the occasion. Engage faith leaders where the family's frame is religious; the temple's own traditions (festivals include feasts) can be recruited as the re-feeding ally.
- **The male gym-presenting anorexic.** Boys present with "bulking-and-cutting" cycles, fat-phobia at normal weight, six-pack pursuit with fear of muscle loss, and protein-only restriction. Parents read dedication; the gym is the illness's church. The screening question: "what happens if you miss a workout or eat a pizza?": panic is the tell.
- **Marriage-market weight reduction.** "Reduce for the alliance" seasons precede engagements; the anorexic trajectory can launch from the dietician's pre-wedding plan. Counsel families: no crash programmes for teenagers; watch the same five markers.
- **The exam fusion.** Board-year restriction begins as time-economy and ends as illness; the post-results period (when appetite should return) is the detection window; the child who does not return to eating after the exam ends needs review, not another year of "she is like this only".
- **Parents at the refeeding table.** The FBT framing delivers the single most useful Indian sentence: "the family did not cause this; the family is the treatment." Reframe the joint family from interrogation suspect to therapeutic instrument: grandmother plates, father supervises the meal, the cousin sits with her for the post-meal hour.
- **Doctor-shopping pathways.** Indian families route amenorrhoea to gynaecology (hormone panels), constipation to gastroenterology, and fainting to cardiology; each specialty needs the "check the weight trajectory and the weight-fear" habit; a SCOFF-equivalent screen at each of these doors would catch most cases a year earlier.

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: the board-year girl.** A 16-year-old Bengaluru student began "eating clean" before the boards: cut snacks, then breakfast, then most lunches; her parents approved the discipline. Post-results, restriction continued; by four months later she had lost 12 kg, menstruation stopped, she fainted at assembly, and the school asked for "a full work-up". Paediatric findings: BMI 15.2, heart rate 48, orthostatic drop, lanugo over the forearms. She admitted a "safe weight" 6 kg below current and described a mirror that showed "my stomach as huge". Diagnosis: anorexia nervosa, restricting subtype, severe; FBC, electrolytes (mild hypokalaemia), ECG (sinus bradycardia, QTc borderline) done; DXA deferred. Management: admission to the adolescent medical service (psychiatry liaison); refeeding started modestly with thiamine before feeds, phosphate/potassium/magnesium daily for 10 days (day-3 phosphate dip managed with supplementation), meals supervised with FBT phase-1 parent-plating introduced on the ward; olanzapine 5 mg nocte for the food-terror rumination; parents trained for full home charge at discharge with weekly tele-supervision and a weight-curve chart. At 6 months: BMI 18.2, menses returned, one planned admission-week after an oedema-fright relapse, back at school half-days first. Teaching points: (1) the exam-season origin story is the Indian standard; (2) refeeding's phosphate dip arrives on day 3, ready or not; (3) parents-as-treatment converted a 2-hour-away family into the primary clinical workforce.

**Case 2: the bulking boy.** An 18-year-old first-year engineering student in Pune, gym-obsessed since school, presented with panic attacks; found to be restricting to "protein-only clean eating", running 10 km daily at 5 a.m., a BMI of 16.4, and a fear of "any carb making me fat overnight"; two months of amenorrhoea-like symptoms (fatigue, dizziness, low testosterone on testing). He had hidden the exercise from parents and the food logging from everyone. Diagnosis: anorexia nervosa (male, restricting subtype, severe); the family's first reaction was disbelief ("boys don't get this"). Management: the medical stabilisation and refeeding course; CBT-E focused on the lean-ideal over-evaluation and the "clean" rules (graded reintroduction of fear foods with the therapist); the gym restored on a written schedule tied to the weight curve; family sessions retiring the "he is just dedicated" frame. At 9 months: BMI 19.5, one relapse month during placement season (caught by the exercise-creep early warning), treated with the drill. Teaching points: (1) male anorexia hides in gym-culture; the fear-question ("what happens if you eat a pizza") is the detector; (2) exercise is a purging-equivalent to be prescribed, not banned; (3) placement/exam seasons are the relapse windows for boys as boards are for girls.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way patients and families ask it

**"She eats SOME food. So it cannot be anorexia, right?"** Anorexia is not about eating nothing; it is about eating far below what the body needs, with fear attached. Some food daily can still be a starvation-level intake. The questions are: what weight is her body at now versus her own healthy trajectory, and what happens in her mind when she eats a full meal?

**"We are a food-loving family. How did our daughter get this?"** The illness is not caused by family food culture; genetics loads most of the gun (this is among the more heritable conditions in psychiatry), and culture pulls the trigger somewhere else entirely. The science has moved firmly away from blaming parents, and, in fact, parents are now the strongest instrument of the cure.

**"He is a boy. Boys don't get anorexia."** They do, less often than girls, but with equal danger, and it hides better because everyone (including doctors) thinks exactly this. In boys it usually wears gym clothes: fat-phobia, "clean eating", compulsive exercise with panic at missed workouts.

**"She says she feels fat. She can SEE she is thin in photos. Is she lying?"** Neither lying nor blind. The body-experience system itself mis-reports: the inner felt-sense of the body reads "large" even when the eyes read "thin", and the felt-sense wins in moments of distress. That is why arguing from photographs fails and body-image therapy exists.

**"Why does her heart rate matter? She is just dieting."** Starvation slows the heart as a survival economy; a rate in the 40s is a warning, not fitness. Along with low blood pressure, weakened muscle, and bone loss, this is why we treat the medical body first: the brain cannot be argued with while the body is in famine mode.

**"The doctor said feeding her in hospital is dangerous, how can treating be dangerous?"** Re-feeding a starved body shifts electrolytes into cells in the first days, the "refeeding syndrome", which can strain the heart. It is predictable and preventable: we start feeding at a measured pace, check the salts daily, and give vitamins before the first meals. Respecting this is the mark of a careful unit, not a reason to avoid treatment.

**"She is top of her class, so disciplined. Isn't this just discipline going far?"** The same perfectionism that made her a topper is the soil, but there is a line: discipline serves goals; this illness serves itself. When the rules cannot be bent even for her own health, when her marks and friendships are being consumed by the food arithmetic; that is illness wearing discipline's uniform.

**"Will medicines fix it?"** Honestly: no tablet treats the core of anorexia; that is why meal-support and therapy carry the treatment. One medicine (olanzapine) helps some patients gain weight faster and quiets the food-fear rumination. Other tablets treat the depression and anxiety that travel alongside, once the body is being refed.

**"The fasting is for her faith. Are you against religion?"** Not at all. Faith is flexible and completes itself; this illness is rigid and never finishes. The tradition itself has feasts as well as fasts; we often work WITH the family's religious leader to restore the full calendar, fasting included, health anchored. The distinction we treat is fear masquerading as devotion.

**"Will she recover fully?"** With early, persistent treatment, most adolescents do recover fully; the arc is 1–3 years with bumps. The honest risks we watch (heart, salts, and despair) are why we take it seriously rather than waiting. Your job is the long table (the steady, non-negotiable, loving meals) and our job is the map.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**Gates to memorise:** restriction + low weight; fear of weight gain; body-image disturbance. Severity by BMI (mild ≥17; moderate 16–17; severe 15–16; extreme <15). **Amenorrhoea deleted in DSM-5**: the evergreen one-liner.

**Mnemonic, medical emergency markers "B-B-E-L-Q": Bradycardia (HR<40-50 daytime), Blood pressure orthostatic drop, Electrolytes (K, PO4, Mg), Low BMI (<15), QT prolongation.**

**High-yield facts:**
- Highest (or among the highest) mortality in psychiatry: cardiac/medical + suicide
- Refeeding syndrome: phosphate-hypokalaemia-hypomagnesaemia shift, day 1–3 onset; thiamine first, start modest, daily labs
- FBT/Maudsley = adolescent first-line; parents as treatment
- SSRIs do NOT work for the core (starved-brain non-response); olanzapine adjunct helps
- Weight restoration precedes effective psychological work (Minnesota lesson)
- Oestrogen does NOT rescue bone; weight does
- Atypical anorexia (normal weight, full psychology) sits under OSFED
- ARFID: restriction without weight-fear, the key differentiator
- SCOFF = the screening instrument (named)
- Male presentations hide in gym culture; insulin omission in type 1 diabetes (diabulimia label) is a high-risk subtype worth naming

**Classic traps:**
1. Waiting for amenorrhoea to diagnose (criterion deleted) or for emaciation to refer (atypical anorexia counts)
2. Refeeding at full enthusiasm on day 1, the iatrogenic catastrophe
3. Prescribing SSRIs for the core illness at low weight
4. Missing the medical admission triggers and "talking" a BMI-14 outpatient
5. Diagnosing depression's appetite loss without the weight-fear questions
6. Missing ARFID behind "picky eating" (and vice versa, treating ARFID with anorexia frames)

**Indian exam corner:** "anorexia nervosa (diagnostic criteria and medical complications", "refeeding syndrome) biochemistry and prevention" (the favourite), "family-based therapy, phases", " differentiate anorexia from bulimia and ARFID". Indian-context marks: the dieting/gym/social-media rise in urban adolescents, the exam-fusion and marriage-market frames, and paediatric under-referral.

**Answer: B.** The sensory/interest-based restriction of ARFID lacks the weight-fear engine, different mechanism, different treatment.

**7. Admission criteria for anorexia include all EXCEPT:**
A. Heart rate below 40 with syncope
B. BMI < 15 with rapid loss

**Answer: D.** Stable vitals with family-supported eating fit outpatient management with FBT.

**8. The Minnesota starvation experiment's clinical lesson:**
A. Starvation has no psychological effects
B. Starvation itself produces eating-disorder psychology; restore nutrition before relying on psychological work

**Answer: B.** The foundation of treatment-sequencing: nourish the brain first, then work with it.
