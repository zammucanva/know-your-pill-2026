# Treating Personality Disorders — Psychotherapies, Pharmacology & Service Design

## 1. In one line + overview

**Personality disorders are treatable, but the treatment that works is organised, structured, consistent care built around psychotherapy, with drugs playing a small, honest, symptom-targeted role, and the service design itself acting as part of the therapy.**

The Oxford treatment chapters open by dismantling therapeutic nihilism with follow-up data: borderline personality disorder, assumed lifelong, shows **88% remission over 10 years**, and even over 4 years more than half remit. They then build the treatment architecture: several psychotherapies with randomised-trial evidence (DBT, mentalization-based treatment, transference-focused psychotherapy, schema-focused therapy, cognitive therapy, brief dynamic therapy) all sharing seven common structural features that may matter more than the brand name. Pharmacotherapy gets a cold, clean-eyed audit: no drug is licensed for personality disorder; SSRIs help impulsive aggression; topiramate and perhaps lithium reduce anger; aripiprazole has one encouraging trial; antipsychotics are equivocal; tricyclics and MAOIs are effectively retired. The management chapter's master concept: **an organised plan of care (consistency, constancy, adequate crisis support) improves outcomes irrespective of the specific intervention**, and the commonest management error is failing to notice the personality disorder at all.

## 4. Epidemiology: who actually gets treated, and the natural course

**The remission revolution.** Longitudinal studies cited in the Oxford chapters rewrote the prognosis: over 10 years, **88% of borderline patients remit** (no longer meeting criteria for 2 years); in the Collaborative Longitudinal Personality Disorders Study, over 4 years, more than half of BPD patients remitted (12 months at two or fewer criteria). Acute symptoms (parasuicide, self-injury) remit most readily (and are what trigger referral); temperamental symptoms (anger, distrust, abandonment concerns, emotional instability) resolve far more slowly. Psychosocial functioning recovers more slowly than symptoms. Stone's classic follow-up found 66% recovering well, but taking 20 years, four times longer than modern cohorts, raising the possibility that some older treatments actively impeded natural recovery.

**Who reaches therapy.** Borderline, anxious/avoidant and dependent PDs are treatment-seeking (**Type S**); antisocial, schizoid and paranoid PDs are treatment-rejecting (**Type R**); most trials therefore study borderline inpatients, and pure disorders are rare (only about 1 in 20 borderline presentations is "pure" BPD; four or more comorbid disorders are common).

**India.** Dedicated PD treatment programmes are scarce (NIMHANS and a few centres); most Indian patients are managed by general psychiatrists in mixed OPDs. The natural-course data is the single most useful import for Indian practice: it justifies long-horizon, structured follow-up with honest hope, in place of the oscillation between over-treatment (polypharmacy during crises) and abandonment ("nothing can be done").

## 5. Causes: what treatment targets (the mechanisms the therapies address)

- **Emotional dysregulation** (Linehan's model): biologically vulnerable emotion systems meeting invalidating environments; maladaptive behaviours (self-harm, rage) are both products of dysregulation and maladaptive attempts to regulate it. DBT trains regulation and distress tolerance directly.
- **Mentalizing failure** (Fonagy/Bateman): under stress or attachment threat, borderline patients lose the capacity to interpret minds (their own and others'); actions then feel inexplicable and malevolent. MBT rebuilds mentalizing.
- **Splitting and object-relations instability** (Kernberg): all-or-nothing representations of self and other drive idealisation-devaluation. TFP works through the transference to integrate them.
- **Early maladaptive schemas** (Young): unconditional core beliefs ("I am bad"; "others will abandon me") formed in childhood, defended desperately when challenged. Schema-focused therapy identifies and reworks them (with "limited reparenting").
- **Self-state switching** (CAT model): partially dissociated self-states, triggered by threat, producing rapid switches between intense emotional states and cut-off states; reciprocal role procedures (learned relational patterns) maintain them.
- **Serotonergic under-functioning and impulsive aggression** (biology): the target of SSRI treatment effects on impulsivity.
- **Environmental mismatch**: for Type R disorders, nidotherapy targets the fit between person and environment rather than the person.
- **The general engine**: whatever the brand, the effective therapies share structure, and structure is itself the therapeutic mechanism hypothesis (see section 6).

## 6. What works: the shared architecture (the chapter's central finding)

Treatments shown moderately effective share **seven features**: (a) well-structured; (b) devoted effort to enhancing collaboration; (c) a clear focus; (d) theoretical coherence for both therapist and patient; (e) relatively long-term; (f) a powerful attachment relationship between therapist and patient; (g) good integration with other services. The chapter's startling honest note: since most trials compared structured therapy with *routine care*, it is impossible to know whether outcomes come from the therapeutic orientation or simply from the structure. Either way, **the treatment package is the treatment**, which is why service organisation (section 9) is treated as part of the therapy, not its container.

## 7. The treatments, one by one (with their evidence)

**Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT)**: the founding evidence-based therapy. A manualised CBT adaptation for repeatedly parasuicidal women with BPD, mixing behavioural functional analysis, skills training, empathy, and Zen-derived acceptance. Structure: weekly individual psychotherapy + group psychoeducational skills training (emotion regulation, distress tolerance, core mindfulness, interpersonal effectiveness) + telephone consultation + therapist consultation team. First trial (44 women, ≥2 suicide attempts in 5 years): control patients attempted suicide more, spent more days inpatient (38.8 vs 8.5), and dropped out more (50% vs 16.7%); benefits at 1 year were weaker on follow-up, and depression/hopelessness did not differ. Replications: reduced parasuicidal acts (Turner, Koons; Bohus inpatient adaptation: self-harm down but higher dropout); DBT was *not* better than a 12-step programme for opioid dependence or than UK treatment-as-usual; context matters. Family programme (Family Connections, 12 weeks) improved relatives' burden, grief, empowerment and depression.

**Mentalization-based treatment (MBT)**: Bateman & Fonagy's psychodynamic approach: making sense of actions of self and others in terms of intentional mental states. RCT of 18-month partial hospitalisation vs standard psychiatric care: significant decreases in suicide attempts, self-harm, admissions, medication use, depression; gains sustained at 18-month and 5-year follow-up (though social function remained impaired in both groups); cost-effective, manualised; active components still unclear; independent Dutch replication favourable.

**Transference-focused psychotherapy (TFP)**: Kernberg-school dynamic therapy confronting defences and interpreting transference, focused on the here-and-now. 12-month cohort: fewer suicide attempts, less severe self-injury, fewer hospitalisations, but 1 in 5 dropped out. Head-to-head vs DBT and supportive therapy: no differences on most measures; TFP better on irritability and assault; TFP improved reflective function (mentalization measure) most. vs schema-focused therapy (88 patients, 3 years): SFT showed greater, more prolonged improvement and was cheaper, but the difference is largely explained by TFP's early dropouts.

**Schema-focused therapy (SFT)**: Young's compendium of CBT, object relations and gestalt techniques plus "limited reparenting", delivered intensively (2–3 sessions/week for 1–2 years). Best evidence versus TFP as above; still to be shown superior to treatment as usual.

**Cognitive analytical therapy (CAT)**: maps self-states and reciprocal role procedures; 24 sessions: half of 27 borderline patients no longer met criteria at 6 months; effects may be non-specific; larger trials were in progress at publication.

**Cognitive therapy (Beck/Davidson tradition)**: builds a developmental formulation, identifies core beliefs ("I am a bad, inadequate person"; "others will abandon or reject me") and overdeveloped coping patterns, then tests new beliefs and behaviours. Longer, more directive, and far more attentive to the therapeutic relationship than depression CBT, because the patient's beliefs operate *inside* therapy too. RCT in avoidant PD favoured CBT over psychodynamic therapy.

**Brief treatments for public-health scale.** MACT (manual-assisted cognitive treatment): a 6-session self-harm-focused package (bibliotherapy, CBT/DBT elements, psychoeducation, crisis planning, problem-solving): fewer suicide acts, less depression, 46% cheaper care; the larger replication (N=480) extended cost savings but not time-to-repeat. A 30-session CBT trial in BPD (N=104) benefited suicidal behaviour. **STEPPS**: 20 weekly 2-hour group sessions of psychoeducation and behavioural management plus family/friends, run *adjunctively* to ongoing care: reduced impulsive and suicidal behaviour, improved depression; well accepted in US and Dutch implementations.

**Group psychotherapy**, relationship-management groups (Marziali & Monroe-Blum): equivalent outcomes to individual therapy at lower cost, group therapy is the cost-effective delivery vehicle, with high dropout as the tax.

**Therapeutic communities (TCs)**: Maxwell Jones's democratic model (residents run the community through meetings and shared responsibility; intensive group interaction is the treatment). Cassel Hospital studies: the **step-down programme** (inpatient then outreach) beat both long-term inpatient care and treatment-as-usual across most measures, with gains persisting 5 years; long-term residential care improved symptoms and social adaptation but not self-harm or readmissions. The Menninger experience shows inpatient analytic care can help without pathological regression; the Cassel naturalistic data warn that extended admission may foster dependency and regression. No randomised controlled trial exists for TCs (too complex to control). *(Modern update, flagged as post-Oxford: the democratic-TC evidence base has since been strengthened by dedicated trials and national programmes, cite cautiously.)*

**Nidotherapy**: for Type R (treatment-rejecting) disorders: systematic, collaborative modification of the *environment* to fit the person (from Latin *nidus*, nest). Phases: boundary identification, full environmental analysis, agreed change, monitoring, target resetting. Randomised evidence so far: mainly cost savings through reduced hospitalisation. Early-stage, but a healthy correction to person-fixated models.

**Per-type verdicts.** *Avoidant PD*: exposure-based behavioural and cognitive treatments work; day programmes for severe cases; only 9% rate themselves completely improved: gains are real but partial; comorbid avoidant PD cuts social-phobia remission probability by 39%. *Antisocial PD*: no clearly efficacious psychotherapy; penal-system programmes (problem-solving training, anger management, social skills) show mixed results, enough to counter nihilism, not enough to recommend a brand; ASPD *with* depression does better in substance misuse treatment than ASPD alone. *Paranoid PD*: trust-building first; presence of paranoid PD dims outcomes of treating comorbid disorders. *Cluster C generally*: brief dynamic therapies (Winston) produce moderate gains maintained at 18 months.

## 8. Diagnosis: evaluating the evidence (the exam-logic of treatment trials)

The chapters set out why personality disorder trials are uniquely hard, a ready-made viva answer:
1. **Duration**: change needs ≥1 year of treatment plus ≥1 year follow-up (shorter gains may just be the comorbid mood disorder speaking).
2. **Comorbidity**: pure disorders are 1-in-20 rare; apparent PD improvement may be Axis-I improvement ("state contamination" of personality measurement).
3. **Adherence**: dropout is the diagnosis's signature; Type R groups never enter trials at all.
4. **Outcome measures**: reoffending, symptoms and personality status each confound the others; global measures are unstandardised; a person can also "improve" by finding an environmental niche without changing at all.
Minimum evidential standard: explanatory trial in the pure disorder, then pragmatic trial, randomised, with a suitable control, judged after at least a year.

## 9. Management: drugs and the service wrap

**Drug treatment: the honest audit.** No drug is licensed for personality disorder. In BPD trials: **Tricyclics**, amitriptyline no better than placebo, and notably unhelpful even for depressive symptoms (the borderline "despair" is not depressive anhedonia). **SSRIs**: fluoxetine superior to placebo for aggression and impulsivity (fluvoxamine helped mood only). **MAOIs** (phenelzine beat haloperidol and placebo for anger and hostility) reserve for exceptional circumstances. **Antipsychotics**: haloperidol short-term benefit without continuation benefit; olanzapine across three trials showed no core-symptom benefit (consistent weight gain); **aripiprazole 15 mg/day**: significant improvement on all measures with self-harm reduction in one volunteer trial, worth replication. **Mood stabilisers/anticonvulsants**: topiramate reduced aggression and hostility (the most impressive result, needs replication); valproate and lamotrigine weaker/self-rated signals; lithium's anti-aggression evidence is unsatisfactory. In other clusters: lithium possibly for dissocial anger (unreproduced), carbamazepine for impulsivity; **Cluster A, no trials exist** (mistrust is intrinsic to the condition); **Cluster C: evidence confounded by neurotic-disorder overlap**.

**The APA 2001 guideline critique** (teach this, because it is how not to prescribe): the guideline recommended psychotherapy plus drug treatment assigned by symptom-domain: SSRIs/venlafaxine for "affective dysregulation", SSRIs for "impulsive-behavioural dyscontrol", low-dose neuroleptics for "cognitive-perceptual symptoms". The Oxford authors' verdict: these domains are **pseudo-diagnoses created to justify drugs**; the subgrouping has no evidence; the final claim that choice "can be guided as much by tolerability and safety as by symptom presentation" applies equally well to placebo, "whose tolerability and safety are unparalleled". Effect sizes are small; publication bias and advertising-recruited volunteers inflate apparent benefit.

**The practical prescribing package (from the management chapter):** organised plan of care first; consider intermittent low-dose haloperidol or aripiprazole for worsening impulsivity; a time-limited topiramate trial for anger; SSRIs for impulsivity, each as a **4–8-week trial with a clear plan to review or stop**; weigh prescribing risk against benefit; **polypharmacy increases risk with no evidence of benefit**; ensure consent capacity, especially for unlicensed use. Three options now carry a **negative risk-benefit ratio: behaviour therapy alone, tricyclics, and MAOIs.**

**Service organisation (treatment in its own right).** Three models: *sole practitioner*, *divided function* (split responsibilities among trained staff), *specialist team* (dedicated PD service). The conclusion: **divided-function or specialist-team models are probably best for risk and outcomes**. Principles that override model: **consistency** (few key workers, good communication, patients detect inconsistency acutely and use it defensively), **constancy** (avoid staff changes; therapist changes re-enact loss), **adequate inpatient access during crises** (the belief that PD patients should be kept out of hospital is opinion, not evidence; comorbid patients with hospital-oriented programmes did *better*; yet long admissions risk regression, hence crisis-short admissions). England was the first country to create dedicated PD services.

**The comorbidity warning, the commonest management error:** failing to recognise personality disorder when an Axis-I disorder dominates the presentation. Hunt for it in emergency psychiatric clinics, homeless-mental-illness services, heavy service users, and patients with multiple admissions, and adjust treatment plans, expectations and follow-up accordingly.

## 10. Indian practice

- **The organised plan beats the perfect drug.** In a system with few PD specialists, the deliverable core is: one named clinician, a written crisis card (whom to call, what to try first, the MACT logic), scheduled follow-up rather than crisis-driven attendance, and 4–8-week time-limited drug trials with explicit review dates. This package captures most of what the trials actually demonstrate.
- **Prescribing culture check.** Indian OPDs see polypharmacy as the default response to recurrent crises, the Oxford chapters' verdict (no benefit, added risk, unlicensed use) should be quoted verbatim in teaching. The SSRI-impulsivity and topiramate-anger evidence supports *one drug at a time, briefly, reviewed*.
- **Admission policy.** Indian families often request admission during crises; the chapters support brief crisis admissions with planned discharge over either blanket refusal ("she is just personality") or indefinite stays (regression and dependency).
- **What is realistically available.** DBT-informed groups at a few centres; individual structured follow-up everywhere else; CAT and schema concepts usable within generic psychotherapy training; nidotherapy, the idea of engineering the environment (job changes, hostel options, family renegotiation), travels well to Indian joint-family settings where environmental levers are real and cheap.
- **Family work.** Family Connections evidence (burden, grief, empowerment improve) maps onto Indian family-carried care; psychoeducation for relatives is the highest-yield low-cost intervention available.
- **Costs (approx 2026).** Generic SSRIs are inexpensive; topiramate and aripiprazole are affordable in Indian pharmacies; the real economic gains in the trials were reduced admissions, achievable in India through planned crisis pathways rather than new technology.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: The revolving-door polypharmacy.** A 25-year-old woman with BPD has had 11 admissions in 3 years and is currently on four psychotropics (two antidepressants, an antipsychotic, a benzodiazepine) accumulated crisis by crisis. Each admission added a drug; none was ever stopped. Management rebuild: one consultant named as responsible clinician (consistency); medications consolidated to a single SSRI trial targeting impulsivity with a written 8-week review-and-stop plan (polypharmacy reversal); a crisis card with Tele-MANAS and clinic numbers; scheduled fortnightly brief appointments replacing admissions as the default response; family session explaining the diagnosis as treatable, with the natural-course data quoted verbatim (88% remission over 10 years); referral to the hospital's weekly DBT-informed skills group. Over the next year: two brief admissions (both <7 days, planned discharge), no new prescriptions, first self-reported "stable month". Teaching points: the admission-medication spiral is the illness's iatrogenic shadow; the organised plan is the active ingredient; hope with numbers is a clinical tool.

**Case 2: The treatment-rejecting brother.** A 34-year-old man with dissocial personality disorder is brought by exasperated parents after a third job loss; he refuses "any therapy, any tablets: this is who I am". Type R disorder meets nidotherapy logic: instead of insisting on treatment, the team works on fit; renegotiating his role in the family business (structured, solitary work with clear targets), moving him out of the joint household's daily friction into a nearby rented room paid by earnings, and coaching the parents out of the criticism-escalation cycle. Six months later: no incidents, employment sustained, one voluntary visit ("to discuss the sleep problem"). Teaching points: treatment-rejecting disorders are not abandonments; environmental engineering is a legitimate, evidence-tinged strategy; the voluntary OPD visit that follows a fit-change is the entry point that force never created.

## 13. FAQ

**"Can personality disorder really be treated?"** Yes: follow-up studies show most people with borderline PD stop meeting criteria within years, and structured psychotherapies accelerate that. What treatment cannot promise is a different temperament; the aim is a life that works with the personality you have.

**"Which therapy is the best?"** Honest answer: no single winner. DBT has the strongest evidence for self-harm; MBT and TFP show sustained dynamic gains; schema therapy beat TFP in one trial; and all effective programmes share the same structural features. Choose by availability, fit, and the therapist's competence, and organise the care around whichever you choose.

**"Will medicine fix it?"** Medicines treat states within the disorder, impulsivity (SSRIs), anger (topiramate, perhaps lithium), worsening perceptual symptoms (low-dose antipsychotics), for defined trials of 4–8 weeks. No drug is licensed for personality disorder, and combinations add risk without benefit.

**"Why did the doctor stop my three tablets?"** Because the evidence says polypharmacy in personality disorder increases risk with no demonstrated benefit. Each drug that stays must be doing a job you can name and review.

**"How long will treatment take?"** Expect months-to-years, not weeks, and know that the follow-up research shows improvement continues even after therapy ends. A realistic arc: intensive phase first, then short bursts of treatment over a long period.

**"Should she be admitted when she cuts herself?"** Brief, planned admissions for genuine crisis yes; long admissions no: they risk dependency and regression. A crisis card and a named clinician are what prevent the revolving door, not locked doors.

**"He refuses all treatment, now what?"** Then change what can be changed: the environment (nidotherapy logic). A better person-environment fit reduces harm even when the person stays the same, and sometimes opens the door to voluntary care later.

**"Our whole family is exhausted."** That exhaustion is treatable territory: family programmes demonstrably reduce burden, grief and depression in relatives. Supporting the family is treating the patient.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **The number pair**: 88% 10-year remission (BPD), the statistic that ended therapeutic nihilism; acute symptoms remit first, temperamental ones last.
- **Seven features of effective therapies**: structure, collaboration, focus, coherence, long-term, attachment, integration (a perfect list-question answer).
- **DBT trial numbers**: inpatient days 8.5 vs 38.8; dropout 16.7% vs 50%: memorise both.
- **Effect sizes** (Leichsenring & Leibing meta-analysis): psychodynamic 1.46, CBT 1.00; dynamic gains larger and longer; both effective.
- **Drug verdicts in one line each**: TCAs, no better than placebo; SSRIs: impulsivity/aggression yes; MAOIs: anger only, exceptional use; haloperidol: short-term only; olanzapine, no core benefit (weight gain yes); aripiprazole, one good trial; topiramate: best anti-aggression signal; lithium: unsatisfactory evidence.
- **The quote examiners love**: the APA-2001 domains "apply equally well to placebo, whose tolerability and safety are unparalleled."
- **Type S vs Type R** and nidotherapy (nidus = nest), short-answer gold.
- **Three service models; divided function/specialist team best; consistency-constancy-crisis access.**
- **Negative risk-benefit trio**: behaviour therapy alone, tricyclics, MAOIs.
- **Indian exam corner**: EUPD management essay structure = organised plan → psychotherapy (DBT-informed where available) → time-limited targeted pharmacotherapy → family psychoeducation → crisis card; quote the polypharmacy warning.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**Q1.** Over a 10-year follow-along period, approximately what proportion of patients initially diagnosed with borderline personality disorder achieve 2-year remission?
- (a) 25%
- (b) 50%
- (c) 88%
- (d) 100%

**Answer: (c).** The McLean Study data overturned the "lifelong" assumption, though temperamental symptoms (anger, distrust) lag behind the acute ones (self-harm).

**Q2.** Which set of features characterises ALL psychotherapies found effective for personality disorder in the Oxford review?
- (a) Brief, focal, manual-light, therapist-neutral
- (b) Well-structured, collaborative, clearly focused, theoretically coherent, relatively long-term, attachment-promoting, integrated with other services
- (c) Strictly inpatient, purely behavioural, single-therapist
- (d) Unstructured, patient-led, crisis-driven

**Answer: (b).** The seven shared features: possibly more decisive than the therapy brand, since most trials compared structured treatment with routine care.

**Q3.** In the first DBT randomised trial, compared with treatment as usual, DBT patients showed:
- (a) More suicide attempts and more inpatient days
- (b) Fewer suicide attempts, fewer inpatient days (8.5 vs 38.8), and less dropout (16.7% vs 50%)
- (c) No differences on any behavioural measure
- (d) Improvement in depression and hopelessness only

**Answer: (b).** The behavioural and service-utilisation outcomes drove DBT's adoption; notably, depression and hopelessness did not differ.

**Q4.** The Oxford chapter's dispassionate verdict on the APA 2001 guideline's pharmacotherapy domains ("affective dysregulation", "impulsive-behavioural dyscontrol", "cognitive-perceptual") is that they:
- (a) Are well-validated BPD subtypes
- (b) Were proven in large independent trials
- (c) Are pseudo-diagnoses created to justify particular drugs, and the tolerability argument applies equally to placebo
- (d) Should be adopted universally

**Answer: (c).** The critique is a model of evidence-based scepticism; the subgroups were constructed to match available drugs, not observed in nature.

**Q5.** A patient with dissocial personality disorder refuses all psychotherapy and medication. Which management strategy was developed for precisely this situation?
- (a) Force admission to a therapeutic community
- (b) Nidotherapy: systematic collaborative modification of the environment to fit the person
- (c) Long-term benzodiazepines for anger
- (d) Behaviour therapy alone

**Answer: (b).** Nidotherapy targets person-environment fit for treatment-rejecting (Type R) disorders, and benzodiazepines invite disinhibition; behaviour therapy alone carries negative risk-benefit.

**Q6.** Which combination represents the three management principles for personality disorder services?
- (a) Polypharmacy, long admission, rotating staff
- (b) Consistency, constancy of personnel, adequate crisis inpatient access
- (c) Crisis-only contact, single-drug policy, no family involvement
- (d) Specialist-only care, no crisis beds, annual review

**Answer: (b).** The organised plan (consistency, constancy, crisis access) improves outcomes irrespective of the specific intervention; the opposites of each option are documented management errors.
