# Psychotherapy with Couples — Behavioural-Systems Therapy and the Schools Around It

## 1. In one line + overview

**Couple therapy treats the relationship as the patient, through four major schools (psychodynamic, behavioural, cognitive, systemic) and a pragmatic Maudsley-bred hybrid (behavioural-systems therapy) that moves along a hierarchy of interventions as the couple's rigidity demands.**

The Oxford chapter (Crowe) opens with the sociology: a marriage institution in crisis, falling marriage rates, divorce reaching 40% of UK marriages by 1996, cohabiting and same-sex partnerships (needing to be *stronger* than average to survive homophobia), multicultural realities including explicitly noted **arranged-marriage preferences in families from the Indian subcontinent**. Then the schools: psychodynamic couple therapy (internal blueprints, projections and shared defences, powerful ideas, thin evidence); **behavioural marital therapy** (low positive reinforcement and mutual coercion corrected through reciprocity negotiation and communication training, the trial-backed core); cognitive/rational-emotive approaches (the couple's automatic negative thoughts, "intolerable" softened to "difficult to accept"); systems therapy (enmeshment, circular causality, genograms, paradox); and the eclectic combinations. The chapter's own method (**behavioural-systems couple therapy**, developed at the Maudsley) is then taught in full: the **Alternative Levels of Intervention (ALI) hierarchy** from reciprocity negotiation (for flexible, motivated couples) up through communication training, induced arguments, tasks and timetables, and paradox (for rigid, symptomatic, individually-focused couples); the session craft (decentring, the ending "message", the split-team intervention); indications including depression, morbid jealousy and psychosexual problems; and an honest evidence appraisal: behavioural components proven, systemic components less so, the combination "likely to be effective" but untried as a package.

## 4. The core ideas

### 4.1 The schools

**Psychodynamic couple therapy** (Dicks and the Tavistock tradition; Daniell; Clulow): each partner carries an **internal blueprint** of self and other, formed by early attachments, blueprints that shape partner choice and the couple's response to stress. **Projections** attribute split-off disowned characteristics (hostility, sadism) to the partner; the relationship accumulates **shared fantasies and defences**. Four premises govern therapy: emotional health is the capacity to hold internal conflict and external stress (fear with trust, pain with pleasure); significant relationships resurrect *and can change* inflexible old patterns; unconscious processes must be understood; change takes time, aided by transference interpretations involving both partners. The format: one therapist or two co-therapists (whose own interactions sometimes carry the couple's projections, understood in joint supervision). **Wile's critiques**: the approach paints an unflattering picture (dependence, narcissism, sadism, exploitation) that saps motivation, and treats hypothetical constructs (defences, projections) as though they were observed behaviour, while controlled-trial evidence remains scarce and gains undramatic.

**Behavioural couple therapy** (Stuart, Liberman; 1969): no internal mechanisms assumed; couples in difficulty give each other low positive reinforcement or use punishment and negative reinforcement to coerce. The remedy: **reciprocity negotiation** (complaints → requests → mutually agreed tasks, linked and reciprocal, everyday and practical) and **communication training** (direct, unambiguous speech about feelings, plans and perceptions, with feedback of what was heard). Many controlled trials; **proven efficacy**: the evidence anchor of the field.

**Cognitive and rational-emotive approaches** (Beck: *Love Is Never Enough*; Ellis): the communication of disturbed couples shows the depressed patient's cognitive faults, misunderstandings, generalisations, untested assumptions, automatic negative thoughts. Beck challenges assumptions, relaxes absolute rules, refocuses on positives; Ellis re-engineers language: "intolerable" → "difficult to accept", demands → desires, and analysis of the repetitive cognitive-behavioural cycles in which each partner attributes negative motive and assumes nothing can change.

**Systems therapy** (Minuchin, Haley, Selvini Palazzoli): the couple as a system. **Enmeshment**: excessive involvement in the other's private business; the distance conflict ("one wants closer than the other") treated by negotiating compromise boundaries. **Circular causality**, no single villain: A's actions caused by B's and B's by A's; therapy targets the pattern, not a partner. Techniques: genograms (transgenerational influences), sculpting (wordless positioning of relationships), family myths; the active methods: creating conflict in-session, homework tasks, **paradoxical injunctions** ("continue as you are; it may be protecting you from worse").

### 4.2 The eclectic combinations and the chapter's method

Four named hybrids: Segraves (psychodynamic understanding + behavioural skills); **Weeks' intersystem model** (individual + interactional + intergenerational, with decentring and paradox); Spinks & Birchler's behavioural-systems (behavioural until "resistance" emerges); Berg-Cross's everything-including-theology canvas. The chapter's own **behavioural-systems couple therapy** (Maudsley, 1980s; Crowe & Ridley): behavioural marital therapy (negotiation + communication training) **plus** the systems dimension (systems thinking, in-session structural moves, tasks and timetables between sessions, paradox). Born in psychiatry, it suits couples where one or both partners carry psychiatric problems alongside relationship difficulty, and works as an adjunct to psychosexual therapy. It is **a menu, not a fixed course**: negotiation, communication and structural moves early in the session; tasks, timetables and paradox in the end-of-session message.

### 4.3 The ALI hierarchy: the clinical algorithm

Seven levels, matched to three couple characteristics, **symptoms, rigidity of the system, and individual (rather than interactional) focus**: 1. **Reciprocity negotiation** → 2. **Communication training** → 3. **Inducing arguments** → 4. **Timetables and tasks** → 5. **Paradox** → 6. **Adjusting to the symptoms** → 7. **Ceasing treatment / other therapies**. The more symptomatic, rigid and individually-focused the couple, the higher (more systemic, more therapist-ingenious, less reliant on the couple's stated goals) the therapist climbs. **Prefer the behavioural end whenever possible** (collaboration with the couple's own goals beats the therapist's managerial role) and move up or down responsively: flexibility gained → step down; rigidity or non-response → step up. Paradox and symptom-adjustment are the high-altitude tools for couples who reject the interactional focus.

### 4.4 The session craft

**The first session (assessment and treatment in one)**: stay in control; build rapport with *both* partners without favouring either; maintain momentum and the interactional focus; maximise the couple's experience of changed interaction. The signature move: **decentring** (Minuchin); ask the partners to talk directly to each other while the therapist becomes theatrical producer rather than diplomat: observing the couple's typical pattern, avoiding side-taking, rehearsing the negotiation they must continue at home. Under pressure to be drawn in, the decentred therapist routes questions through the couple ("Could you ask your partner what she thinks about your coolness?"). **Obstacles and remedies**: the monologuing spokesperson (invite the non-verbal partner to comment, circular questioning's cousin, provoking the useful mini-crisis); intractable arguments (put them "on ice"; negotiate everyday house-and-children business instead); the therapist's own good habits (empathic listening slows couple work, decentre instead); empathy-without-side-taking (the skilful art of validating one partner without antagonising the other); intellectual-only communication (help both channels, the committee-couple suppressing feeling; the imbalance of one-open-one-logical partner).

**Ending the session, the message**: next appointment; positive sympathetic framing for both partners (keep them on-side: "we think you have what is basically a good relationship, and you are both working hard"); the level-matched content: negotiated plans (behavioural level), or a task, timetable, or paradoxical injunction (systemic level), or the **split-team message** (one part of the team favours the task, the other "prefers to prescribe the symptom"; injecting ambivalence the couple must resolve); closing positive reiteration (people remember positive statements and link them to the tasks); and where possible a **written copy** of the message for between-session work. The therapy overall: **short-term, 5–10 hour-long sessions over 3–6 months**; developed with one-way screen and live supervision, but fully deliverable in any ordinary consulting room.

### 4.5 Indications, contraindications and selection

**Indicated**: relationship arguments and tensions; the individual patient who spends the therapy hour complaining about the absent partner; health deterioration in one partner following the other's individual therapy; **sexual dysfunction** (desire disparity, sexual phobia: with individual therapy added where childhood abuse survives in one partner); **depression and anxiety** where the relationship exacerbates them, the Leff trial: couple therapy effective and acceptable for depressed patients living with a partner; **morbid jealousy** (conjoint sessions nearly always useful, per De Silva); post-crisis addictions and psychosis (once stabilised: limited aims); **schizophrenia's most useful psychological interventions involve the nearest relative** (McFarlane). **Less amenable/contraindicated**: phobias and PTSD unconnected to the home; the actively addicted or acutely psychotic patient (emotionally unavailable, revisit after stabilisation). **Selection gates**: partner availability and willingness (cultural non-attendance reasons, the chapter names the Indian subcontinent explicitly, usually to be respected, with judicious pressure where the non-attendance *is* the problem); the continuing-relationship question (mediation for better breakups (domicile, children) is a growing cousin of couple therapy); pre-relationship problems (individual therapy may fit better, but one or two couple sessions still map the impact on the partner).

### 4.6 The evidence and training

**Evidence honesty**: psychodynamic couple therapies seldom trialled (short-term forms only), gains undramatic, uncertain value; behavioural couple therapy: many controlled trials, proven efficacy (Baucom's review; Snyder & Wills' behavioural-vs-insight comparative trial; Crowe's own conjoint-therapy outcome study; Emmelkamp's comparative evaluation; Johnson & Greenberg's experiential-vs-problem-solving comparison); the behavioural-systems combination: components proven, **the package itself untried**; "likely to be effective". A self-help version with homework exercises and explanations now exists. **Training**: seminars (couple and family dynamics, human development phases, life events, sexual function, physical illness impacts) plus role play (each trainee playing husband, wife, therapist and observer in turn, building empathy and technique, especially for communication training), observation and supervised practice (live observation → co-therapist → sole therapist behind the screen, with trainer assessment and remediation).

## 5. Why it matters clinically

- **The couple as treatment unit for individual illness**: the Leff trial places couple therapy alongside antidepressants for depression in partnered patients, the psychiatric indication most under-used in ordinary practice.
- **The complaining-about-the-partner patient** is a couple-therapy referral wearing an individual-therapy disguise, recognising it saves years of one-sided history-taking.
- **Sexual dysfunction and desire disparity** are relationship problems with mechanical components and vice versa; the couple format treats the context that maintains the symptom.
- **Jealousy involves the non-jealous partner too**; conjoint sessions are almost always worth having.
- **Stabilised psychosis and addiction** benefit from couple/family-level interventions (McFarlane); the nearest relative is a therapeutic resource, not a bystander.
- **Rigidity is the treatment-planning variable**: the ALI hierarchy converts a couple's presentation into an intervention choice, the difference between floundering and finishing.

## 6. India lens

The chapter names India directly: families from the subcontinent preferring **arranged marriages**, sometimes insisting the couple live with the husband's parents, and the clinician's task of respecting cultural non-attendance reasons while reading them clinically. The Indian extensions: the joint family as the invisible third partner in most conjugal therapy (the mother-in-law is often the system's power centre; the couple's "distance problem" is usually negotiated across three households); the arranged-marriage couple presenting with desire disparity, consummation failure and in-law conflict, exactly the psychosexual-plus-systems hybrid the Maudsley method addresses; divorce's lower frequency but rising trajectory, with mediation-shaped breakups increasingly needed; and the delivery reality: formal couple-therapy services are scarce (Relate-equivalents barely exist), making the chapter's self-help package and the psychiatrist's repertoire-negotiation skills the practical Indian toolset. The decentring technique travels perfectly: in a culture where couples address each other through intermediaries, teaching direct partner-to-partner speech *is* the intervention.

**FAQ**
*"Is this counselling or therapy?"*: Counselling improves adjustment to the situation as it is; therapy aims at radical change in the couple's functioning. In practice the borders blur; what matters is the formulation and the techniques, which overlap across the divide.
*"Whose side are you on?"*: The relationship's. The decentred therapist observes the pattern rather than judging the persons, and the skill of validating one partner without antagonising the other is learned, not innate.
*"My husband refuses to come."*: Sometimes the work waits; sometimes non-attendance *is* the clinical problem worth a careful push; and cultural reasons for non-attendance are respected while remaining part of the formulation.
*"Can couple therapy treat my depression?"*: Yes, where the relationship feeds the illness: the London trial found couple therapy effective and acceptable for depressed patients living with a partner, often alongside, sometimes instead of, medication.
*"How long does it take?"*: The behavioural-systems format is brief: five to ten hour-long sessions over three to six months, each ending with a message that converts the interval into homework.
*"Does it work?"*: The behavioural core is proven in controlled trials; the systemic additions are promising but less tested; the combined package inherits its parents' efficacy without yet having its own trial, an honest verdict.

**Self-test MCQs**

**Q1.** In the ALI (Alternative Levels of Intervention) hierarchy, the escalation from reciprocity negotiation toward paradox and symptom-adjustment is driven by:
- (a) The therapist's theoretical preference
- (b) Increasing couple symptomatology, rigidity of the system, and individual rather than interactional focus
- (c) The number of children
- (d) Insurance limits

**Answer: (b).** The three couple characteristics govern the climb: more symptoms, more rigidity and more individual focus push the therapist toward systemic, therapist-ingenious levels.

**Q2.** "Decentring" in couple therapy refers to:
- (a) The therapist leaving the room
- (b) Directing the partners to talk to each other rather than to the therapist, the therapist becoming theatrical producer rather than negotiator
- (c) Removing the couple from their home
- (d) Hypnosis

**Answer: (b).** Minuchin's technique: it exposes the couple's typical interaction, prevents side-taking, and rehearses the negotiation they must continue at home.

**Q3.** The behavioural analysis of a distressed couple proposes that the partners:
- (a) Have unresolved oedipal conflicts
- (b) Exchange low levels of positive reinforcement or use punishment and negative reinforcement to coerce each other
- (c) Are genetically incompatible
- (d) Lack serotonin

**Answer: (b).** Stuart and Liberman's operant formulation: corrected through reciprocity negotiation and communication training, the trial-proven core of the field.

**Q4.** The Leff et al. (2000) London depression intervention trial found:
- (a) Couple therapy ineffective for depression
- (b) Couple therapy an effective and acceptable treatment for depressed patients living with a partner (compared with antidepressants)
- (c) Antidepressants superior in all measures
- (d) Neither treatment worked

**Answer: (b).** The psychiatric indication: couple therapy treats depression where the relationship participates in maintaining it.

**Q5.** Which couple is LEAST suitable for immediate couple therapy?
- (a) A couple with desire disparity and relationship conflict
- (b) A couple where one partner has morbid jealousy
- (c) A couple in which one partner is acutely psychotic
- (d) A couple where one partner has depression and low self-esteem

**Answer: (c).** Acute psychosis (like active addiction) renders a partner emotionally unavailable; defer until stabilisation, then offer couple-level work with limited aims.

**Q6.** The honest evidence verdict on behavioural-systems couple therapy as a package is:
- (a) Multiple RCTs demonstrate package-level superiority
- (b) Its two component approaches have proven efficacy (behavioural strongly; systemic less so), but the combination itself has not been trialled
- (c) No component has evidence
- (d) Only pharmacotherapy works

**Answer: (b).** "A combination of two probably effective treatment approaches, and therefore likely to be effective", the chapter's calibrated claim.
