# Anxiety Disorders in Children & Adolescents

## 1. In one line + overview

**A child with an anxiety disorder is not "just shy" or "just naughty"; an over-sensitive internal alarm has taken up residence, producing weeks of stomach-aches, school refusal, clinginess at the gate, night fears and relentless "what if" worrying, and the treatment is beautifully effective: gentle, graded facing of the feared thing (exposure) plus teaching the child to out-talk the alarm, with parents coached to stop feeding it by rescue.**

Anxiety is the mind's smoke detector: essential when there is smoke, miserable when it false-alarms. In children it rarely announces itself as "I have anxiety", it arrives in PAEDIATRIC costume: the recurring Sunday-evening stomach-ache, the school-morning headache that vanishes by lunch, the bed-wetting regression, the tantrum at the school gate, the child who cannot sleep alone, the one who vomits before every exam. The anxiety disorders of childhood are the same alarm misplaced in different rooms: **separation anxiety** (alarm attached to being apart from the attachment figure, the commonest in younger children, and the commonest engine of school refusal), **generalised anxiety** (alarm attached to everything worry-able, the school-age worrier), **social anxiety** (alarm attached to others' judgment, the frozen or blushing child), **specific phobia** (alarm attached to dogs, injections, lifts, lizards), and **selective mutism** (the child frozen speechless at school who talks at home, social anxiety's most extreme costume). Left alone, anxiety compounds: avoidance grows (each avoided thing confirms the alarm's credibility), school attendance decays, confidence erodes. Treated (CBT built around graded exposure and parent coaching, with SSRIs for the moderate-severe) most children recover fully; childhood anxiety is one of child psychiatry's genuine success stories. The Indian paediatric OPD sees this child as "recurrent functional pain" for years before anyone asks the fear questions; this note exists to end that wait.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Recognise childhood anxiety's five costumes: somatic complaints, school refusal, clinginess, frozen speech, night fears
- Name the five childhood anxiety disorders and their age fingerprints (separation anxiety earliest, social/selective-mutism at school entry, GAD in middle childhood, panic mostly post-pubertal)
- Explain the three mechanism stories: the sensitive alarm (amygdala threshold), the avoidance trap, and the temperament-plus-parenting transmission (behavioural inhibition and the accommodation cycle)
- Distinguish disorder from developmental normality (stranger anxiety at 8 months is normal; the same at 8 years is a disorder)
- Run the school-refusal differential, the four engines (separation, anxiety-specific, mood, truancy-with-conduct), because the treatment differs per engine
- Deliver CBT the child way: the Coping Cat-style structure (name it, externalise it, boss it back), graded exposure ladders, and the parent module (reducing accommodation, brave-praise)
- Use SSRIs correctly in children: which, at what dose logic, what monitoring (suicidality-class warning in context), for whom CBT-only vs combined
- Handle Indian realities: the paediatric-somatisation carousel, the hostel/boarding question, exam-season spikes, joint-family accommodation loops, and the "just shy" dismissals that cost years

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Childhood anxiety disorders are the COMMONEST mental-health conditions of childhood and adolescence: any-anxiety prevalence roughly 6–10% in mid-childhood, rising in adolescence (15–20% for any anxiety by late teens in some surveys). Separation anxiety is the most common in younger children (roughly 4% in the 6–11 band), specific phobias and GAD across ages, social anxiety spiking at secondary-school entry. Girls carry roughly double the risk after age 6. Age fingerprints matter: stranger anxiety peaks 6–12 months and separation fear 9–18 months as NORMAL development; the same behaviours at school age are disorder. Half of adult anxiety disorders began as childhood/adolescent conditions, the strongest argument for treating early rather than consoling with "she'll grow out of it" (a third do remit; the rest entrench).

**India.** Community data (National Mental Health Survey 2015–16 adolescent supplement; school-based studies) place anxiety among Indian adolescents in the high-single-digit to mid-teens percentage range, with the exam-linked and somatic presentations dominating clinics. The Indian pathways shape the visible picture: the child first tours the paediatrician and the ultrasound-lab carousel for recurring abdominal pain (functional abdominal pain and anxiety travel together, in paediatric functional-pain clinics, anxiety disorders ride along in a large fraction); the school-refusal spike is fed by middle-class academic pressure, one-child families with full parental attention to lose, and the hostel transition (a culture that treats distress as homesickness to be toughened through); joint families supply superb safety, AND superb accommodation: three adults dispatch rescue missions. Detection still routes through the paediatric stethoscope: any Indian clinician seeing "recurrent pain with normal reports, Sunday evenings, school mornings" owns this diagnosis until they have asked the fear questions.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**Temperament / biological**
- Behavioural inhibition (Kagan's construct): the 20% of infants who withdraw from novelty, high-reactive to new faces and sounds, the strongest early childhood predictor of later social/separation anxiety
- Genetic loading: anxiety runs in families (heritability ~30–40%); the anxious parent transmits by gene AND by demonstration
- Amygdala threshold differences: the alarm ships pre-set sensitive; experience then calibrates it

**Psychological**
- The avoidance trap (the maintainer, more than the cause): each escape/rescue lowers anxiety NOW and teaches the brain the thing was dangerous, the cycle that converts a temperament into a disorder
- Informational transmission: parental catastrophising narration, news-fear (accidents, kidnapping stories), teacher threats
- Trauma and adverse events: accidents, medical procedures, bullying, a parent's illness; the alarm gets REAL material to misplace

**Family / social**
- Parental anxiety/overprotection (the "hover" parenting shape): rescue as love, the accommodation cycle (parents adjust the world so the child never faces the feared thing)
- Attachment insecurity adds risk; secure attachment with a calm facing-model is protective
- School climate: punitive teachers, bullying, performance terror; entrance-coaching cultures (the Std 9–12 anxiety engine)
- Loss/illness in the family: separation anxiety spikes after a parent's hospitalisation or death, the alarm protects against the next catastrophe

**Indian context**
- The "shy child" virtue-label: quiet withdrawal is praised as sanskaari; social anxiety is protected from treatment by a compliment
- Hostel-at-8 traditions: forced separations on an unready nervous system; some children adapt, a subset breaks, and the break reads as "weakness", not anxiety
- The joint-family rescue economy: the alarm never faces its lesson because three adults race it to the rescue
- Exam-anxiety culture: board marks as family destiny; panic symptoms normalised as "everyone feels this, push harder"

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: the sensitive alarm.** Picture a smoke detector set to trigger at the faintest hint, burnt toast two flats away. The amygdala is the brain's alarm; in temperamentally inhibited children it ships with a hair trigger. It is not broken; it is over-eager: heart races, stomach clenches (the vagal-gut axis, the actual biology of the Sunday-evening stomach-ache), palms sweat, sleep frets. The felt experience is REAL pain and REAL fear; the absence of "anything on the scans" is the anxiety diagnosis's signature, not evidence of faking. The treatment insight: alarms recalibrate through EXPERIENCED NON-EVENTS, you cannot talk the detector down; you must let it hear toast burn, again and again, without a fire.

**Story 2: the avoidance trap.** Anxiety's gravity bends all behaviour toward escape. The child who fears the classroom leaves it, and feels the wave of relief, the brain's most effective teacher. Each relief lesson deepens the alarm's credibility ("it WAS dangerous; see how good it felt to flee"). Within weeks the avoided list grows: school → specific lessons → the gate → the bus → the morning itself. This is why rescue is the trap's ally and why the SPACE-style parent module targets the family's rescue patterns: the kindest long-run parenting is the short-run tolerance of the child's discomfort.

**Story 3: the calibration classroom.** The alarm's settings are partly inherited, but CALIBRATION is learned from the attachment figures: the anxious parent who flinches at every step teaches alarm; the calm parent who names ("it's just a lift, it grumbles a bit, hold my hand, we're riding it") teaches reset. The inhibited infant with a calm, brave-modeling, non-rescuing parent frequently lands off the anxiety track; the same infant with an anxious over-protecter converts temperament into disorder. Transmission is real, and it is exactly why TREATING THE PARENT (often the same referral's real patient) is standard child-anxiety medicine.

## 7. Symptoms

**The somatic front (the Indian clinic's main door):**
- Recurrent abdominal pain: characteristically school mornings / Sunday evenings, absent weekends and holidays, with normal examinations
- Headaches, nausea, vomiting before school or exams; dizziness, multiple "gas" complaints
- The pattern is the diagnosis: time-linked to separation or performance, gone in its absence
- Sleep: difficulty settling alone, night waking, nightmare era, refusing own bed/room
- Appetite dips on school days; toilet-frequency before tests

**Separation anxiety (the cling engine):**
- Excessive distress at separation (wailing beyond age norm, panic at the school gate, clinging physically)
- Worry about attachment figures' harm ("what if Amma dies while I'm at school"); refusal to sleep apart; shadowing the parent through the house
- Physical symptoms on school mornings; refusal to ATTEND without the parent (the school-refusal engine of the 5–8 age band)
- Regression at separations: bed-wetting reappearing, baby-talk, sleep refusal

**Generalised anxiety (the worry engine):**
- The "what if" chorus about everything: marks, health, parents' safety, world events, weather
- Perfectionism: erasing whole pages, redoing homework, crying over 90%
- Restless, tiring easily, irritability; concentration whittled by worry (misread as ADHD)
- Muscle-tension complaints, jaw clenching, the "can't switch off" description

**Social anxiety & selective mutism:**
- Frozen in performance settings: reading aloud, vivas, assemblies; blushing, tremor, nausea beforehand
- Avoids: raising the hand, canteen queues, birthdays, being photographed
- Selective mutism: consistent speech in comfort settings (home), consistent SILENCE at school/with strangers for 1+ months, not defiance, not shyness: the alarm jamming the voice; often mistaken for stubbornness or hearing problems
- Pre-teen social anxiety announces itself through school refusal too (the "everyone will laugh" engine)

**Specific phobias (the focused alarm):**
- Dogs/strays (the Indian street reality makes this a daily gauntlet), injections and medical procedures (the vaccination meltdown beyond age norm), lizards/cockroaches, lifts, tunnels, dark, thunder
- Immediate intense fear, avoidance architecture (route plans around dogs), distress out of proportion to actual danger

**Panic (mostly post-pubertal):** racing heart + breathlessness + doom, then fear-of-the-fear; see the Panic Disorder note; rare before 12.

**The functional price (the disorder gate):** attendance decaying, grades sliding, friendships shrinking, family holidays impossible (flights! dogs at the resort!), the family orbiting the alarm's rules; impairment is what makes it a disorder rather than a temperament.

## 8. Diagnosis

**The logic (DSM-5/ICD-11 paraphrased):** the disorder-specific symptom patterns, at developmental-INAPPROPRIATE intensity, duration beyond the normal window (weeks-to-months by disorder), with clear impairment; separation anxiety, selective mutism and specific phobia are diagnosable in childhood with the same adult criteria (DSM-5 moved separation anxiety out of the "childhood-only" wing); GAD in children requires the worry + 1 somatic-concentration symptom (vs 3+ in adults, a child-specific gate worth remembering). Severity is gauged by avoidance spread, somatic load and impairment, not by how loud the child cries.

**The assessment sequence:**
1. **The fear interview with the CHILD alone (drawings, sentence-completions, the "worry monster" naming for younger ones) AND the parent-separately account**, children under-report to protect parents; parents over-report somatics; both inform
2. **The timeline mapping**: symptom diary against the school calendar (the Sunday-evening, Monday-morning, exam-week and holiday-vanishing patterns write the diagnosis in dates)
3. **The separation probe**: what actually happens at the gate (the 20-minute wail vs the child who enters fine, different engines)
4. **The school report**: teacher's observation (the frozen-at-desk child vs the texting-parent-on-phone child), attendance record, the objective truth serum
5. **Somatic workup, targeted, not blanket**: the paediatric evaluation for recurring pain (typically already done, often thrice); urine/stool where indicated; NO endoscopy for the time-locked pattern with normal basics; the carousel is a cost, not a comfort
6. **The screens**: depression (the school-referral of a 13-year-old "anxious" child is often depression's anhedonic fatigue), trauma/bullying (the sudden-onset anxiety demands an event hunt), ADHD (concentration complaints), thyroid (the one lab worth drawing in the anxious, weight-losing child), hearing for the "not responding" child
7. **Family map**: who rescues what (the accommodation inventory, which adult does which rescue), parental anxiety screen (the GAD-7/ASRS tier for parents, named), the marriage-stress audit where separation anxiety is intense (the child guarding against a fracture)
8. **Rating scales (named only)**: SCAS/SCARED-lineage child-and-parent versions, tracking tools, not diagnostic oracles

### Differential table

| Looks like child anxiety | The distinguishing feature |
|---|---|
| Normal developmental fears (stranger 8 mo, monster-fears 3–5 y, separation 9–18 mo) | Age-window-appropriate, transient, respond to comfort; disorder requires wrong window + persistence + impairment |
| ADHD (inattention at school) | Attention intact on preferred non-feared tasks; somatic time-pattern; worry content elicited |
| Depression in adolescents | Anhedonia, sustained mood change, sleep/appetite global shift; the "can't be bothered" vs the "too scared to" |
| Trauma/PTSD after a specific event | Onset datable, re-experiencing/avoidance of event-linked cues, hypervigilance; screen every sudden-onset anxiety |
| Bullying-driven school avoidance | Event between child and schoolmates; the "anxiety" is specific and rational; treat the bullying system |
| Learning-disorder school aversion | Fear of the SUBJECT or exposure of failure; oral-bright-print-struggling; see the Learning Disorders note |
| Absence epilepsy (the staring child) | Stereotyped seconds-long spells interrupting activity; no worry content; EEG |
| Autism (frozen at school, rigid) | Social-reciprocity history + sensory profile; selective mutism is the differential both ways, probe both |
| Physically-ill child (the somatic mask) | The targeted workup; anxiety remains in the differential even with disease, they coexist |

## 9. Management

**The frame: exposure is the medicine; everything else is packaging and dosing.** The child's alarm resets only through experienced non-events. The treatment architecture arranges those experiences kindly, repeatedly, and with the family out of the rescue business.

**1: CBT, child edition (the first-line for all severities).**
- The Coping Cat-style package (named; the best-evidenced child-anxiety CBT): FEEL the fear (identify somatic signals), EXPECT the worst (name the thought), ACT brave (the plan), PRAISE the attempt; 12–16 sessions with parent modules
- Externalisation first: the worry monster / the alarm / the "worry bulbul" gets a NAME, the child fights a character, not themselves; worry time scheduled (10 minutes daily, contained); worry journals
- **The exposure ladder, the core tool**: rank feared situations 0–10 by fear; start at 2–3; stay till the wave falls (habituation); repeat; climb. Examples: for school refusal, parent at the gate → parent leaves for 10 min → half-day → full day; for dog fear: puppy video → puppy across the road → walking past a leashed calm dog → patting with owner
- Cognitive work kept age-honest: probability games ("how many times did the monster's prediction come true?"), the detective metaphor, evidence for and against; for teens, full thought-recordings
- Relapse-prevention at the end: the plan-for-the-return-of-the-monster written like a fire drill

**2: The parent module (runs parallel; sometimes IS the treatment).**
- The accommodation inventory → the gradual withdrawal of rescue: the parent who slept on the floor beside the child's bed moves to the chair, then the doorway, then out, one rung at a time with brave-praise each step
- Brave-modeling: naming parental anxiety and handling it aloud ("my tummy is doing the wobble-thing before this meeting; I'll breathe and go anyway")
- Praise-shape: reward the APPROACH and the attempt, never the avoidance-free day alone; attention withdrawn from the alarm's symptoms (the somatic complaints get "I'm sorry your tummy hurts; you're still going": kind and unmoved)
- The school-morning script: short, warm, boring-same-every-day; long goodbyes are the alarm's oxygen
- *India note: run this module with ALL the adults (grandparents included); the programme dies at the grandmother's 6 a.m. rescue mission; one family meeting with the three-generation attendance list is standard Indian child-anxiety work*

**3: School liaison (the third rail of treatment).**
- The re-entry plan for school refusal: gradual (half-days first), a named safe-person and safe-place at school, the no-phone-home rule negotiated with parents, teacher briefed NOT to spotlight the child
- Accommodations: exemption-from-reading-aloud initially (fading later), the assembly plan, the canteen-buddy
- Anti-bullying actions where the hunt found them; bullying is treated as a system event, not a "resilience lesson"

**4: SSRIs (the moderate-to-severe tier).**
- For moderate-severe impairment, or insufficient CBT response: sertraline or fluoxetine (the best-paediatric-evidence members; fluvoxamine has the RUPP separation-anxiety trial history); start low, go slow, expect 4–6 weeks; the suicidality-class-warning conversation held honestly (in anxiety trials the aggregated signal shows benefit exceeding risk, say both sides)
- Combine with CBT where possible (the POTS/combination evidence: combination > either alone in some analyses, CBT alone often sufficient for milder cases)
- Short-course benzos: essentially no role in children (dependence and paradoxical disinhibition), an exam point and a practice point
- *India note (approx 2026): sertraline ₹50–150/month, fluoxetine similar; the liquid formulations useful for younger children; resist the alprazolam-for-exams culture: it teaches chemical rescue to a brain that needs exposure lessons*

**5, Specific packages:**
- Selective mutism: a blend of stimulus-fading (the speech-generalisation ladder across people and settings), shaping (whisper → word → sentence) and the MISC/communication-training approaches, NOT "wait till she opens up" (the untreated course entrenches); SSRI adjuvant for the frozen-in-general child
- Specific phobias: single-session intensive exposure literature exists (adults) but the child ladder version (3–6 sessions) is the practical gold; injection phobias get applied-tension (the fainter's tool; see the Phobias note)
- Exam anxiety: the CBT skills plus practice-exam exposure under real timing; performance psychology basics (routines, breathing, self-talk scripts); school cooperation for mock simulations: with the underlying GAD/perfectionism treated, not just the symptom papered

**6, When to treat the parent TOO.** Parental anxiety disorders undermine the child's programme (the rescue-gene expresses itself nightly); the joint plan, child CBT + parent's own treatment, is the honest architecture; say it without blame ("your alarm taught hers; we now both do the programme").

*India note (approx 2026): child-guidance clinics (medical-college psychology departments) deliver CBT-tier care at low cost; private child psychologists ₹800–2,500/session in metros; school counsellors (increasingly present in CBSE/ICSE schools) are the exposure-ladder's in-situ allies, arm them with the plan rather than bypassing them; the paediatrician is the front door and the somatic-carousel's circuit-breaker: the doctor who says "the reports are normal, the timing is the diagnosis, let's meet the fear" at the THIRD visit saves the family years.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **The paediatric carousel is where these children live.** Indian child-anxiety patients average years of ultrasound, stool and blood carousels before the fear questions are asked. The intervention that changes this: teach the pattern-recognition sentence to every paediatrician and GP; "pain that follows the school calendar is a message about school". The somatic child with normal reports and time-locked symptoms deserves the fear interview in the same visit.
- **The hostel decision.** Indian families ship children to hostels at 8–12 for "discipline and education". For the temperamentally-inhibited, the forced separation entrenches the alarm (and produces the school-refusal, bed-wetting, weeping-for-months cohort that lands in your clinic). The balanced position: readiness is the child's nervous system, not the family's ambition; a graduated boarding plan (weekends home first term, a named house-parent confidant) protects most children; the child with active separation-anxiety disorder gets treatment BEFORE boarding, not boarding as treatment.
- **The joint-family rescue economy.** Three generations of loving adults means the alarm has a 24×7 response team. The Indian parent module runs the whole family or it doesn't run: schedule the Sunday assembly, map who does which rescue (Pati brings the mid-day meal; dadi sleeps beside; mother leaves work for the phone-call), and negotiate the family-wide withdrawal ladder with the grandparents' dignity intact (their job becomes brave-praising, which they do better than anyone).
- **The exam-season national weather.** March in India is a somatic-symptom monsoon for adolescents. The system-level medicine: school counsellors running pre-board exposure workshops (practice papers under exam conditions, timing routines, the relaxation script), parents briefed on the no-countdown-calendar rule, and the message that marks are recoverable and panic is treatable, the individual-level medicine: the GAD and perfectionism under the exam fear gets treated in its own right.
- **The "shy child" praise wall.** Indian culture's approval of quiet withdrawal delays social-anxiety treatment by years; the clinical counter: "shy is a temperament; frozen is a disorder. Your child can stay a quiet person AND be able to read aloud and make one friend; those are different things."
- **The selective-mutism hearing-test detour.** The non-speaking school child tours ENTs first (fine, once), then is declared stubborn. The tell is home-speech: a child who narrates cricket at full volume in the kitchen is not aphasic; treat the mutism as social anxiety's extreme form, early.
- **The mother's own untreated anxiety is the elephant.** In the Indian clinic the child arrives as the index patient with the mother as case-manager; screen her (the two-question GAD tier), and offer treatment; the child's programme's success rate doubles when the household's second alarm gets handled.

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: the Sunday-evening stomach.** A 7-year-old Coimbatore girl, only child of two software engineers, was brought to paediatrics for the fifth time in four months: recurring abdominal pain, Sundays 6 p.m. onward and every school morning, absent Saturdays and holidays; ultrasound twice, stool tests, blood panels, all normal; prescribed antacids, then antispasmodics, then a "digestive tonic". Pain escalated to vomiting by the Std 2 annual-exam week; the school reported she was "otherwise quiet, perfect child"; mother confessed, teary: "she will not enter unless I stand at the gate; the driver says she watches me through the window all morning". The paediatrician, at visit five, asked the fear questions directly. Assessment: classic time-locked somatic pattern; separation anxiety symptoms in full (the gate wail, the window-watching, "what if you die at office" worry, bed-sharing regression since a paternal hospitalisation 8 months prior, the engine's origin), no bullying, learning intact, mother's own GAD-7 elevated (the catastrophising narrator). Diagnosis: separation anxiety disorder with somatic presentation, riding on the grandfather's-hospitalisation trigger, in an inhibited temperament, with maternal-anxiety amplification. Management: the somatic carousel closed (the sentence delivered: the timing is the diagnosis); the school re-entry ladder (mother at the gate → gate-then-depart 10 minutes → the named teacher-helper at the window-seat → full days, over three weeks); parent module: the boring-same-goodbye script, the no-window-watching agreement with the school, brave-praise calendar; the father assigned the morning routine (the mother's alarm-model made her the worse gate-parent; the swap was honest and worked); the "worry monster" externalisation with worry time and the worry-journal; the hospitalisation-story repair session (drawing the visit, naming the fear, the doctor's note that "thatha's operation succeeded; he lives"); mother's own GAD treated (her CBT). Six weeks: full attendance, pain to rare Sundays; three months: no bed-sharing, one gate hug and departure. Teaching points: (1) the paediatric carousel broke at the sentence "the timing is the diagnosis"; (2) the loss-event origin was findable, the 8-month timeline; (3) the window-watching was the maintaining behaviour BOTH ways, the mother's waving made it possible; (4) the father-swap was the Indian family's route around the maternal-anxiety loop; (5) treating the mother was treating the child.

**Case 2: the boy who spoke only at home.** A 6-year-old Ludhiana boy, referred by his school after two terms: he had never spoken in class, not to teacher, not to classmates; answered by pointing and nodding; on the sports field, competent and integrated in play (the tell: social PARTICIPATION without speech). At home: "a full cricket commentary channel", bilingual fluency, age-appropriate milestones, no autism red flags on history (pointed, showed, pretended as a toddler; pretend play rich; reciprocal gaze excellent; no repetitive/sensory cluster). Grandmother's history: "he has been the slow-to-warm one since babyhood, hides behind my dupatta for ten minutes with any guest, then warms." Assessment: consistent home-speech with consistent school-silence beyond 1 month (the selective-mutism gate), inhibited temperament, no hearing loss (tested once, normal), no trauma event. Diagnosis: selective mutism, social anxiety's extreme costume in a behaviourally-inhibited boy. Management: the school-and-family stimulus-fading ladder (the class teacher started with yes/no card games → whisper-games with one friend during play → the "carrier phrase" technique with the same friend in the corridor → speech to the teacher via the friend as intermediary → the mother pre-visited the classroom after hours and he "played teacher" reading aloud in the empty class → the class with three children present → the full class), the no-pressure protocol (no spotlight, no bribe-for-words, communication-ONLY-not-speech goals on paper), home bravery-generalisation practice (ordering at shops, answering the phone, the Indian joint family's daily exposures), teacher's aide trained by the child psychologist (2 visits), and NO medication (severity did not warrant it; the SSRI tier reserved). One year (the honest clock): speaking to the teacher in short sentences, two friends in full voice, class presentations still "the next rung". Teaching points: (1) home-speech tells the diagnosis; the kitchen-commentary child is not aphasic, not autistic on this evidence, and not stubborn; (2) participation-without-speech is the selective-mutism signature that separates it from autism's withdrawal; (3) the ladder works through FRIENDS first; peer speech returns before adult speech; (4) the honest timeline (months-to-a-year) protects the family from the quack-cure industry; (5) the SSRI tier stays reserved, not reflexive.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way parents ask it

**"She's just shy like me. What's the problem?"** Shy is a temperament, slow to warm, quiet by preference. A disorder is when the fear COSTS things: she cannot read aloud, cannot join the canteen, cannot attend school without stomach-ache. A quiet person who functions is fine. A silent person who is suffering needs help, and, gently, my question back is how your own shyness has treated you over the years.

**"The reports are all normal. So the pain is not real?"** The pain is completely real; anxiety's gut-signalling is genuine physiology, not imagination. Normal scans don't mean "no pain"; they mean the cause is not in the organs. The clue was always the timing: Sunday evenings, school mornings, gone in holidays. That pattern is the body printing its worry, and it is treatable.

**"Should we just force her to school? She cries but the school says attendance matters."** Structure, yes: force, no. The forced-in-with-wailing child learns the school is a place she gets abandoned at, and the alarm grows. The working route is the LADDER: gate-visit, half-day, safe-person, full day, with brave-praise at each rung. Firm and graduated beats hard and sudden every time.

**"Will he grow out of it?"** Some do. Roughly a third of childhood anxieties remit on their own, which means two-thirds persist or grow, and the longer the avoidance architecture stands, the deeper the foundations. The treatment is short (weeks-to-months) and highly effective; waiting is a real strategy with real odds, the wrong direction to gamble a school year on.

**"Is medicine needed? I don't want my child dependent on tablets."** Most children get better on CBT-and-parent-work alone, no medication. For the more severe or stuck cases, an SSRI for some months, tapered after recovery, is evidence-backed and non-addictive (this family is not the benzo-withdrawal story). The real dependence risk is the anxiety itself: the rescue patterns the family builds around it.

**"Should we stop the hostel plan?"** For this child, right now: yes, treat first, board later. Hostels are wonderful for the ready nervous system and a furnace for the anxious one. After the programme, a graduated boarding plan (weekends home, a named confidant) protects most children. Readiness is the child's, not the family's timetable.

**"We never scold him about fears. Why does he have this?"** Anxiety is not caused by scolding; it arrives through temperament (a sensitive alarm shipped from the factory), heredity, and what the alarm was TAUGHT by events and examples. If anything, the softest homes produce it most, the loving rescue that never lets the alarm hear a non-event. Nothing here is anyone's fault; the programme is everyone's job.

**"The school says he's being defiant, he refuses to speak. Punishment?"** No. A child who speaks fluently at home and is silent at school is not defying you; his alarm is jamming the voice. Punishment deepens the jam (silence becomes safer than risk). The treatment is a slow speech-ladder through friends and play, pressure-free. It resolves in months when run properly, and entrenches when run with the stick.

**"Can we just home-school till she settles?"** School-avoidance is the trap's favourite exit; each month out raises the return price. The better plan: attendance in the smallest tolerable form (an hour, a half-day, the gate-visit) while the programme runs. Keep the child IN the building, build up the dose; home-schooling is the emergency exit for genuine, rare impossibilities, not the first move.

**"How long will this treatment take?"** Most families see movement in 4–8 weeks and substantial recovery in 3–6 months of proper work. The somatic pains fade first, the sleep next, the school-ladder takes its weeks. The last thing to leave is the temperament, which stays as a careful, watchful, sensitive adult who knows how to handle an alarm.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**The classification skeleton for viva:** childhood anxiety = separation anxiety, selective mutism, specific phobia, social anxiety, GAD, (panic post-puberty); each with duration/persistence gates and the developmental-inappropriateness clause; GAD child-gate = worry + at least 1 of restlessness/fatigue/concentration-irritability/muscle/sleep (vs 3+ for adults, the exam-perfect detail).

**Mnemonic ("S-G-S-S-S the five rooms":** Separation, Generalised, Social, Selective-mutism, Specific phobia) the alarm misplaced in different rooms. For school refusal's four engines: "S-A-M-T", Separation, Anxiety-specific (performance/bullying), Mood (depression), Truancy (conduct).

**High-yield facts:**
- Childhood anxiety disorders = the commonest child mental-health conditions (6–10% mid-childhood, higher by teens); girls ~2× after 6
- Separation anxiety = commonest in young children and the top engine of early school refusal; often begins after a family loss/illness/hospitalisation
- Selective mutism = speaks at home, silent at school 1+ month; participation-without-speech (autism's differential); treat early, waiting entrenches
- The alarm recalibrates through exposure non-events; avoidance/rescue maintains: the treatment logic in one sentence
- Best-evidenced CBT: the Coping Cat lineage (FEEL-EXPECT-ACT-PRAISE); parent accommodation reduction (the SPACE logic)
- SSRI tier for moderate-severe: sertraline/fluoxetine; combination > monotherapy in some trials (POTS); suicidality-class-warning conversation
- Benzos: no meaningful child role (disinhibition + dependence), the exam point
- Normal windows: stranger 6–12 mo, separation 9–18 mo, dark/monster 3–5 y; the window makes the disorder
- Indian pattern: somatic-front presentation → paediatric carousel → time-locked pain sentence as circuit-breaker

**Classic traps:**
1. Treating the pain, missing the pattern (normal reports + school-calendar timing = the fear interview)
2. "Force her into school" as treatment (entrenchment by abandonment)
3. Selective mutism treated as stubbornness/deafness/hearing-carousels
4. Missing depression under the adolescent "anxiety" referral
5. Missing bullying or trauma as the engine of a sudden-onset school refusal
6. The single-parent parent-module (grandmother's rescue mission kills the ladder; train ALL adults)
7. Alprazolam-for-exams as "treatment": chemical rescue where exposure was needed

**Indian exam corner:** expected patterns; "a 7-year-old with recurring abdominal pain, normal reports, school-morning timing: diagnosis and management"; "school refusal: differential diagnosis and approach"; "selective mutism vs autism differentiation"; "treatment of separation anxiety disorder". Indian-context marks: the paediatric-somatisation pathway, hostel-readiness counselling, joint-family accommodation module, exam-season system interventions.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. The child-specific GAD gate differs from adults in requiring:**
A. Six months' worry with only 1 additional symptom
B. Three months' worry with 3 additional symptoms
C. Somatic complaints mandatory
D. Teacher-report mandatory
**Answer: A.** The paediatric mercy clause: 1+ of the restlessness/fatigue/concentration/muscle/sleep cluster suffices in children.

**2. Stomach-aches occurring Sunday evenings and school mornings, vanishing in holidays, with normal workup, suggest:**
A. Crohn's disease
B. Anxiety disorder with somatic presentation (the time-locked pattern)
C. School laziness
D. Malingering
**Answer: B.** The timing is the diagnosis, the sentence to teach every paediatrician.

**3. The MOST common anxiety disorder in a 6-year-old and the top engine of early school refusal:**
A. Panic disorder
B. Separation anxiety disorder
C. Social anxiety disorder
D. OCD
**Answer: B.** The cling engine of the 5–8 band, often post a family illness/loss event.

**4. The core corrective experience that recalibrates the child's alarm:**
A. Reassurance by parents
B. Graded exposure: experienced non-events (the ladder)
C. Avoidance till readiness
D. Sedation before feared situations
**Answer: B.** Alarms reset through lived non-events; rescue deepens the setting.

**5. A 6-year-old speaks fluently at home, is silent at school 4 months, participates in play, milestones normal. Best next step:**
A. Punishment for defiance
B. Stimulus-fading speech ladder + no-pressure school protocol (selective mutism management)
C. Hearing surgery referral
D. Speech for autism workup
**Answer: B.** The participation-without-speech signature; ladder through peers first, early treatment before entrenchment.

**6. The best-evidenced medication tier for a child with moderate-to-severe GAD after CBT-only insufficiency:**
A. Alprazolam as needed
B. SSRI: sertraline/fluoxetine, low-start, weeks-to-effect
C. Risperidone
D. Melatonin
**Answer: B.** The POTS/CAMS-tier evidence; benzos have essentially no child-anxiety role.

**7. "Forceful immediate full-day school attendance" for separation-anxiety school refusal:**
A. Is the treatment of choice
B. Risks abandonment-learning and entrenchment: graduated re-entry is correct
C. Is neutral
D. Works only in boys
**Answer: B.** The ladder beats the dump, firm AND graduated.

**8. In an Indian joint family running a child's exposure programme, the critical implementation step:**
A. Train the mother alone
B. Train ALL rescuing adults together (grandparents included) with the accommodation inventory
C. Admit the child
D. Prescribe for the grandmother
**Answer: B.** The programme dies at the fastest rescuer; the Sunday assembly is standard Indian child-anxiety work.
