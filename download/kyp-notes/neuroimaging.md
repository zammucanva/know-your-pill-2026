# Brain Imaging in Psychiatry — PET, SPET and MRI Windows on the Living Brain

## 1. In one line + overview

**Psychiatric neuroimaging runs on three technologies, PET and SPET (radiotracer imaging of receptors, blood flow and metabolism), structural MRI (anatomy with millimetre resolution) and functional MRI (blood-oxygenation imaging of activation, radiation-free), and its psychiatric record is a mix of real findings (ventricular enlargement, hippocampal volume loss, hypofrontality with its complexities, receptor alterations) and methodological honesty (artefacts, patient samples, the localisation traps).**

The Oxford chapters (2.3.6-2.3.8) present the complete technical and clinical picture. **PET and SPET** (functional positron emission tomography; single-photon emission tomography): the methodology, radiotracer production (cyclotron-generated positron emitters ¹⁵O, ¹¹C, ¹⁸F; SPET's ⁹⁹ᵐTc and ¹²³I), the annihilation-coincidence detection, the pharmacological-inert microgram doses, the quantitative modelling (receptors, blood flow, glucose metabolism), and the psychiatric applications: the dopamine system imaging (D₂ receptors and antipsychotic occupancy, the 65%-for-antipsychotic-effect, 80%-for-EPS threshold logic), the schizophrenia findings (**hypofrontality**, found in ~50% of resting studies and more in activation paradigms; its symptom-specificity (negative-symptom and poverty-of-speech associations) and the **cortical inefficiency** counter-model of hyperfrontality; the pacing-task dilemma), the serotonergic imaging (WAY 100635 for 5-HT₁A; altanserin and setoperone for 5-HT₂A; the transporter ligands), the depression findings and drug-occupancy studies. **Structural MRI** (2.3.7): the physics (T1 and T2 relaxation; spin-echo sequences; the grey-white-CSF contrast advantage), morphometric findings in schizophrenia (lateral ventricular enlargement, one of the most replicated findings in biological psychiatry; temporal-lobe and hippocampal volume reduction (hippocampal atrophy visible in ~one-third of patients), the thalamus and cerebellar findings, progressive- versus static-change debates) and in the affective disorders. **Functional MRI** (2.3.8, Bullmore & Suckling): the BOLD contrast (neurovascular coupling (neural activity increasing blood flow without proportional oxygen extraction, diluting paramagnetic deoxyhaemoglobin and prolonging T2*) signal changes of 3% or less), gradient-echo and echo-planar imaging (whole-brain multislice in ~2 seconds), the artefacts (movement, even submillimetre; susceptibility in inferior temporal and orbitofrontal regions, the sinus-proximity problem that blinds fMRI precisely to psychiatry's favourite regions; the speech-articulation confound), study design and analysis (block and event-related designs; the statistical maps), and the clinical position: fMRI's clinical role remains limited to specialised applications (hemispheric dominance before surgery), research tool, not yet test.

## 4. The core ideas

### 4.1 PET and SPET: the molecular window

**The methodology**: positron-emitting isotopes (¹⁵O, half-life 2.03 min; ¹¹C, 20.4 min; ¹⁸F, 109.8 min) produced by a **cyclotron** and incorporated into biological molecules (the radiotracers); SPET's longer-lived isotopes (⁹⁹ᵐTc, 6.02 h; ¹²³I, 13.2 h). The PET detection principle: emitted positrons annihilate with electrons, producing **two 180°-opposed gamma rays** detected by coincidence-linked crystal pairs, localising radiation to the inter-detector volume; three-dimensional images over minutes to hours; quantitative modelling of the time-activity data into **receptor numbers, regional cerebral blood flow, glucose and oxygen metabolism**. The doses: microgram masses with no pharmacological effect. **PET versus SPET**: PET's tracer diversity and quantitation make it the research standard (with the hot-cell radiochemistry burden); SPET's simplicity and cost make it the clinical-deployment option. The resolution: millimetre-level (with SPET somewhat coarser).

**The psychiatric radiotracers and findings**: the dopamine system, D₂/D₃ ligands ([¹¹C]raclopride, [¹¹C]FLB 457; the SPET tracers IBZM and epidepride) enabling the **antipsychotic-occupancy studies**: striatal D₂ occupancy of ~65% for antipsychotic effect, with extrapyramidal symptoms emerging beyond ~80%, the imaging-pharmacology bridge (the dopamine-synthesis-capacity ligands; the D₁ ligands; the amphetamine-challenge dopamine-release paradigms showing schizophrenia's excessive release). **Schizophrenia's functional findings**: **hypofrontality**; the field's founding observation, present in about **50% of resting-state studies** and more often in activation paradigms; the moderating variables: task nature and demands, patient performance, and symptom profile, with **negative symptoms** showing the strongest associations (the large 30-patient resting-flow study's factor-analytic support); poverty of speech reducing left-DLPFC flow **irrespective of diagnosis** (depression or schizophrenia, symptoms, not diagnoses, drive blood flow); the pacing dilemma (slowing tasks to match performance may hide the dysfunction expressed at normal demands); and the **cortical-inefficiency reformulation**, hyperfrontality: when patients and controls perform at similar levels, patients show *enhanced* frontal activation, reflecting inefficient signal processing. The serotonin system: [¹¹C]WAY 100635 for **5-HT₁A** (the reduced-availability findings in depression and anxiety); [¹¹C]NMSP, [¹⁸F]altanserin and [¹⁸F]setoperone for **5-HT₂A**; the transporter ligands ([¹¹C]DASB and successors) for the depression and treatment studies; the benzodiazepine-receptor ligands.

### 4.2 Structural MRI: the anatomy window

**The physics**: protons in a strong static field, excited by radiofrequency pulses at the Larmor frequency; **T1** (spin-lattice, longitudinal recovery) and **T2** (spin-spin, transverse dephasing) relaxation, tissue-environment-dependent; the **spin-echo sequence** (90° pulse, dephasing, 180° rephasing pulse, the echo at TE); spatial encoding by slice-selective, frequency- and phase-encoding gradients; multislice acquisition; voxel resolution ~0.5-2 mm at 1.5T. **The outstanding MRI advantage**: visible grey-matter/white-matter/CSF contrast, the anatomical examination without radiation, repeatable across an illness course. **The morphometric findings in schizophrenia**: lateral **ventricular enlargement** (among the most replicated findings in biological psychiatry, the CT-era discovery confirmed and quantified by MRI); **temporal-lobe volume reduction** and **hippocampal volume decrease** (macroscopically visible in about one-third of patients; associated with the memory impairments); thalamic and cerebellar vermis abnormalities; the structural brain changes distributed rather than focal, with the developmental debates (static neurodevelopmental lesions versus progressive tissue loss; the first-episode studies showing many changes already present, the longitudinal studies showing some progression); the interpretation disciplines (hospital-sample bias; medication effects: the caudate enlargement with typical antipsychotics; the substance-misuse confounds; manual-versus-automated measurement). **In the affective disorders**: structural MRI contributing the hippocampal-volume literature (the HPA-cortisol link of the neuroendocrinology note) and the subcortical and white-matter hyperintensity findings of late-life depression (the vascular-depression connections of the elderly-mood note).

### 4.3 Functional MRI: the activation window

**The BOLD chain**: neural activity (within ~100 ms of the stimulus) → **neurovascular coupling** (Roy and Sherrington's 1894 insight; the mechanisms complex and not fully defined) → local **blood flow increasing 3-8 s after stimulus onset** *without* commensurate oxygen uptake → the oxygenated:deoxygenated haemoglobin ratio rising → **deoxyhaemoglobin's paramagnetic effect diluted** → T2* prolongation → the measurable **BOLD signal (blood-oxygenation-level-dependent), changes of 3% or less**. **The imaging sequences**: gradient-echo (the dephasing accelerated and reversed by brief gradient pulses, faster than spin-echo; the flip-angle tuning for T1- or T2*-weighting) and **echo-planar imaging** (rapid gradient blipping, up to 128 echoes per excitation, whole-brain multislice in 2 seconds or less; the hardware: rapidly-switched gradient coils with their slew rates, shielding and eddy-current management). **The artefacts**: **movement** (inevitable; submillimetre motion causes significant artefact; anxiety-reduction, clear task instruction, comfort, and button-press rather than speech responses; the cardiorespiratory pulsation aliasing); **susceptibility** (signal loss near bone and sinuses, **inferior temporal and orbitofrontal regions**, precisely the frontal-temporal territories psychiatry most wants; compounded by speaking (sinus deformation mimicking activation); images should not be acquired during articulation); the usual MRI contraindications (ferromagnetic implants, claustrophobia). **The study designs and analysis**: block and event-related designs; the statistical parametric-mapping tradition (the general-linear-model and multiple-comparisons machinery); the repetition advantage, no radiation, so a single subject can be examined on many occasions and patients studied ethically and serially. **The clinical position**: the honest statement; fMRI's clinical role remains limited to specialised applications such as **hemispheric dominance assessment before neurosurgery**; the psychiatric applications are research (cognitive-activation studies across disorders; the connectivity and network analyses maturing post-Oxford), not yet diagnostic tests.

## 5. Why it matters clinically

- **The D₂-occupancy thresholds are prescribing logic**: 65% occupancy for antipsychotic effect and 80% for extrapyramidal symptoms explain dose-response ceilings (why risperidone beyond ~6 mg adds side effects, not benefit), imaging-derived pharmacology in daily practice.
- **The hypofrontality lesson** inoculates against single-scan interpretation: symptoms and tasks, not diagnoses, determine activation; performance-matching changes findings; and the cortical-inefficiency reversal shows the same data read two ways.
- **Ventricular enlargement and hippocampal volume loss** are the radiological findings most worth quoting in schizophrenia workups, while remembering their group-level, non-diagnostic nature.
- **The BOLD delay and the susceptibility blind spots** explain both fMRI's power (safe repetition) and its limits (orbitofrontal silence; why the "imaging the emotions" literature must be read with regional caution).
- **The imaging cascade in psychiatric workup**: structural MRI to exclude lesions (the standard clinical use, tumours, hydrocephalus, the dementias); functional imaging as research; the distinction keeps requesting habits rational.
- **India**: MRI availability defines the practical tier (CT everywhere, MRI in most districts, fMRI in research centres); the lesion-exclusion scan is the clinical workhorse; PET in psychiatry is metro-research (its oncology deployments paying the cyclotron bills).

## 6. India lens

- **The clinical MRI standard**: the Indian psychiatric referral for "MRI brain, ?organicity" serves the lesion-exclusion function (tumour, infarct, hydrocephalus, the leukoariosis of vascular depression), the appropriate clinical deployment of a research-heavy technology.
- **The two-tier reality**: district psychiatry works with CT (adequate for haemorrhage, large lesions, gross atrophy); MRI with temporal-lobe protocol (mesial temporal sclerosis, the epilepsy-psychiatry interface) in district hospitals; research fMRI in the few centres (the NIMHANS and metro-academic tradition).
- **Costs (approx 2026)**: CT head inexpensive everywhere; MRI brain in the few-thousands of rupees in public institutions (higher private); PET-CT where available is oncology-priced, out of psychiatric reach for most.
- **The D₂-occupancy teaching without the scanner**: the 65/80 thresholds translate into prescribing rules (dose ceilings, the high-dose-antipsychotic critique) deliverable in any Indian OPD, imaging's clinical lessons travelling without the machine.
- **First-episode Indian studies**: the admission queues (months long in places) make the Indian first-episode window wide, the neurodevelopmental-versus-progressive questions testable on Indian cohorts (as the Indian research tradition has begun).
- **The interpretation disciplines India needs most**: the hospital-sample bias (Indian tertiary samples being extreme examples of severity-filtered selection) and the medication-confound (treatment histories longer than illness recognition), the honest reading of any Indian imaging study.

**FAQ**
*"What does a brain scan show in schizophrenia?"*: On average: enlarged ventricles and reduced temporal/hippocampal volumes; findings that replicate across studies but are group-level statistical differences, not diagnostic markers: many patients have normal scans, and the individual scan neither confirms nor excludes the diagnosis.
*"Why did they scan before treatment?"*: To exclude a structural cause (tumour, lesion, hydrocephalus), the standard clinical use of structural imaging; functional imaging's psychiatric role remains research.
*"Can imaging measure antipsychotic effect?"*: Yes, in research: D₂-receptor occupancy studies showed ~65% occupancy separating effective from ineffective doses, and ~80% marking the extrapyramidal threshold, the science behind dose ceilings; clinical monitoring uses clinical observation, not scanners.
*"What is the fMRI scan actually measuring?"*: Blood, not thoughts: neural activity increases local blood flow more than oxygen use, changing the magnetic properties of blood (the BOLD signal), an indirect, delayed (seconds), small (under 3%) index of activation.
*"Why can't fMRI see the orbitofrontal cortex well?"*: The susceptibility artefact: air-bone boundaries near the sinuses distort the magnetic field in exactly the inferior-frontal and temporal regions, an ironic blind spot over psychiatry's emotional anatomy.
*"Is fMRI ready for diagnosis?"*: Not yet: its clinical role is specialised (language dominance before surgery); psychiatric fMRI remains a research tool, the honest position of the field.
*"Do the scans prove psychiatry is brain disease?"*: They show real, replicable group differences (ventricular, hippocampal, activation), strong evidence of brain involvement; they do not yet individualise diagnosis, and "involved" is a fairer word than "diseased" at present resolution.

**Self-test MCQs**

**Q1.** The detection principle of PET is:
- (a) Magnetic relaxation
- (b) Positron annihilation producing two 180°-opposed gamma rays, detected by coincidence-linked crystal pairs
- (c) Ultrasound reflection
- (d) X-ray attenuation

**Answer: (b).** The coincidence geometry localises the source to the inter-detector line, the physical basis of PET's 3D quantitative imaging.

**Q2.** The antipsychotic-occupancy thresholds established by D₂-receptor imaging are approximately:
- (a) 30% for effect and 50% for side effects
- (b) 65% occupancy for antipsychotic effect, ~80% for extrapyramidal symptoms
- (c) 90% and 95%
- (d) No thresholds exist

**Answer: (b).** The imaging-pharmacology bridge: explaining dose-response ceilings (adding dose beyond the therapeutic window adds only EPS).

**Q3.** Hypofrontality in schizophrenia has been found in approximately what proportion of resting-state PET/SPET studies, and is most associated with which clinical picture?
- (a) 100%; positive symptoms
- (b) About 50% of resting studies (more in activation paradigms); most pronounced with negative symptoms
- (c) 10%; mania
- (d) Never found

**Answer: (b).** The finding's inconsistency is itself the teaching: task, performance and symptoms moderate it, with negative-symptom and poverty-of-speech associations the strongest.

**Q4.** The BOLD fMRI signal arises because neural activity:
- (a) Directly emits radio waves
- (b) Increases local blood flow more than oxygen uptake, diluting paramagnetic deoxyhaemoglobin and prolonging T2*
- (c) Heats the tissue
- (d) Changes bone density

**Answer: (b).** The neurovascular-coupling chain: with the 3-8 second delay and the ≤3% amplitude as its clinical-physics caveats.

**Q5.** The susceptibility artefact in fMRI disproportionately affects:
- (a) The motor cortex
- (b) Inferior temporal and orbitofrontal regions near bone and sinuses
- (c) The cerebellum only
- (d) Visual cortex

**Answer: (b).** The ironic blind spot: the emotional and social brain regions being hardest to image; the speaking-confound compounds it.

**Q6.** Among the most replicated structural MRI findings in schizophrenia are:
- (a) Cortical dysplasia and arachnoid cysts
- (b) Lateral ventricular enlargement and temporal-lobe/hippocampal volume reduction
- (c) Pituitary adenomas
- (d) Normal scans only

**Answer: (b).** From the CT-era ventricular discovery through the MRI-era hippocampal literature, group-level, replicated, non-diagnostic: the honest status of psychiatric structural neuroimaging.
