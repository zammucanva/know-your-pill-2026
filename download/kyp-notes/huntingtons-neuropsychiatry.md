# Huntington's disease — neuropsychiatry

## 1. In one line + overview

**Tagline:** Huntington's disease is a **single-gene, autosomal dominant** brain illness in which movement disorder, psychiatric disturbance and a subcortical dementia travel together over 15–20 years, with the psychiatric symptoms often arriving **a decade before the chorea**, and a family's medical future decided by a blood test that **nobody should take without counselling first**.

**Overview, in plain words:** One spelling error, a repeated stretch of DNA (CAG repeats) in the *huntingtin* gene on chromosome 4, is enough to slowly destroy the striatum (the deep brain's selector of movement and habit) and, with it, the person's motor control, mood regulation, impulse control and thinking. Because the gene is dominant, **each child of an affected parent has a 50-50 chance** of carrying it; and because the repeat tends to lengthen when passed from father to child, children can fall ill earlier than the parent (**anticipation**). Onset is typically **35–45 years** (the years of jobs, marriages and school-going children) with chorea (dance-like involuntary movements), irritability and depression, apathy, and a slow "subcortical" dementia: thinking slows, plans dissolve, but memory storage holds relatively long. Death usually comes 15–20 years from onset. Psychiatry's role is enormous and specific: the behavioural and mood symptoms bring patients to us first; our prescribing must respect the movement disorder; and our counselling protects the family from two traps, testing children, and testing adults casually. Nothing cures the underlying disease today; nearly everything about comfort, function and family integrity can still be treated.

## 2. Learning objectives

1. Explain autosomal dominant inheritance and anticipation in one minute each.
2. Recite the triad (motor / psychiatric / cognitive) and give the typical onset-to-death timeline.
3. Describe the psychiatric sequence: irritability, depression, apathy, impulse loss, obsessive features, psychosis (uncommon).
4. Explain the subcortical dementia profile (processing speed, executive, memory-to-cue) and why bedside memory screens mislead.
5. Outline the predictive-testing counselling protocol and its ethical anchors (adult autonomy, no testing minors, confidentiality within families).
6. Manage the behavioural and motor interface: SSRI-first prescribing, tetrabenazine's depression trap, cautious antipsychotics.
7. Set up the Indian family plan: caregiving rosters, marriage-genetic questions, disability certification, genetic referral for loaded trees.

## 4. Epidemiology

### Global
- Prevalence roughly **5–10 per 100,000** in Western European populations; lower in East Asia and, as far as regional data go, in India.
- Onset typically **35–45** (juvenile onset with very long repeats, before 20, in a small subgroup, bradykinesia and rigidity instead of chorea, school failure, seizures).
- Mean survival **15–20 years** from first symptoms; suicide and aspiration pneumonia are landmark causes of death; suicidal ideation is elevated several-fold over general population rates.

### India
- Numbers are small but real, clusters reported from Kerala, Maharashtra, Bengal and Tamil Nadu in older series; under-detection is the rule, with families carrying the "family curse" label across generations without a name for it.
- **Marriage-discrimination reality:** unaffected descendants of affected parents face marriage-market obstacles once family history is known; families therefore actively hide the diagnosis; one more reason genetic counselling must address confidentiality in Indian terms, not just in Western ones.
- The predictive test is available through a handful of centres; the counselling protocol around it is frequently skipped in practice, the exact failure this note argues against.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

- **Genetic, the whole story of cause:** an expanded CAG repeat (≥ 36–40, with diagnostic thresholds around 40) in the huntingtin gene; one affected parent → 50% risk per child; **anticipation** = longer repeats transmitted → earlier onset, especially through the paternal line. Juvenile disease usually comes from paternal transmission of very long repeats.
- **Biological cascade:** mutant huntingtin protein misfolds and accumulates, neurons in the striatum die first; fronto-striatal circuits disconnect → the "habit and gate" system fails (chorea = the gate failing to filter movement; impulsivity = the same failing for behaviour).
- **Neurochemical early losses:** serotonin depletion early (depression, irritability); later widespread involvement.
- **No environmental modifiers of onset are proven**; physical activity and engaged routine appear to help function, not fate.
- **Indian context risks are social, not biological:** concealment, delay of first consultation until chorea is obvious, and children of affected parents reaching marriageable age without any counselling, the preventable harms live in these gaps.

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: the deep gate that fails.** Deep in the brain sits the striatum; the gatehouse that selects which movements, thoughts and impulses get through and which are quietly suppressed. In Huntington's, that gatehouse's cells die. What we call chorea, the dance of the limbs, is **not movement being created; it is movement being *released***, in the same way disinhibition in FTD releases behaviour. When the gate also fails for behaviour: impulses surface unfiltered (sudden anger, blurted remarks), routines disintegrate (can't sequence a recipe), and eventually both movement and mind stream unchecked.

**Story 2: the slow executive starvation.** The striatum is the frontal lobes' executive assistant; the part that actually runs a plan step-by-step while the frontal cortex sets the goal. As the assistant dies, goals remain but execution collapses: the patient *knows* he should take his medicines, *intends* to, and cannot run the sequence. Memory storage (hippocampal) is spared until late, which is why memory screens look reassuringly normal while the household is falling apart. This is "subcortical dementia" in one image: **the filing system works; the office manager is starving.**

**Story 3: the test that changes families before it changes patients.** Unlike any other common neuropsychiatric disease, the diagnosis can precede symptoms by decades. A blood test can tell a 28-year-old at-risk person: you carry the expansion. That knowledge cannot currently change the disease's course; it changes careers, marriages, childbearing and self-image. Hence the internationally agreed scaffolding: counselling before, support during, support after, and the absolute rule that **the choice to know is the patient's alone**.

## 7. Symptoms

**Psychiatric (often first, and first missed):**
- **Irritability and low frustration tolerance:** disproportionate anger at minor domestic triggers, slamming, shouting, usually the earliest and most family-damaging symptom, often blamed on "work stress" or the spouse.
- **Depression:** common, genuine, treatable; **suicidal ideation is elevated at every stage**, before diagnosis, at disclosure, and in early symptomatic years. Ask directly, every time.
- **Apathy:** as the disease advances, flat initiative replaces storms, families often misread this as "finally calm".
- **Impulse-control and behavioural disintegration:** sexual disinhibition, spending, reckless driving, aggressiveness out of character; obsessive-compulsive-like rituals in a subgroup.
- **Psychosis:** genuinely uncommon (unlike its prominence in exam folklore), when present, treat carefully (antipsychotics may help symptoms but worsen motor function).
- Anxiety, insomnia, and later emotional lability.

**Motor:**
- Early: restlessness, fidgeting, piano-playing fingers, facial grimacing, clumsy gait, dropped objects.
- Established: chorea worsens with stress, disappears in sleep; abnormal eye movements (slow, "sticky" saccades: a clinical signature); dysarthria (speech loses clarity and rhythm).
- Late: chorea may give way to rigidity and bradykinesia; swallowing fails; weight loss becomes severe (movement burns calories while dysphagia limits intake).
- **Juvenile form:** parkinsonian stiffness, seizures, school decline, before 20; usually paternal transmission of long repeats.

**Cognitive (subcortical):**
- Slowing, sequencing failure, poor planning and abstraction; visuospatial difficulty; **memory retrieval impaired but cueable**; insight often persists painfully into mid-disease.

## 8. Diagnosis

- **Clinical triad + family history** → genetic confirmation (CAG repeat length on blood), the one neuropsychiatric disease where a single test settles the diagnosis; borderline/intermediate repeat ranges (36–39) exist, which is one reason testing belongs in counselled settings.
- **Presentations to psychiatry first:** irritability, depression, obsessive features or a midlife "personality change" with a positive family tree, before any visible chorea. The high-yield family-history question: "anyone who 'wasted away' or 'went strange' and died before sixty?"
- **Examination pearls:** observe hands during the whole interview (piano-playing), ask him to hold tongue protruded (cannot sustain), slow/impersistant saccades on eye-movement testing, tandem gait difficulty, handwriting serial samples (deteriorating).
- **Imaging:** caudate atrophy ("boxcar ventricles") on CT/MRI supports but never replaces the test.
- **Rule-outs in midlife chorea before blaming Huntington's:** thyrotoxicosis, systemic lupus, neuroacanthocytosis (check blood smear), tardive dyskinesia from psychiatric prescriptions (the classic iatrogenic confounder in a psychiatric cohort), Wilson's disease in the young (<40: always check, treatable, see the amnesic/metabolic notes' logic).
- **Predictive testing protocol (for asymptomatic at-risk adults):** dedicated counselling sessions, neurological and psychological assessment, discussion of motives (childbearing? career? marriage pressure?), a cooling-off period, results given in person with support arranged; the partner included where the patient wishes. **Never test minors; never test for third parties (employer, prospective spouse's family); never disclose one person's result to relatives without consent**, the family-confidentiality trap unique to dominant diseases.

## 9. Management

### (a) The multidisciplinary spine
Neurology (motor, genetic), psychiatry (mood, behaviour, suicidality), genetic counselling, speech/swallow therapy, physiotherapy, dietitian (high-calorie, texture-modified feeding), social work, and, from the start, family planning for the caregiving decade ahead.

### (b) Psychiatric prescribing with the movement disorder in mind
- **First-line: SSRIs** (sertraline, escitalopram), for irritability, depression, obsessive-type symptoms and mild impulsivity; genuinely effective and motor-neutral. Titrate properly; partial response is common and worth pursuing.
- **Mood instability/irritability persisting:** consider careful addition of an anticonvulsant mood stabiliser (valproate-type, with the usual pregnancy counselling) or low-dose antipsychotic where danger overrides.
- **Antipsychotics (olanzapine/risperidone in low dose, quetiapine):** only for psychosis, dangerous impulsivity or severe chorea; they blunt chorea slightly while risking parkinsonism, swallowing deterioration and sedation, a trade, not a treatment.
- **Tetrabenazine** for disabling chorea: effective; **cautions: depression and suicidality are flagged side effects**: screen and stabilise mood first, never combine with SSRIs of the MAOI family, and re-ask about despair at every visit.
- **Benzodiazepines:** sparingly, short courses; they add imbalance and cognitive dulling.
- Late-stage: antidepressant and antipsychotic loads are often *reduced* deliberately as swallowing and consciousness decline, palliative comfort over psychiatric completeness.

*India note: sertraline costs under ₹100–150/month generic (approx 2026); tetrabenazine is costlier and variable in availability; the real costs are the caregiver decade and the genetic test (offered in few centres, priced accordingly).*

### (c) Behavioural management: the household engineering
- Written routines, one instruction at a time, calm low-stimulation environments (chorea and irritability both worsen with crowds and confrontation).
- Do not argue with impulse-driven outbursts; step away, return calm. What is released by a failing gate cannot be lectured back.
- Safety: cooking and driving decisions made early and kindly; weapons and vehicle keys relocated at the first signs of impulsivity.
- Weight and swallow: texture-modified high-calorie diet, supervised meals, upright posture, hand-feeding techniques over early tube decisions, with honest late-stage conversations.
- Caregiver architecture: the spouse is usually similar age; the caregiving decade belongs to the children, build the roster early; respite is the scarcest and most necessary prescription.

### (d) Genetic and family work
- Counselling for at-risk adults (the protocol above), plus practical Indian additions: whom in the extended family to tell, how to answer marriage-related enquiries honestly without injustice, and how to prevent covert testing pressure ("just check the boy before the alliance").
- Referral for **PGT-M (preimplantation genetic testing)** exists in a few Indian private fertility centres for gene-positive couples, expensive, but a real option to mention without overselling.
- Juvenile-form families need school liaison and seizure management with paediatric neurology.

## 10. Indian practice

- **Label before diagnosis:** families often know it only as the inherited "wasting-and-madness" running in the line. Naming the disease in front of the family, drawing the 50-50 coin-flip diagram, and explaining anticipation converts three generations of superstition into an actionable map.
- **Where care fragments:** neurologists treat chorea, psychiatrists treat depression, genetic centres test, nobody owns the family. The single best Indian service innovation is a combined Huntington's clinic (a handful exist in metros); short of that, one named coordinating doctor per family.
- **Disability and financial protection:** disability certification for the combined motor-cognitive decline; insurance to be arranged *before* the diagnosis is formalised wherever legally possible; property/POA planning while executive function is intact, the same early-urgent legal package as young-onset FTD.
- **Marriage and confidentiality ethics:** a recurring Indian dilemma; the at-risk adult who does not want to know, whose marriage alliance is being screened by the other family. The clinical stance: protect the patient's right not to know; refuse third-party testing; counsel both families on the ethics; document.
- **Death care:** aspiration pneumonia, cachexia, restraint-free comfort; palliative referral appropriately early: "we are not abandoning you; we are protecting his comfort now" is the sentence that lands.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: the "temper problem" that preceded the dance.** A 38-year-old bank officer was referred for "unmanageable temper" of two years: shouting at clerks, throwing files, a near-suspension. His father had "wasted away" and died at 51, "some nerve disease", per the family. Depression screening positive; memory testing near-normal; but on examination his protruded tongue would not stay still, fingers drummed on the chair, and rapid alternating movements were clumsy; eye movements were slow and impersistent. Genetic testing confirmed the expansion. SSRI treatment, household engineering and early legal planning began; chorea became visible 18 months later, by which time the family was already organised.

*Teaching points: midlife irritability with a "wasted away before sixty" family tree is Huntington's until excluded; the psychiatric prodrome outpaces chorea; bedside motor mini-signs (tongue, saccades, fingers) are the diagnosis's fingerprints; early legal work while executive function is intact is as important as any prescription.*

**Case 2: the family that wanted the girl tested before the wedding.** A 26-year-old woman, whose grandfather died of Huntington's, was to be married; the groom's family quietly requested "a blood test for that brain disease" from a local laboratory. The treating psychiatrist declined third-party testing, met both families, explained the 50% odds, the right not to know, and the option of the woman's own counselled predictive testing should she wish; she chose not to know. The alliance proceeded; documentation protected everyone.

*Teaching points: predictive testing is the patient's decision alone; refusing covert third-party testing is a hard ethical rule, not a formality; Indian marriage dynamics are precisely where this rule bends if nobody guards it; counselling both families converts suspicion into information.*

## 13. FAQ

**"Is it curable?"**
Not yet: the underlying gene's damage cannot currently be reversed. But almost every symptom that hurts the family (the temper, the depression, the sleep, the swallowing safety) can be eased, and the family's future can be protected with planning and counselling.

**"My father has it. What are my chances?"**
Fifty-fifty: a coin flip decided at your conception. A counselling-based blood test can tell you whether you carry the gene; no one can or should force that test, and not knowing is a respected choice.

**"Why does my son seem worse than my brother was at that age?"**
This is anticipation: the repeated DNA stretch can lengthen as it passes to the next generation, especially through the father's line, so onset can come earlier and harder. It is a fact of the gene, not of anything your family did.

**"He was such a gentle man. Why the rages?"**
The deep brain gate that filters impulses is damaged. The rages are released, not chosen. We treat them with medicines (SSRIs first) and by lowering household triggers, not by confrontation.

**"He keeps saying he'd be better off dead."**
Take it seriously every time; this illness carries a high risk of suicide, and the thought can be a symptom. Tell the doctor the same day; safety planning and treatment adjustments genuinely help.

**"Should our children be tested early, so we can plan their lives?"**
No, testing minors is prohibited worldwide: the choice to know belongs to the adult they become. What you can do now: keep the family tree honest, so at 18 they can choose with full information.

**"The marriage proposal is asking whether the disease is in the family."**
Answer honestly with facts: one affected person in a line, a 50% risk for that person's children, and a test that exists for adults who choose it. Concealment risks both health and justice; counselling both families is better than covert testing.

**"Is the test available and affordable in India?"**
It exists through a small number of centres; the price varies. The counselling around it costs time but is not optional; it is what separates an informed choice from a wound.

**"What actually happens at the end?"**
Gradual weakness, swallowing difficulty and chest infections, usually 15–20 years in. Palliative care focuses on comfort, dignity at meals, and keeping the person at home with the family that knows him.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **Mnemonics:** the triad as **"Moves, Moods, Mind"** (chorea → psychiatric prodrome → subcortical dementia); risk-figures as **"50-50, 36-and-up, 15-to-20"**.
- **Highest-yield facts:** chromosome 4, CAG repeat, autosomal dominant with anticipation (paternal); striatal (caudate) atrophy; psychiatric prodrome precedes chorea; suicidal risk elevated; tetrabenazine-depression link; SSRI-first psychopharmacology.
- **Classic traps:** (1) calling late-onset "schizophrenia" or "personality disorder" in a 38-year-old with a bad family tree; (2) missing tardive dyskinesia as a chorea mimic in psychiatric patients; (3) forgetting Wilson's in young chorea (treatable!); (4) prescribing antipsychotics for chorea reflexively and converting movement chaos into parkinsonian collapse; (5) genetic testing without counselling, or of a minor.
- **Viva favourites:** anticipation mechanism; juvenile vs adult presentation; subcortical vs cortical dementia; saccadic eye-movement abnormality; boxcar ventricles; ethics of predictive testing (autonomy, no minors, no third parties, family confidentiality); why the suicide question must be asked at every visit.
- **Indian exam corner:** the marriage-alliance testing ethics case; concealment and stigma; paucity of genetic counselling centres; PGT-M as an option; combined-clinic service design as a short note.
- **Rehearse one line:** "In midlife irritability with a suspicious family tree, watch the tongue and the eyes before you write a prescription."

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. A 28-year-old wants predictive testing for Huntington's, requested on the marriage form by the other family. The correct action is:**
A. Send the test as requested
B. Refuse third-party-motivated testing; offer the individual her own counselled testing choice
C. Test her youngest sibling instead
D. Declare her "probably negative" clinically

**Answer: B.** Predictive testing belongs to the autonomous adult alone, with counselling; third-party pressure is a hard exclusion; minors are never tested.

**2. Earlier onset in the child than the affected parent is due to:**
A. Mitochondrial inheritance
B. CAG repeat expansion lengthening on transmission (anticipation), especially paternal
C. Better medical care
D. Recessive gene interaction

**Answer: B.** Anticipation: the repeat lengthens across generations, most often through the paternal line.

**3. The usual psychiatric harbinger, years before chorea, is:**
A. Grandiose delusions
B. Irritability with low frustration tolerance, depression
C. Catatonia
D. Panic attacks

**Answer: B.** Irritability and depression lead the prodrome; psychosis is genuinely uncommon.

**4. The memory profile in Huntington's dementia is:**
A. Rapid forgetting with no cue benefit
B. Retrieval failure that improves with cueing
C. Pure retrograde amnesia
D. Confabulatory amnesia

**Answer: B.** Subcortical dementia: storage intact, retrieval slow, cueable; screens that ignore executive function mislead.

**5. Tetrabenazine is prescribed for chorea. Before starting, the essential step is:**
A. Start an antipsychotic together
B. Screen and treat depression; tetrabenazine can worsen mood and suicidality
C. Confirm with PET
D. Start levodopa

**Answer: B.** The depression/suicidality caution is the drug's defining prescribing trap.

**6. A treatable chorea mimic that must be excluded in a young patient is:**
A. Alzheimer's disease
B. Wilson's disease
C. Pick's disease
D. Normal pressure hydrocephalus

**Answer: B.** Wilson's (copper) is treatable and presents young with movement disorder, always in the young-chorea rule-out list, along with thyrotoxicosis and tardive dyskinesia.
