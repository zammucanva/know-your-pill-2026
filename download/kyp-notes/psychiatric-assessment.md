# Psychiatric Assessment — Principles of Clinical Assessment in General Psychiatry

## 1. In one line + overview

**The initial psychiatric assessment aims at a comprehensive short- and long-term treatment plan, built by sorting complaints into the right conceptual categories (disease, illness, sickness; symptoms, impairments, disablement), conducted as a multi-disciplinary activity, and delivered as a formulation, a prognosis and a written report.**

The Oxford chapter (Cooper & Oates) is written for the general adult clinician doing the initial assessment of patient and family, and it deliberately addresses principles over procedures. Its three areas of special attention: **multi-disciplinary assessment** (and its central diagnostic question for team disagreements: "is this about disease, illness or sickness?"); the **disease/illness/sickness trio**: the doctor's pathological entity, the patient's subjective experience of symptoms and distress, and the social role-interference recognised by others; and **structured instruments**: the schedules behind most clinical research, which clinicians should understand. The architecture: concepts (form/content/effects, the chapter's opening; subjective vs objective information; the diagnostic process with the honest ICD/DSM position that these are *classifications of disorders, not diagnoses*; the disablement frameworks ICF and Nagi); the assessment sequence (collection, analysis, synthesis, review, the "from complaints to formulation" cascade); contextual realities (home and primary-care assessment, privacy and confidentiality procedure, interpreters, cultural framing of the private interview, multiple sources and information conflicts); the multi-disciplinary chapter in full (practice vs teamwork; leadership; key workers; the doctor's reserved domains); instruments; and the ending skills: prognosis, reviews, report-writing.

## 4. The core ideas

### 4.1 Concepts: sorting what you hear

**Form, content, effects.** Psychiatric key information arrives embedded in complaints about relationships, work, housing and money, which may cause, result from, or merely accompany the disorder. The first discipline is sorting: the **form** (the technical term (phobia, delusion) identifying a recurring pattern known to matter) allows disorder identification; the **content** (what the phobia is of, what the delusion says) reveals current concerns; the **effects on functioning** determine management and grade severity. The presenting complaint is usually the *functional interference*; enquiry reveals the contents; close questioning by someone who knows what to ask uncovers the symptoms of form. The interview's built-in tension: acknowledging distress while pursuing the questions that identify symptoms; learning to balance this conflict of interest is a core clinical skill.

**Subjective and objective information.** Is there a truly objective account of events? The chapter works the categories: information as events (potentially multiple-source corroborable), as subjective experience, as observation, as interpretation, with simple definitions useful in clinical discussion, and the reminder that all informants' accounts are constructions. **Disease, illness, sickness**: the medical and sociological trio, disease (the doctor's pathological entity), illness (the patient's experience of symptoms and distress), sickness (the social recognition of role-interference). Its daily use: when team members disagree, ask which of the three is being discussed, the disagreement usually dissolves into legitimate differences of emphasis (physical disease vs personal distress vs social interference). Its historical weight: the definitional debates of Lewis and Wootton; the multi-dimensional resolutions (more than one aspect; the disease level not always the best explanatory level: Taylor's account of symptoms and distress); the lineage through Susser's dimensions. The family's question "is it a real illness?", asked in the patient's own terms, should be anticipated in every initial assessment, and the concepts of lay illness models ("mental illness", "nervous breakdown") and culturally-shaped illness behaviour belong to the standard information set.

**The diagnostic process.** Scadding's dictum from general medicine: the diagnosis "may state no more than the resemblance of the symptoms and signs to a previously recognized pattern"; in psychiatry, **"may" becomes "usually"**, acknowledged by ICD-10 and DSM-IV presenting *classifications of disorders*, not of diagnoses: ICD-10's "clinically recognizable set of symptoms or behaviour associated in most cases with distress and interference with personal functions"; DSM-IV's "clinically significant behavioural or psychological syndrome... associated with present distress or disability". The terminological honesty: "diagnostic criteria" are better labelled "criteria for the identification of disorders", and "diagnosis" reserved for the minority of instances indicating knowledge of something underlying. But the patient expectation stands: all human groups expect healers to discover causes and provide remedies; the relief at an official pronouncement is universal, and its explanatory power grows when the terms are understandable (a major reason for the survival of ethnic and complementary healers, they *provide* a meaning-rich diagnosis). The anti-diagnosis position (Menninger's 1950s–60s dismissal of classification) fails on two counts: the diagnostic term is only one part of the assessment that also produces a **personal formulation**; and any assessment of a person is unavoidably an act of classification.

**Disablement.** Interference with activities often matters more to the patient than the symptoms (the unvoiced fear of long-term dependence). Two frameworks: the **ICF** (functioning, disability, contextual factors, environmental and personal) and **Nagi's** similar US scheme; both are descriptive conceptual frameworks rather than classifications, best used as **team checklists** ensuring all effects have been considered, and mapping disciplines to concepts: social workers to work and relationships, occupational therapists to daily activities, psychologists to cognitive functions. Causality runs both directions (bereavement, a lost relationship, causing disability and emotional impairment; recovered patients refused work by prejudiced employers). Terminological vigilance: impairment, disability and handicap are used interchangeably by different authors.

### 4.2 The sequence: from complaints to formulation

Good psychiatric practice is **whole-person medicine**, the patient seen both as an individual with attributes, abilities, problems and experiences, and as a member of a group subject to family, social and cultural influences; analysis and synthesis alternate. The cascade: **complaints** (unpleasant symptoms, inability to do everyday things, relationship problems) → sorted into **symptoms and impairments** (some are both: disorientation to time is a symptom contributing to dementia recognition and an impairment disrupting daily routine) → the left pathway toward **disorder and possible diagnosis** (treatments, outcomes) and the right pathway from **impairment through disability to participation restriction** (the ICF line). The assessment is incomplete until both sides are considered, with variable emphasis by patient and phase: physical-cause disturbances demand diagnostic accuracy; socially-imposed disturbances demand network and relationship assessment, the diagnostic label adding little. **Life events and the life-chart**: temporal relationships between events and onset (particularly if repeated) inform management and prognosis; the Life Events and Difficulties Schedule (Brown & Harris) being the research gold standard whose length illustrates the technical difficulty of the topic; patients' and families' causal attributions (the universal human assumption that illness follows unpleasant experience) are heard with respect while the clinician concludes by experience, common sense and research acquaintance. **Psychodynamics and the life story**: the interactions of events, relationships and personality attributes (plus defences and coping), in some patients the paramount finding, indicating specialist psychotherapy referral; the formulation draws these threads together.

### 4.3 Context, confidentiality, culture

**Where**: the assessment is not automatically fixed in the clinic, **home interviews** (patient and family at ease, richer information about circumstances, behaviour different from clinic; privacy harder; particular value in puerperal disorders) and **primary-care premises** (patients who dislike hospitals; GP consultation ease; the spreading consultant-liaison style). **Privacy and confidentiality procedure** (explained from the start): patient and family members are each entitled to speak to the doctor in private, with confidence that nothing reaches the other unless requested; the patient agrees not to interrogate family members about their sessions and vice versa; relatives' attempts to arrange secret interviews are firmly resisted, these elementary-sounding points build the trust on which everything else runs (families may be in fear of each other's reactions to "critical" statements). **Interpreters**: always sought when fluency is absent; mental-health professionals who can interpret are increasingly available in multi-minority services; **a professional of the same sex as the patient is always preferred to family members** (confidentiality). **Cultural framing**: the private interview between two strangers discussing intimate and unpleasant matters freely is a **middle-class Western construct**, not shared by all cultures; pre-interview consultation with a professional familiar with the patient's background clarifies what to aim for in intimate enquiry. **Multiple sources and conflicts**: always an advantage for objective topics (events); clinical experience guides which account to use; serious conflicts are resolved by **obtaining more information, not early confrontation**, confrontation (if ever) belongs to planned later interventions.

### 4.4 The multi-disciplinary architecture

**Practice vs teamwork**: in *multi-disciplinary practice*, the consultant leads clinical meetings, depends on senior nurses' and others' views, but decisions are clearly the doctors' responsibility, other professionals welcomed but not necessary members; in *multi-disciplinary teamwork* (evolved with the growth of social workers, OTs and psychologists in services where multiple needs are the rule), significant time-commitment to team meetings, **sharing of responsibilities and blurring of roles**, most obvious in information-gathering and programme-planning, while each member retains parent-discipline skills. **Leadership**: a recognised leader keeps discussions brief and practical, facilitates decisions between reasonable alternatives, and arbitrates insoluble disagreements, and need not be a dominant speaker ("leading from behind"); in crisis, rehabilitation and ID teams the everyday leader need not be a doctor, but decisions about **physical illness, medication and laboratory investigations can only be made by a medically qualified person**, and acute-ward teams must accept free medical and nursing access to patients. **Key workers/case managers**: allocated per patient, matching needs to skills; they carry the agreed programme's contacts, and report progress and problems back to the team. **Written care plans** are a statutory responsibility (UK), amid reorganisations driven by administrative and financial rather than evidential forces. **The psychiatrist's team-specific expertise**: risk and dangerousness assessment; and the **formulation**: the summarising skill that reflects team-agreed policy. **Team maintenance**: tolerance of different viewpoints; meetings about policy, referrals, interpersonal problems and work-related stress (especially in crisis teams with rapid turnover); the line-management balance (professional supervision vs team decision-making; minimised when members are senior); students as observers, not members. **Discharge and referral disagreements**: the common team frictions, resolved by agreed policies sensitive to local service structure.

### 4.5 Instruments, prognosis, reviews, reports

**Instruments**: the structured interviews and rating schedules behind most clinical research and service development; given the shortest mention in research reports yet deserving clinician literacy (their design logic, reliability disciplines, and the structured-interview lineage: SCAN/CIDI/PSE in the research tradition; the questionnaires and ratings of the behavioural-assessment chapter). **Prognosis**: formulated from the collected material with explicit uncertainty, outcome statements graded by confidence; reviews convert assessment from event to process (interval re-assessment of progress, added interventions, and toward episode-end, global statements of improvement and quality of life from the patient's viewpoint). **Reports**: written for their readers (referrers, courts, teams), the formulation in the lead, the evidence behind it, and the plan; the chapter's writing counsel: reports are the assessment's public face, and their clarity is a clinical duty.

## 5. Why it matters clinically

- **The trio dissolves most team conflict**: "disease, illness or sickness?" converts rows into recognitions of different legitimate emphasis, the single most practically useful sentence in the chapter.
- **The formulation is the psychiatrist's signature contribution** to the team; the synthetic summary that orders priorities and makes the plan legible.
- **Disorder-not-diagnosis honesty** protects against overclaiming (and against the anti-diagnosis dismissal): the label identifies a pattern; the formulation explains the person.
- **Context changes content**: home and primary-care settings elicit what clinics suppress, and the interpreter discipline (professional, same-sex, never family) is a confidentiality matter, not a convenience.
- **The privacy procedure** is the trust architecture of family-inclusive assessment, taught explicitly because families fear each other's reactions more than they fear doctors.
- **India**: the whole chapter travels; with the disease/illness/sickness trio being *the* Indian consultation tool (the family asks "sickness" questions, the doctor answers "disease", the traditional healer answers "meaning"), and the interpreter discipline being daily Indian practice.

## 6. India lens

- **The trio in an Indian consultation**: the family's question is usually about *sickness* ("will she marry? can he work? what will people say?"), the doctor's answer is about *disease* (diagnosis, medication), and the shrine's answer is about *meaning*, naming the three levels openly converts the classic Indian consultation-cross-purposes into a coherent conversation.
- **Home assessment**: Indian practice already home-assesses much of the time (families bring the clinic to the home; home visits in community programmes): the chapter's rationale (more revealing, less performative behaviour; privacy harder) applies verbatim, with the joint-family privacy problem solved by the negotiated private-room rule.
- **The interpreter rule**: in multilingual India, the professional-same-sex-non-family interpreter discipline is a real service constraint (professional interpreters are scarce; family translation is the default it warns against, with the confidentiality risks spelled out); bilingual professionals and Tele-MANAS's language-matching are the Indian solutions.
- **The private-interview cultural caution**: the chapter's "middle-class Western construct" point is the Indian clinic's daily reality; private one-to-one disclosure is unfamiliar to many patients; the negotiated sequence (family present, then alone, then family again for the plan) is the culturally intelligent adaptation.
- **Teamwork Indian-style**: the DMHP district team (psychiatrist, social worker, nurse, community officer) is a young multidisciplinary team, the chapter's practice-vs-teamwork distinction, key-worker allocation and doctor-reserved domains map directly onto its teething problems.
- **Written plans**: the statutory care-plan discipline is the answer to the Indian continuity problem (records lost between clinics, follow-ups unbooked); a written plan the family carries is the technology that never fails.

**FAQ**
*"What is the assessment actually for?"*: A comprehensive treatment and management plan with short- and longer-term components: not a diagnosis chased for its own sake, but a formulation (the person, the disorder, the functioning, the context) that orders what to do first.
*"The team meeting descended into argument, what went wrong?"*: Ask which level is being discussed: the disease (is there something physical?), the illness (what does the patient suffer?), or the sickness (what can't she do socially?). The arguments are usually two disciplines answering different questions.
*"Isn't a diagnosis the point?"*: In psychiatry, mostly we identify *disorders*, recognisable patterns with distress and dysfunction, because underlying causes are usually unknown; the diagnosis-language survives because patients and families need causes named, but the formulation is the fuller truth.
*"Should I interview the patient alone or with the family?"*: Both, in sequence, with the privacy procedure explained first: each party can speak in confidence, neither will interrogate the other about it, and secret interviews are refused. Families fear each other's reactions: the procedure is what lets everyone speak.
*"Can't her son translate?"*: Preferably not: use a professional, same-sex interpreter; the son is inside the family dynamics the privacy procedure exists to protect against.
*"Where should the assessment happen?"*: Not automatically the clinic: home (more revealing, behaviour in context, privacy harder) and primary-care premises (less threatening, GP-adjacent) are serious options; the Indian house call is the textbook's recommendation, not a compromise.

**Self-test MCQs**

**Q1.** The aim of the initial psychiatric assessment is:
- (a) A definitive diagnosis only
- (b) A comprehensive plan for treatment and management with both short-term and longer-term components
- (c) A research dataset
- (d) A prescription

**Answer: (b).** The chapter's opening statement: the plan, not the label, is the product.

**Q2.** In team disagreements, the recommended clarifying question is whether the discussion concerns:
- (a) The budget, the roster or the menu
- (b) The patient's possible physical disease, the patient's personal experience of symptoms and distress, or the interference with social activities
- (c) The doctor's preference
- (d) The referral letter's wording

**Answer: (b).** The disease/illness/sickness trio: disagreements typically dissolve into legitimate differences of emphasis once the level is named.

**Q3.** ICD-10 and DSM-IV are, strictly, classifications of:
- (a) Diseases with known causes
- (b) Disorders: clinically recognizable syndromes with distress and dysfunction, causes usually unknown
- (c) Personality types
- (d) Treatments

**Answer: (b).** Scadding's dictum applied: in psychiatry, the diagnosis usually states "no more than the resemblance... to a previously recognized pattern", hence "criteria for the identification of disorders" as the honest label.

**Q4.** For interpreting with a patient who lacks fluency in the interviewer's language, the preferred interpreter is:
- (a) The patient's husband
- (b) A professional of the same sex as the patient, ideally a mental health professional
- (c) The youngest available relative
- (d) Another patient

**Answer: (b).** Confidentiality requires a professional outside the family, and the same-sex preference follows from the same protection.

**Q5.** In multi-disciplinary teamwork (as opposed to practice), decisions about the presence of physical illness and the need for medication or investigations:
- (a) Are voted on by all members equally
- (b) Can only be made by a medically qualified person, owing to unique ethical and legal responsibilities
- (c) Are delegated to the key worker
- (d) Are decided by the social worker

**Answer: (b).** Role-blurring notwithstanding, the medical domain stays medical, one of the chapter's structural rules of teamwork.

**Q6.** The formulation, as described in the chapter, is:
- (a) A transcription of the interview
- (b) The synthetic summary, reflecting team-agreed policy, that orders the patient's attributes, problems, disorders and context into a treatment plan; a special psychiatric skill
- (c) A statistical test
- (d) The prescription chart

**Answer: (b).** The psychiatrist's signature contribution to the multi-disciplinary assessment, analysis converted into an ordered, shared plan.
