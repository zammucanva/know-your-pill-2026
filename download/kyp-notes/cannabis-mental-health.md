# Cannabis and mental health

## 1. In one line + overview

**Tagline:** Cannabis is the world's most-used illegal drug and India's most-normalised one (and while most occasional users are not harmed, **high-potency, frequent, adolescent use genuinely raises the risks of psychosis, dependence, academic collapse and the amotivational grey zone**) plus a withdrawal syndrome (yes, cannabis has one) that families constantly mistake for "laziness".

**Overview, in plain words:** From the charas/ganja traditions to college "joints" and the new wave of semi-legal products and edibles, cannabis sits in Indian life somewhere between religion, recreation and denial. The honest science is two-sided and both sides matter. On the one side: most people who use occasionally, starting as adults, do not develop psychotic illness or the horror-film outcomes of old propaganda. On the other side: **modern cannabis is far stronger than the village variety of past generations**; regular use (especially daily, high-potency, started in the teens) measurably **doubles-to-quadruples the risk of psychosis** in vulnerable people, produces a **real dependence syndrome** in roughly **1 in 10 users overall and 1 in 6 starting as adolescents**, drives a withdrawal syndrome of irritability, insomnia, craving and appetite loss, and its second face, **synthetic cannabinoid street products**, delivers doses of chaos the plant never contained. Psychiatry meets cannabis at four doors: the **psychosis question**, the **motivation-grey zone** of heavy young users, the **withdrawal misdiagnosed as attitude**, and the **schizophrenia patient whose use doubles relapse rates.**

## 2. Learning objectives

1. Give the balanced risk picture (occasional adult use vs frequent adolescent high-potency use) without propaganda or minimisation.
2. Describe cannabis intoxication, its pleasant and unpleasant faces (including panic and acute toxic reactions).
3. Diagnose cannabis withdrawal, and reframe the "lazy adolescent" presentation.
4. Explain the **cannabis–psychosis relationship** as a dose-response, gene-environment interaction, and answer the "did cannabis cause schizophrenia?" question honestly.
5. Recognise synthetic cannabinoid toxicity: the emergency door.
6. Build the treatment plan: motivational work, structured reduction, family contract, comorbidity treatment.
7. Counsel the schizophrenia-family question: "he smokes a little, is it okay?": with the relapse data.
8. Address Indian legal/social realities: NDPS framing, bhang-normalisation, edibles culture.

## 4. Epidemiology

### Global
- Estimated **around 220 million past-year users**, the most-used illicit substance on earth; ~4% of the global adult population.
- About 1 in 10 users develop dependence overall; ~1 in 6 with adolescent onset; past-year users with daily use patterns carry the syndrome burden.
- High-potency cannabis markets (skunk-type products) have shifted the risk curve; in those markets, daily high-potency users show the strongest psychosis associations.

### India
- The national survey lineage: about **3% of adults** used cannabis in the past year (ganja/charas beyond legal bhang), with **higher rates among urban young men and street youth**; dependence burdens concentrate in this group.
- Cultural layering matters: **bhang** (legal, festival-normalised) builds permissive attitudes; **charas/ganja** carry religious-mystical narratives; **vapes, edibles and semi-commercial oils** reach school and college populations who would never touch a chillum.
- Synthetic cannabinoid street products ("spice"-type) have appeared in urban seizure data and casualty presentations, the emergency tier of this note.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

- **Pharmacological:** potency (THC content, modern bred products many times the village-varieties), route (smoking/vaping onset speed), frequency (daily vs occasional), and the **CBD content** (antipsychotic-adjacent buffer largely bred out of high-THC products).
- **Developmental:** age of onset (the pre-25 brain is building its prefrontal control and endocannabinoid maturation; early start multiplies every risk).
- **Genetic:** family history of psychosis (the interaction is multiplicative); AKT1/COMT-type gene-interaction literature exists, clinical translation: **family history is the usable screening variable**.
- **Psychological:** relief-seeking for social anxiety, insomnia, ADHD-type restlessness and trauma; the "quiet-the-mind" answer that must be tested, not assumed.
- **Social:** peer normalisation; availability; the "harmless herb" myth absorbed from adult culture; edibles/vapes marketed invisibility; the studying-from-home era's unstructured days.

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: the volume knob and the filament.** The CB1 receptor is the brain's own fine-tuner of mood, memory, appetite and reward, using its own cannabis-like molecules (anandamide-type) as short-range whispers. THC shouts through the same channel: relaxation, time-warping, hunger. The system adapts by **turning down its own production and sensitivity**, so without the drug, the whispering system is quiet: flat mood, irritability, poor sleep, lost appetite, craving. That is cannabis withdrawal, real, physical, and week-long.

**Story 2: the unfinished building.** The prefrontal cortex (judgement, planning, motivation) is under construction until roughly 25, and its endocannabinoid wiring is part of that construction. Daily THC during the building years is like double-locking the scaffolding: **learning, motivation and emotional regulation take the hit; and the addiction machinery, still wet cement, sets around the drug.**

**Story 3: the psychotic diathesis door.** In brains carrying vulnerability (family history, early subtle signs), high-potency THC does not merely relax; it can **switch on the dopamine salience machinery** this site knows from the schizophrenia note: meaning becomes too bright, coincidences become messages, perception loosens. For most, it passes with the high. For the vulnerable, with dose and repetition, it can **precipitate the illness years earlier and colder**: the honest population-level sentence is that frequent high-potency use accounts for a meaningful minority of psychotic cases, preventable cases.

## 7. Symptoms

**Intoxication (the pleasant and the ugly):**
- Pleasant: relaxation, laughter, sensory enhancement, time distortion, appetite.
- Ugly (especially dose, inexperience, anxiety-prone): **panic attacks, depersonalisation/derealisation ("I am outside my body"), paranoid thoughts, nausea/vomiting, tachycardia**, and the "white-out" faint of novice orthostatic drops.
- Cognitive: immediate memory and attention clouded during effect; driving impaired for hours beyond the subjective "fine" (the road-safety point).

**Chronic heavy use:**
- Tolerance and **withdrawal on stopping**: irritability, anxiety, insomnia with strange vivid dreams, appetite loss with weight drop, craving, headaches, starting day 1–3, peaking around day 2–6, easing over 1–3 weeks.
- **The grey zone:** apathy, lost initiative, blunted ambition, academic and vocational slide, "amotivational" presentation.
- Cognition: attention and working-memory thinning during heavy use, largely recovering over weeks-months of abstinence (adolescent-onset recovery is slower and less complete).
- Chronic bronchitis (smoking); **cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome**: the paradoxical cyclical vomiting of daily long-term users, classically relieved by hot showers (the exam pearl, and frequently missed in casualty).

**Psychosis interface:**
- Transient toxic psychosis at high dose (clears with the drug).
- **Cannabis-associated persistent psychosis** in heavy vulnerable users.
- In established schizophrenia: use roughly **doubles relapse rates**, worsens positive symptoms, undermines medication adherence.

**Synthetic cannabinoid toxicity (the emergency door):** severe agitation, seizures, psychosis, hyperthermia, hypertension, arrhythmias; worse than anything the plant produces (full CB1 agonism, dose-unpredictable sachets).

## 8. Diagnosis

- The **severity-graded single-disorder logic** applied to cannabis; frequency + age-of-onset + potency are the risk modifiers to record.
- **The quantified history:** grams/₹ per week, potency (vape oil/ganja/charas), age at first use, days-per-month, longest break and why it ended, academic/work function trajectory.
- **Screen every young psychosis patient** for use, with family collateral, and screen every heavy user's family history of psychosis (the two-directional screening rule).
- **Withdrawal diagnosis** in the "laziness/attitude" presentation of stopped-using adolescents: irritability + insomnia + appetite loss + craving in the first week of abstinence = withdrawal, not character.
- **Rule-outs:** hyperemesis presentation → the hot-shower clue and the cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome; panic presentations → cannabis panic vs primary panic disorder (the timeline tells: first panic attacks in the hours after use).
- **Synthetic product history** in severe/odd toxicity; ask in street-drug language, not brand language.

## 9. Management

### (a) The two-sided counselling script (use it early, use it honestly)
"Most people who use occasionally as adults are okay; that is the truth, and pretending otherwise destroys trust. But you (or your son) are not 'most people': starting early, using daily, high-potency products, and our family history together change the arithmetic. The brain still being built, the withdrawal that will be mistaken for attitude, and the risk of switching on something that may not switch off; these are the facts we act on."

### (b) Reduction and abstinence plan
- **Motivational interviewing** opens the door (the adolescent was sent, not referred); **contingency structures** (verified abstinence linked to privileges; phone, bike, allowance, college-attendance documentation) carry the evidence.
- Structured reduction for the dependent user who cannot go straight to zero: dated reduction calendar, trigger-mapping (the evening friend-circle, the hostel room, the exam panic), alternative evening architecture (gym/sport, the natural anandamide route), family contract as in the other notes.
- **No approved pharmacotherapy** (honest table): trials of N-acetylcysteine, gabapentin-type agents, and cannabinoid-adjacent strategies show modest or negative signals; the treatment is behavioural and structural.
- Treat the riders that self-medication was quieting: social anxiety, ADHD, trauma, insomnia; treating them is relapse prevention.

### (c) Specific situations
- **Cannabis withdrawal:** supportive framing (dated end: ~1–3 weeks), sleep hygiene, exercise, brief symptomatic cover where misery demands; warn the family in advance that week one looks like attitude; it is the brain, not the boy.
- **Cannabinoid hyperemesis:** the under-recognised casualty presentation; cessation is the only real treatment (hot showers/capsaicin topical are the classic clues and temporising measures); look for it in "recurrent gastritis" of daily users.
- **Psychosis presentations:** abstinence-first alongside psychiatric care; antipsychotic treatment per the schizophrenia note's logic; **re-diagnose at 4–6 weeks abstinence** where the picture is ambiguous (same rule as the stimulant note).
- **In established schizophrenia:** the family message with numbers; use roughly doubles relapse; the "just a little" exemption does not survive the data; motivational work (not confrontation) plus structured alternatives; treat the use as part of the psychosis treatment plan, documented at every visit.
- **Synthetic cannabinoid toxicity:** supportive emergency care, benzodiazepines for agitation/seizures, cardiac monitoring, cooling; then abstinence-focused follow-up (these products are not "cannabis care"; they are poison-team follow-up).

## 10. Indian practice

- **The legal frame:** NDPS governs ganja/charas (with bhang's anomalous quasi-legal traditional status); low-quantity personal-use provisions are distinct from trafficking penalties; counsel accurately, don't threaten mythologically.
- **The normalisation wall:** families of adolescents hear "even sadhus use it"; the answer is the two-sided script: dose, age, potency and family history, not the sadhu.
- **The hidden school layer:** edibles and vape oils reach populations that never touch the traditional forms, ask in product language ("gummies? oils? pens?"), not ceremony language.
- **The rehab-camp shortcut:** Indian "de-addiction camps" are mostly irrelevant for primary cannabis dependence (no dangerous withdrawal); the effective unit is the **structured family programme**; the wasted money is the camp.
- **Screening integration:** every young first-episode psychosis referral carries a cannabis screen and family collateral; every heavy-use adolescent gets the psychosis-family-history question; the two-directional rule is cheap and changes both sides' counselling.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: the "lazy" boy who was withdrawing.** A 17-year-old was dragged to the OPD for "laziness and attitude" after hostel authorities confiscated vape pens: three weeks after stopping daily use of 2 years, he was irritable, slept badly, ate little and refused to study. The diagnosis that changed the household was **cannabis withdrawal**, a neurological week, not a character flaw. Management: dated-withdrawal psychoeducation (irritability week 1, strange dreams week 2, appetite's return by week 3), contingency contract (phone/bike privileges linked to verified abstinence and morning college attendance), evening gym substitution, family sessions replacing surveillance with structure, and testing of the "racing thoughts" answer (comorbid social anxiety treated with CBT-based work and an SSRI). Six months: attending college, off daily use, one lapse mapped to the old friend-circle and survived.

*Teaching points: withdrawal misdiagnosed as attitude is the commonest Indian cannabis presentation; the "what does it do for you" answer (quieting thoughts) named the real treatment; contingency and structure outperformed confrontation.*

**Case 2: the joint before the voices.** A 19-year-old engineering student, daily high-potency user since 16, with a maternal uncle with schizophrenia, presented with three weeks of declining studies, social withdrawal, then referential thinking (classmates "signalling" through messages) and 2 a.m. voices. Cannabis screen positive; family minimized ("only smoking, he's young"). Treatment: monitored abstinence, low-dose antipsychotic for the active symptoms, psychoeducation with the two-directional family-history data, contingency-anchored follow-up and the 6-week re-assessment rule. Symptoms resolved fully over six weeks; the antipsychotic was tapered off at 6 months with an explicit written prevention contract; daily use was the trigger his family history had loaded.

*Teaching points: the vulnerability×dose×age multiplication; transient cannabis-associated psychosis resolves with abstinence but demands the prevention contract; the family's minimisation is the treatment target in the first three sessions.*

## 13. FAQ

**"Isn't cannabis natural and harmless, even our sadhus use it?"**
Occasional adult traditional-potency use is genuinely low-risk; I will not pretend otherwise. But today's products are many times stronger, and early, daily use in a developing brain is a different exposure entirely. The sadhu's occasional bhang and a 16-year-old's daily vape oil are not the same medicine.

**"Can you be addicted to weed? I thought it wasn't addictive."**
Yes, about 1 in 10 users overall, and closer to 1 in 6 who start young: tolerance, withdrawal (irritability, insomnia, appetite loss, craving), and failed attempts to stop. That is the addiction definition.

**"My son became lazy after he stopped. Isn't he just having attitude?"**
That pattern (irritable, sleepless, eating little in the first weeks after stopping) is cannabis withdrawal, a brain readjustment with a dated end. Punishing it as attitude breaks the alliance at the exact moment it is needed.

**"Did cannabis cause my brother's schizophrenia?"**
It contributed, and honesty serves better than blame: he had a loaded family background, and daily high-potency use from an early age pulled the trigger years earlier. Was it inevitable? Unknowable. Was the risk multiplied? Clearly. The useful sentence now: staying off it roughly halves his relapse risk.

**"He says it calms his racing thoughts. Should we let it?"**
Test the hypothesis: if an anxiety or attention problem genuinely drives the use, treating it properly (therapy, proper medicines, structure) removes the need the joint was filling, and unlike the joint, it does not carry the psychosis multiplier.

**"He only eats edibles, not smoking, is that safer?"**
Edibles remove the lung damage and delay the effect; the delay is its own danger (people re-dose before the first dose lands, and the "overdose" is a panic-and-vomiting night). The dependence and psychosis arithmetic are unchanged.

**"Is CBD oil the same thing?"**
CBD is the second, non-intoxicating cannabinoid, a different substance, with its own regulatory grey areas. The problem of this note is THC: the more modern breeding raised THC and stripped CBD, the steeper the psychosis association grew.

**"What are the emergency signs to watch for?"**
Severe agitation, seizures, chest symptoms or collapse after street "spice"-type sachets (synthetic cannabinoids); those are poison, not cannabis, and need hospital now. Repeated morning vomiting relieved by hot showers in a daily user is hyperemesis; stopping is the cure.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **Mnemonics:** the risk multipliers as **"Pot-Pot-Freq-FHx"** (potency, early start, frequency, family history); withdrawal as **"IAAC"** (Irritability, Anxiety, Appetite loss, Craving + sleep disturbance); hyperemesis as **"Daily user + cyclical vomiting + hot showers"**.
- **Highest-yield facts:** ~220 million users globally; 1-in-10/1-in-6 dependence; withdrawal is real (the misdiagnosis!); psychosis risk doubling-to-quadrupling in heavy early high-potency use; relapse-rate doubling in comorbid schizophrenia; hyperemesis shower pearl; synthetic cannabinoid toxicity tier; no approved pharmacotherapy.
- **Classic traps:** (1) missing withdrawal inside "adolescent attitude"; (2) diagnosing schizophrenia at first contact without a 4–6 week abstinence re-assessment; (3) hyperemesis treated as "gastritis" for years; (4) accepting "it's natural" as risk data; (5) confronting instead of motivational-interviewing the adolescent (loses the patient).
- **Viva favourites:** THC/CBD ratio story; gene×environment interaction (AKT1-type framing with family-history translation); anandamide system; two-directional screening; contingency management evidence; cannabinoid hyperemesis management.
- **Indian exam corner:** bhang-normalisation counselling; NDPS personal-use provisions; the school edibles/vape layer; the "camp is irrelevant, family programme is the unit" argument; tele-MANAS linkage for families.
- **Rehearse one line:** "Cannabis is the diagnosis where honesty is the treatment's engine: no propaganda, no denial, dose, age, potency and family history decide the risk."

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. The strongest risk multiplier profile for cannabis-associated psychosis is:**
A. Occasional adult use of traditional preparations
B. Daily high-potency use begun in adolescence with a family history of psychosis
C. Weekend social use after age 30
D. CBD-dominant products

**Answer: B.** Potency × early onset × frequency × family history, the multiplication that drives preventable cases.

**2. An adolescent stops daily cannabis and for 2 weeks is irritable, sleepless, eats little and craves. Diagnosis:**
A. Primary depression
B. Cannabis withdrawal syndrome
C. Personality disorder
D. Schizophrenia prodrome

**Answer: B.** The syndrome is real, week-limited, and the commonest Indian misdiagnosis ("attitude"); name it to keep the alliance.

**3. A daily long-term user has years of recurrent morning vomiting relieved by hot showers. Diagnosis:**
A. Migraine
B. Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome
C. Peptic ulcer
D. Bulimia

**Answer: B.** The hot-shower pearl; cessation is the definitive treatment; commonly misdiagnosed as "gastritis" for years.

**4. In a patient with established schizophrenia, ongoing cannabis use:**
A. Is neutral for outcome
B. Roughly doubles relapse risk and worsens positive symptoms
C. Improves negative symptoms reliably
D. Should be ignored as a lifestyle matter

**Answer: B.** The most actionable fact for psychosis families; the treatment plan treats the use as part of the psychosis care.

**5. Treatment evidence for cannabis use disorder chiefly involves:**
A. An approved maintenance agonist
B. Motivational interviewing, contingency structures and comorbidity treatment
C. Methadone
D. Long-term benzodiazepines

**Answer: B.** No approved pharmacotherapy exists; behavioural-structural evidence carries the field.

**6. Synthetic cannabinoid street products are more dangerous than plant cannabis because:**
A. They contain nicotine
B. They are full CB1 agonists with unpredictable doses, severe agitation, seizures, cardiac toxicity
C. They are always contaminated with opioids
D. They are injected

**Answer: B.** The receptor pharmacology and sachet-dose chaos create a genuinely toxic tier that plant cannabis never reaches.
