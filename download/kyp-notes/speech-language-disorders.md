# Speech & Language Difficulties in Children — The Disorder That Hides Behind Behaviour

## 1. In one line + overview

**Communication is the medium of learning, friendship and self-control, so a child who cannot understand or be understood presents, in the first instance, as a behaviour or school problem.**

This note covers the Oxford chapter on children's speech and language difficulties. Its central facts: speech and language development is the fastest and most consequential learning task of early childhood, and children must be competent communicators *before* school starts, because schooling runs on language. Difficulties span speech (intelligibility: phonology, dysarthria, cleft palate), language (vocabulary, syntax, word-finding) and social communication (pragmatics), and travel with psychiatric disorders in both directions: children with primary language impairment are at elevated risk of behaviour problems, and children with primary psychiatric disorders (ADHD, selective mutism) carry undetected language disorders. **Specific language impairment (SLI)** (language delayed/disordered out of proportion to otherwise normal non-verbal intelligence) affects 3–7% of children, is heritable, and is defined by contested exclusion/discrepancy criteria. The chapter's modern discoveries: these difficulties *persist* (into adolescence and adult life, affecting employment, relationships, and even arrest rates), they coexist uneasily with autism at the pragmatic boundary, and the speech and language therapist belongs inside the child mental health team.

## 4. Epidemiology

Speech and language difficulties broadly: up to **24.6%** of children by inclusive criteria. **SLI: 3–7%**, the tighter the discrepancy criteria, the fewer the cases. **Selective mutism: ~0.75–0.8%**, slightly more common in girls, onset typically 3–5 years. ADHD is the psychiatric disorder most commonly reported with speech and language difficulties; high rates of undetected language disorder are found in child psychiatric populations generally. **India**: no equivalent national data; the structural context, multilingual homes (language-switching children), mid-year medium switches, hearing loss from untreated otitis media, and thin speech-therapy coverage (speech-language pathology services are concentrated in cities and a handful of institutions), means both over-identification (normal bilingual variation misread as impairment) and severe under-identification (the child nobody can understand just falls behind silently).

## 5. Causes & risk factors

- **Hearing impairment** (the first exclusion to check), visual impairment, general learning disability, epilepsy, and syndromes (Down, fragile X), the *explained* speech and language difficulties.
- **SLI (unexplained)**: heritable, genetic research targets both general processing-deficit accounts (phonological memory deficit as genetic marker) and modular accounts (tense-marking/syntax representational deficits); specific cognitive deficits in phonological memory, verbal and visuospatial memory, and symbolic play despite normal non-verbal IQ.
- **Structural/neurological**: cleft lip/palate (with post-repair fistulas and velopharyngeal incompetence), cerebral palsy (dysarthria).
- **Developmental**: phonological disorders with auditory processing/discrimination involvement; childhood apraxia of speech (neuromotor planning debated).
- **Acquired**: head injury, cerebrovascular lesions, cerebral infection, tumours, epilepsy, including **Landau–Kleffner** syndrome (acquired aphasia with seizures, usually ages 3–7, receptive language devastated).
- **Environmental**: language stimulation affected by poor parent–child interaction or the child's inability to attend (the ADHD interaction loop).
- **Selective mutism**: multi-factorial, social phobia and anxiety central; comorbid behaviour problems, communication difficulties, developmental delay.

## 6. What happens in the mind: three short stories

**The five-year gate story.** Most children complete language acquisition so fast it looks effortless, speaking clearly, understanding and building complex grammar, negotiating and predicting, conversing confidently with adults and peers, knowing letter sounds and reading some words *by school entry*. Language is not a school subject; it is the medium of every school subject. A child who crosses the school gate without this equipment begins failing immediately, and the failure compounds: language → literacy → attainment → self-esteem → behaviour. The critical-age hypothesis makes the clinical rule: difficulties still impairing communication *after age 5* are significant and warrant prioritised intervention.

**The dissociation story (SLI).** Some children's language runs years behind their non-verbal intelligence, a puzzle that fuels the deepest debates in cognitive science: is language a separable mental module, or the visible end of general processing limits? Clinically, the dissociation means the child *looks* capable (normal non-verbal IQ, often normal social interests) but cannot follow instructions, retrieve words, or build sentences; the "goed to make some vegetable circles" transcript in the chapter shows the exhausting effort of a child with word-finding and syntax problems attempting a simple narrative.

**The pragmatic boundary story.** Some children with fluent grammar and good vocabularies still fail conversationally, not knowing how much information a listener needs, misreading humour and sarcasm, monologuing on special interests. Historically labelled "semantic-pragmatic disorder" within SLI, this profile (Pragmatic Language Impairment) now sits on contested ground between SLI and autism: the children show the conversational impairments *without* the non-verbal repetitive behaviours of autism. The practical rule: pragmatic impairment alone does not diagnose autism, and any child's social communication difficulty may be *compounded* by language difficulty that treatment can reduce.

## 7. Symptoms: the difficulty types

**Speech difficulties.** *Cleft lip/palate*: post-repair velopharyngeal incompetence and fistulas impairing intelligibility; therapy from birth (feeding first, then speech). *Dysarthria*: neurological (cerebral palsy), slow, weak, uncoordinated speech; severe forms with shallow breathing, nasal voice, reduced vowel/consonant range. *Developmental phonological disorder*: intact musculature but an incomplete or atypical speech-sound system with systematic error patterns (always replacing 's' with 'd'); auditory processing implicated; often resolves with therapy, sometimes persists. *Childhood apraxia of speech*: inconsistent errors plus oral-motor difficulty (drooling, feeding, blowing); reduced babbling; often co-occurs with motor apraxia; a residual label for persistent, unexplained speech disorder. *Stuttering*: not an articulatory problem; core behaviours are repetitions, blocks, prolongations and struggle; secondary avoidance behaviours (circumlocution, telephone avoidance); normal non-fluency between 2–5 years resolves spontaneously.

**Language difficulties.** *Vocabulary*: small vocabularies; word-finding difficulty with searching behaviours ("I can't do that thing... you know... where you put sharp shoes on"); fillers ("stuff", "thingy"); gesture and first-sound substitutions; linked to phonological working memory. *Syntax*: problems with tense inflections ("goed"), complex sentences and passives; narratives fall apart ("they is erm... printing them out and then they put them in the oven because they'll taste crunchy [to cook]"). *Social communication/pragmatics*: eye contact, initiation, turn-taking, sharing, requesting and responding problems; higher-level: inferring information, giving the listener adequate information, self-monitoring; secondary to the language difficulty in SLI, intrinsic in autism, compounding in both.

**Syndrome patterns**: Down syndrome (vocabulary stronger than grammar); Williams syndrome (apparently competent communicators with significant language learning problems, the fluent surface misleading); learning disability generally (language parallel to IQ with delay; profound disability may never establish communicative intent; mild-moderate plateau in adolescence).

**Selective mutism.** Persistent refusal to talk in certain social situations despite speaking in others, classically talking at home, not at school; not better explained by a communication disorder, pervasive developmental disorder or psychosis; duration >1 month (not the first month of school; bilingual children: 6 months and both languages); interfering with education and social achievement. Social anxiety is the predominant feature; many affected children nonetheless have measurable speech and language difficulties (articulation, expressive and receptive).

## 8. Diagnosis

**The framework**: is the difficulty *speech*, *language* (comprehension/production; vocabulary/syntax), or *social communication*? Is it *specific* (cognitive development age-appropriate) or *explained* (hearing loss, syndrome, structural, neurological)? Language is "delayed" when behind chronological age and "impaired/disordered" when the delay persists severely. Exclude hearing loss first, always. **SLI criteria** (contested): ICD-10 strict, language ≥2 SD below chronological age *and* ≥1 SD below non-verbal IQ; liberal: non-verbal IQ ≥75 with language often 1 SD below mean; strict criteria risk missing children with poor long-term outcomes, liberal criteria risk pathologising the low-normal tail. **The psychiatric interface**: screen children with ADHD for receptive, expressive and pragmatic difficulties (excessive talking, poor topic maintenance); assess language in any child with unexplained behaviour problems, school failure or social difficulty; undetected language disorder is common in psychiatric populations; for selective mutism, verify language competence before assuming pure anxiety. **Identification checklist** (school-age): speech, difficult to understand? sounds omitted? less intelligible in sentences than single words? comprehension, follows conversations with children and adults? carries out verbal instructions? understands time/space concepts? production, wide vocabulary? complex sentences? can narrate past/future events? filler words and hesitation? social communication, participates in and enjoys conversation? contributions meaningful and relevant? appropriate verbal/non-verbal behaviour?, plus school functioning and persistence beyond age 5. Any positive → speech and language therapy referral.

## 9. Management

**Speech and language therapy (SLT) is the core service**, working across education, health and social care: schools (statements of special educational needs, individual learning plans, curriculum access), community programmes, child development centres, and increasingly **within CAMHS teams**, where the therapist identifies communication difficulties, assesses their impact on mental health, and delivers direct (individual/group) or indirect (family/classroom) intervention.

**Selective mutism treatment, multi-modal by aetiology**: behavioural (contingency management, shaping and stimulus fading, systematic desensitisation, self-modelling); CBT; family therapy (relationship contributions); psychodynamic play/art therapy; **fluoxetine has reported but unreplicated success**; SLT as adjunct: desensitising communication via a hierarchy of easy-to-hard speech tasks in easy-to-hard situations. The communication-facilitation rules: one-to-one settings with a familiar person first; accept non-verbal communication (nods, writing, drawing, gesture); whispering/quiet talking is easier than loud; factual or yes/no questions before feelings or opinions.

**General principles for communicating with language-impaired children**: forced-choice answers; break long instructions into short steps; slow delivery with pauses; short simple sentences, familiar vocabulary, no ambiguity; visual supports (pictures, objects, symbols); remember written language is impaired too. When you cannot understand the child: show interest, honestly say which parts you did and didn't understand, offer choices to narrow the guess, invite showing/pointing/drawing, and try again later without blame.

**Long-horizon planning**: risk stratification (severity, receptive involvement, low IQ, low SES = risk; pure speech difficulty, high IQ, high SES, specialist support = resilience); prioritise anything persisting beyond age 5; post-16 educational support for qualifications and workplace entry; inform families with the honest outcome literature.

## 10. Indian practice

- **The bilingual/multilingual rule**: assess in the child's strongest language, through an interpreter or bilingual worker where needed; normal code-switching and home-language delay before a school-language boom is *not* disorder; conversely, do not let "he'll catch up with English" hide a genuine impairment in the home language.
- **Hearing first, always**: untreated chronic otitis media with conductive loss is among the commonest reversible causes of "language delay" in Indian clinics, otoscopy and audiometry before any behavioural label.
- **Speech therapy scarcity**: speech-language pathology services cluster in metros and a few institutions; the realistic Indian pathway is therapist-designed, parent-and-teacher-delivered programmes with periodic review; the checklist and communication principles in this note are designed for exactly that delivery.
- **School accommodations**: RPwD Act provisions (scribes, extra time, exemptions where appropriate) apply to speech and language impairment as a specified disability; the certificate pathway matters more than most clinicians realise.
- **The behaviour gateway**: in Indian child guidance clinics, the language-impaired child usually arrives with a conduct or school-failure label; one screening pass with the checklist changes the management plan more often than any investigation.
- **Costs (approx 2026)**: audiometry and behavioural audiology are inexpensive in public centres; private speech therapy sessions are a recurring middle-class expense; the parent-delivered model costs time, not money.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: The "naughty boy" who couldn't follow instructions.** A 6-year-old in an English-medium school is referred for "oppositional behaviour and not concentrating". His teacher reports he "refuses to do work and fights at line-up". The checklist interview: speech barely intelligible in sentences; cannot carry out two-step verbal instructions without prompting; vocabulary narrow, filler-filled ("that thing... you know"); no narrative; enjoys rough play but conversation is minimal. Hearing: bilateral conductive loss from untreated otitis media with effusion, treated. Language assessment: receptive and expressive language far below chronological age, non-verbal IQ average. Formulation: specific language impairment presenting as behaviour; the "refusal" is incomprehension; the line-up fights happen where verbal instructions move fastest. Management: hearing treatment; SLT assessment with a school-delivered programme and home carryover; classroom adaptations (short steps, visual supports, forced-choice); teacher psychoeducation: the label changes from naughty to struggling. Teaching points: the behaviour is the referral ticket, the language is the diagnosis; hearing exclusion first; the school-medium switch can bury the problem.

**Case 2: The girl who speaks at home.** A 5-year-old has never spoken a word at school in six months, though she chatters at home. Teachers assume autism; the parents are told "she is stubborn". Assessment: at home she speaks in full sentences; at clinic she nods, points, and writes her name; hearing and language comprehension age-appropriate (verified with non-verbal testing); no restrictive or repetitive interests; a shy, watchful temperament with a similarly reticent father. Formulation: selective mutism (bilingual home, the 6-month both-languages criterion applied; duration satisfied). Management: multi-modal, stimulus fading (speech with mother in the classroom after hours, gradually widening), teacher coaching (accept nods and writing, no pressure to speak, yes/no questions first), family session (lowering the anxiety atmosphere, the father as ally in shared temperament), and a 4-week review with fluoxetine held in reserve. Speech returns at school over the following term. Teaching points: selective mutism is anxiety, not autism and not stubbornness; the fading hierarchy works from easy to hard; untreated mutism predicts later social anxiety.

## 13. FAQ

**"He'll just grow out of it, won't he?"**: Some do (normal non-fluency of 2-5-year-olds, many phonological difficulties); many do not. Difficulties persisting beyond age 5 predict literacy problems, lower attainment and adult psychosocial difficulty; the "wait and see" year is the year of compounding.
**"Is it delay or disorder?"**: Delay means the child is on the normal path but behind; disorder means the pattern is atypical or stuck. The distinction matters for prognosis and is made by formal assessment, not by watching.
**"His behaviour is the problem, why look at language?"**: Because the association runs both ways: language-impaired children are at elevated risk of behaviour problems, and behaviour-referred children often carry undetected language disorders. One checklist pass frequently rewrites the formulation.
**"Is speech therapy available and does it work?"**: Yes and yes: speech and language therapists assess, diagnose and treat; phonological disorders often resolve with input; programmes can be school- and home-delivered where therapist numbers are thin.
**"Could this be autism?"**: Only if the social communication impairment is intrinsic and pervasive (with the repetitive behaviours and interests of autism). Pragmatic difficulty alone does not diagnose autism, and in many children the social difficulties shrink when the language difficulty is treated.
**"She talks at home but not at all in school, is she being defiant?"**: That is selective mutism, an anxiety condition affecting up to 0.8% of children: speech is intact, the setting triggers silence. Treatment is graded, multi-modal, and works better early.
**"Will this affect his adult life?"**: Persisting difficulty raises risks: lower literacy and attainment, bullying, poorer employment and relationships in adults with SLI. The counterweights: pure speech difficulty, good IQ, support, and intervention before the compounding starts.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **The prevalence pair**: speech/language difficulties up to 24.6% (inclusive criteria); SLI 3–7%; selective mutism ~0.75–0.8% (girls > boys, onset 3–5).
- **SLI definition (short-answer classic)**: language impairment out of proportion to non-verbal IQ (within normal range), with no medical, neurological, sensory or functional cause; heritable; phonological-memory and syntax-deficit genetic-marker hypotheses.
- **The diagnoses list**: cleft palate, dysarthria (cerebral palsy), developmental phonological disorder, childhood apraxia of speech, fluency disorders, learning-disability patterns (Down vocabulary>grammar; Williams fluent-but-impaired), acquired childhood aphasias (Landau–Kleffner: aphasia + seizures, ages 3–7).
- **The critical age**: impairment persisting beyond 5 years = significant, prioritise intervention.
- **ADHD-language triad**: receptive, expressive, pragmatic (excessive talking, poor topic maintenance).
- **Selective mutism criteria**: persistent refusal in specific situations despite ability; not a communication/PDD/psychotic disorder; >1 month (bilingual: 6 months, both languages); functional interference; social anxiety core.
- **Outcome risk/resilience table**: a ready-made 5-mark answer.
- **Indian exam corner**: bilingual assessment rule; hearing exclusion (otitis media); RPwD entitlements; the behaviour-gateway screening habit.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**Q1.** Specific language impairment is defined by:
- (a) Language delay with below-average non-verbal intelligence
- (b) Language impairment disproportionate to non-verbal IQ within the normal range, with no identifiable medical, neurological, sensory or functional cause
- (c) Language difficulties caused by hearing impairment
- (d) Any speech difficulty at all

**Answer: (b).** The exclusion/discrepancy definition: with the ongoing strict-vs-liberal criteria debate about where the discrepancy thresholds should sit.

**Q2.** The "critical age" for prioritising speech and language intervention, per the hypothesis cited in the chapter, is:
- (a) 18 months
- (b) 3 years
- (c) 5 years
- (d) 11 years

**Answer: (c).** Difficulties still impairing communication after age 5, school entry, are significant and should be prioritised: language is the medium of schooling.

**Q3.** Which profile best describes Pragmatic Language Impairment (the SLI-autism boundary group)?
- (a) Fluent grammar and vocabulary, but inappropriate conversational behaviour without the non-verbal repetitive behaviours of autism
- (b) Complete mutism at school
- (c) Weak jaw muscles with dysarthria
- (d) Normal language with hearing loss

**Answer: (a).** Bishop & Norbury's subgroup: initiating conversations, humour/sarcasm, contextual adaptation and non-verbal communication impaired, but not autistic in the restrictive-behaviour sense.

**Q4.** A 5-year-old speaks fluently at home but has never spoken at school in 8 months. Language comprehension is age-appropriate. The most likely diagnosis is:
- (a) Autism
- (b) Social anxiety disorder with selective mutism
- (c) Landau–Kleffner syndrome
- (d) Oppositional defiant disorder

**Answer: (b).** Selective mutism: situation-specific silence with preserved ability, social anxiety core; autism requires pervasive social communication impairment plus repetitive behaviours.

**Q5.** In ADHD, the speech and language difficulties consistently reported include:
- (a) Only motor speech problems
- (b) Receptive and expressive language problems and pragmatic difficulties (excessive talking, poor topic maintenance)
- (c) Only stuttering
- (d) No association at all

**Answer: (b).** ADHD is the psychiatric disorder most commonly reported with speech and language difficulties, assess for them even when not the presenting complaint.

**Q6.** Among adults in their mid-30s with a history of specific language impairment, the long-term studies show:
- (a) Complete resolution of all difficulties without exception
- (b) High levels of social maladaptation with employment, relationships, independent living and health adversely affected
- (c) Better outcomes than non-impaired peers
- (d) Only speech difficulties persist

**Answer: (b).** The chapter's central corrective: this is not a benign, self-resolving childhood condition, and resilience factors (pure speech difficulty, high IQ, high SES, specialist support) mark the better-outcome paths.
