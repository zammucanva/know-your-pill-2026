# Anxiety, Stress-Related & Obsessional Disorders in the Elderly — Common, Costly, Treatable, Ignored

## 1. In one line + overview

**The neurotic disorders of old age are common, distressing, costly, and treatable, yet still go untreated or get treated with the wrong thing (a benzodiazepine) instead of the right things (an antidepressant and adapted CBT).**

This Oxford chapter covers stress-related, anxiety and obsessional disorders in the elderly. Its organising claims: these conditions are better understood as aspects of a **general neurotic syndrome** than as discrete categories (extensive comorbidity, diagnostic instability over time, all persisting into old age), with **OCD as the exception**, a distinct, stable condition with a different aetiology. Old age changes the presentation, not the suffering: worries focus on health, finances and crime; the fear of falling is the signature geriatric phobia; panic attacks get misrouted to cardiologists; new agoraphobia after 65 usually follows an alarming physical illness rather than panic; post-stroke anxiety becomes chronic and predicts poor functional recovery; and first-onset obsessions after 50 demand a search for organic disease. The aetiological frame is the **vulnerability–destabilisation–restitution** model, with old-age life events (illness, bereavement, retirement, institutionalisation) doing the destabilising. The management message is precise: primary care is where these live; **antidepressants first-line** (comorbid depression worsens outcomes); benzodiazepines reluctantly, short-acting, briefly; CBT adapted for sensory and cognitive limits; and prevention folded into stroke, heart-attack and aftercare.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- State the general-neurotic-syndrome model and why OCD stands apart from it.
- Describe how anxiety content and presentation change with age (health/finance/crime worries; fear of falling; somatic misrouting).
- Explain why "reasonable worry" must be judged by frailty and support, not by age alone.
- Work the differential web: depression (comorbidity and prognostic drag), dementia (bidirectional anxiety), delirium, paranoid states, and the physical disorders that present as anxiety in old age.
- Give the organic-first rule for late-onset obsessions (after 50: dementia or space-occupying lesion; or part of affective disorder).
- Quote the epidemiological pattern (community rates significant, clinical rates low, a filter-to-care problem; female preponderance; falling rates with age; late-onset phobic/panic/OCD exceptions).
- Apply the vulnerability–destabilisation–restitution model with old-age life events.
- Prescribe the treatment ladder: CBT adapted, SSRIs/venlafaxine first-line, benzodiazepine cautions (oxazepam), buspirone's slow start, beta-blocker contraindications.
- Deliver the primary-care management checklist and the post-event prevention opportunity.

## 4. Epidemiology

Neurotic disorders are **relatively uncommon in clinical populations but have significant community prevalence**; they do not pass easily through the filters on the pathway to care (the central service fact). Survey criteria differ, but general findings: female preponderance for most disorders; prevalence and incidence **fall with age**; most elderly people with neurotic disorders developed them before their fifties; the exceptions that arrive late being **phobic disorder, panic and OCD**. Anxiety-disorder mortality is elevated in elderly patients. **India**: community-old-age mental health surveys report persistent anxiety-symptom burden (often somatically expressed, "gas", "tension", sleep complaints), and the pathway filter is even tighter: anxiety in an Indian elder is normalised ("what do you expect at this age?"), somatised into medical clinics, or tranquillised with renewing benzodiazepine prescriptions rather than assessed; exactly the mistreatment pattern the chapter warns against.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

- **Premorbid vulnerability**: genetic factors contribute significantly (evidence from younger subjects; the old-age contribution is unknown), temperament, early experience.
- **Destabilisation, old-age life events**: physical illness, bereavement, retirement, institutionalisation; the *meaning* of the event matters (loss events → depression; threatening events → anxiety).
- **Social adversity and social networks**: smaller networks associate with anxiety disorders; but a hard life may equip one for old-age coping; the effect of adversity on self-esteem in old age is not straightforward.
- **Early experience**: parental loss and childhood physical/sexual abuse effects persist into old age.
- **Physical illness as cause**: life events (post-MI "cardiac neurosis"; post-stroke anxiety, some lesion-location relationship; arthritis, balance disorders and sensory impairment raise vulnerability and avoidance).
- **Physical illness as consequence**: decades of anxiety-driven smoking and drinking.
- **Drugs**: oral hypoglycaemics, corticosteroids, caffeine excess, sympathomimetic preparations.
- **Trauma**: PTSD in elderly survivors (war, holocaust) is persistent, with late onset or recurrence precipitated decades after the original trauma; late-life trauma also produces PTSD, which tends to persist.
- **Dementia**: associated with higher anxiety rates (unrelated to cognitive severity; vascular dementia possibly more vulnerable): from insight-related distress or response to psychotic misinterpretations; **caregivers of dementia patients carry high anxiety-depression risk**.

## 6. What happens in the mind: two short stories

**The thermostat story (vulnerability–destabilisation–restitution).** Picture a lifelong thermostat: each person's baseline setting (vulnerability) determines how far an insult (destabilisation) swings the system, and what patient and doctor then do (restitution, benzodiazepines, avoidance, worry rituals, reassurance-seeking) either restores the setting or elaborates the symptoms. Old age multiplies destabilising events (illness, loss, retirement, institutionalisation) while support systems thin. The same old thermostat now faces more shocks with fewer buffers; the symptoms that emerge are still anxiety and depression in their lifelong shapes, focused on the new realities: a body that can fall, a heart that has failed, a street that feels less safe.

**The somatic detour story.** Anxiety in the elderly exits through the body: palpitations send the patient to cardiology, dizziness to neurology, gut symptoms to gastroenterology; each specialty investigates honestly and finds nothing (or finds the true abnormality the anxiety has amplified). Meanwhile the real disorder (panic, phobia, generalised anxiety) remains untreated behind years of negative tests, and the "cardiac neurosis" consolidates: every somatic flutter now means *the heart*, avoidance follows, and deconditioning makes the next flutter more likely. The chapter's corrective: physical disorders can present as anxiety and anxiety presents as physical disorder, so the assessment always includes a drug history and physical examination, and a physical cause is suspected when there is **no past psychiatric history and no life event** to account for onset.

## 7. Symptoms: the geriatric faces

**Psychological.** Anxiety and depression mingle in all of these disorders. Elderly worry targets health, finances, crime; phobias resemble younger adults' with geriatric additions, **fear of falling** prominent. The critical judgement rule: clinically significant fears are dismissed as "reasonable for her age", but it is **physical frailty and the availability of social support, not age, that determine perceived vulnerability**; reasonableness should be assessed against those.

**Somatic.** Panic presentations get misdirected (cardiology, neurology, gastroenterology); misdiagnosis and inappropriate investigation are more likely at this age, not less.

**Behavioural.** Phobic avoidance; sedative and alcohol abuse (the mistreatment trap); abnormal illness behaviours: somatisation, hypochondriasis; in cognitively impaired patients, disturbed behaviour may be the *main presenting feature* of anxiety.

**Obsessional.** OCD features in old age resemble younger patients'; **first onset after 50 is rare**, and demands investigation for dementia or a space-occupying lesion, or recognition as part of a primary affective disorder.

**Late-onset patterns.** Most disorders are long-standing; the late-onset minority causes the diagnostic difficulty; post-65 agoraphobia usually arises after alarming physical ill health rather than panic; post-stroke anxiety disorders are common, become chronic in a significant proportion, and track poor functional recovery.

## 8. Diagnosis: the differential web

- **Depression**: extensive comorbidity; depressive symptoms are integral to many neurotic disorders in old age; assess and treat them in their own right; **comorbid anxiety predicts poorer antidepressant response and more relapse/recurrence**.
- **Dementia**: early dementia can present with anxiety and obsessinality; anxiety and depression cause *subjective* cognitive impairment (the presenting symptom); dementia carries higher anxiety rates independent of severity; screen when cognition and anxiety co-travel.
- **Delirium**: quiet in the elderly (see M1 note) but affectively stormy responses to frightening hallucinations; conversely severe anxiety can precipitate delirium in the vulnerable.
- **Paranoid states/schizophrenia**: fear in response to psychotic experience rarely confuses; unusual hypochondriacal ideas vs monosymptomatic delusions is the exception.
- **Physical illness**: cardiovascular, respiratory and endocrine disorders presenting as anxiety or depression with little else; drugs (oral hypoglycaemics, corticosteroids), caffeine, sympathomimetics. **The rule: drug history and physical examination always; suspect physical cause when no psychiatric history and no life event explains onset.**
- **Presentation setting**: these disorders present in primary care and the general hospital; the specialists who must recognise them; old-age psychiatry advises and takes the complex cases.

## 9. Management

**The framework (from the outset, wherever care happens)**: thorough assessment and accurate diagnosis; the full treatment range (patient education, lifestyle advice, bibliotherapy, supportive counselling); clear agreed goals; an adequate trial; stated duration; review frequency; anticipated adverse consequences (dependency, side effects, self-harm risk).

**Psychological.** CBT goals and techniques are the same as for younger adults (see the F-group notes) with **adaptations for sensory impairment, physical illness/disability, and cognitive dysfunction**; individual tailoring limits group CBT, though task-centred group activities (anxiety management) work well; clinicians should understand the psychodynamics of elderly concerns and defences, and audit their own preconceptions about elderly people's capacity for growth and change.

**Pharmacological.** *Benzodiazepines*: the most used and most inappropriately used; elderly sensitivity (accumulation → **delirium, incontinence, falls**); **oxazepam** (short half-life, no active metabolites) least problematic; withdrawal symptoms occur on erratic discontinuation; long-term use avoided where possible, reserved for the few unresponsive to everything else. *Antidepressants: now first choice in generalized anxiety and panic*, especially with depressive symptoms (SSRIs and venlafaxine carry the evidence); SSRIs specifically effective in OCD. *Neuroleptics*: limited role (extrapyramidal burden), short low-dose courses (haloperidol, zuclopenthixol) only for benzodiazepine-intolerant patients; hydroxyzine as a sedative antihistamine alternative. *Beta-blockers*: sympathetic symptom control limited by geriatric contraindications (COPD, sinus bradycardia, heart failure). *Buspirone*: well tolerated, but ~2 weeks to effect, useless acutely, indicated for severe chronic generalized anxiety and dependence-risk patients.

**Prevention.** Primary prevention is presently limited, but the physical-illness association opens a window: **intervene early after strokes, heart attacks and falls** to prevent chronic neurotic disability; whether better earlier-life treatment lowers late-life chronicity will show as cohorts age.

## 10. Indian practice

- **"Tension" is the doorway diagnosis.** Indian elders present with "tension", body heat, gas, sleeplessness and head pressure; beneath these somatic labels sit the full anxiety disorders. The screening skill: ask what the tension is *about* (health, money, safety, falling), when it started, and what happened just before.
- **The benzodiazepine street.** Indian prescriptions renew alprazolam/clonazepam/diazepam for years; the chapter's warnings (falls, confusion, accumulation) apply verbatim; converting long-term users to an SSRI plus taper is the single highest-yield geriatric intervention many Indian OPDs can offer. Oxazepam logic guides any necessary hypnotic.
- **Family-delivered exposure.** Graded exposure for fear of falling and post-illness agoraphobia is deliverable by trained family members (the accompany-then-wait ladder), the Indian joint family as behavioural co-therapist.
- **The post-event clinic moment.** Indian stroke and MI follow-ups rarely include an anxiety screen, one question ("Since the attack, does your chest fear stop you from going out?") identifies the post-event neurotic disability the chapter says is preventable.
- **Late-onset obsession rule.** New rituals or ruminations after 50 in an Indian elder are not "old-age habit"; they are dementia or structural disease until examined; the imaging and cognitive workup is available in district hospitals.
- **Costs (approx 2026)**: SSRIs are inexpensive generics in India; CBT capacity is thin (tele-MANAS counselling; the Bibliotherapy/education materials cost nothing); oxazepam and buspirone are available; the deconditioning-and-falls costs of untreated anxiety dwarf the treatment costs.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: The heart that fell.** A 70-year-old retired teacher, four months after an anterior MI, has stopped walking to the temple, will not climb the stairs to her flat, and checks her pulse hourly. Cardiology reviews are normal; her daughter reports she "has become housebound". No prior psychiatric history; onset clearly after the MI. Formulation: post-event agoraphobia and health anxiety ("cardiac neurosis"), the classic post-65 pattern, illness-triggered rather than panic-driven. Management: psychoeducation linking anxiety symptoms to autonomic arousal (the palpitations are adrenaline, not the stent failing); graded re-exposure (doorstep → courtyard → temple, with the daughter as company, then waiting); SSRI for the generalised anxiety component; cardiology review scheduled so reassurance is authoritative, not casual. At 3 months: temple visits resumed, pulse-checking abandoned. Teaching points: post-65 agoraphobia follows illness, not panic; deconditioning perpetuates symptoms; reassurance works only inside a graded exposure plan.

**Case 2: The new checker.** A 76-year-old man develops checking rituals (locks, gas stove, wallet) over five months, plus hoarding of newspapers; his family report memory complaints. No lifelong obsessinal history. Formulation: first-onset obsessional symptoms after 50; organic cause to be excluded. Cognitive testing shows impairments; MRI: frontal atrophy consistent with a frontotemporal process. The "OCD" is the presentation of a dementia, the checking driven by memory failure and frontal rigidity, not ego-dystonic obsession. Management: family psychoeducation reframing rituals as compensation; environmental adaptations (single lock, written checklist, safe stove); no SSRI-ERP package as such. Teaching points: the after-50 organic rule in action; behavioural management of the dementia's rituals, not treatment of OCD proper; informant history separates the lifelong from the late.

## 13. FAQ

**"Isn't worry normal at her age?"**: The content of worry (health, money, safety) is normal; clinically significant disorder is defined by severity, persistence, avoidance and suffering; judged against her frailty and support, not her birth certificate.
**"Why does the doctor prescribe an antidepressant for anxiety?"**: Because antidepressants (SSRIs, venlafaxine) are the first-line, evidence-backed treatment for generalized anxiety and panic in old age, and depression usually travels with the anxiety, while benzodiazepines, the old reflex, carry falls, confusion and dependence.
**"She's been on sleeping tablets for years, can she ever stop?"**: Yes, with a planned taper and replacement strategies; the long-term risks (falls, memory, dependence) are precisely why the chapter prefers withdrawal where possible.
**"Since his stroke he won't leave the house, is that physical weakness?"**: Sometimes both: post-stroke anxiety and agoraphobia are common, become chronic, and slow functional recovery; treating the fear is part of the rehabilitation.
**"Grandmother has started checking everything, is that OCD?"**: First-time rituals after 50 are more often the surface of a dementia or another brain condition; the workup comes before the label.
**"Can old people really benefit from talking therapy?"**: Yes: CBT works in later life, adapted for hearing, vision, mobility and memory; and older patients often bring the motivation and life perspective that make it succeed.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **Classification stance**: general neurotic syndrome (Tyrer), comorbidity and instability; OCD distinct and stable.
- **Reasonableness rule**: frailty + social support, not age, calibrate the judgement.
- **Late-onset patterns**: phobic, panic and OCD are the elderly late-onset exceptions; post-65 agoraphobia = physical illness trigger; post-50 obsessions = organic search.
- **Comorbidity drag**: depression + anxiety → poorer response, more relapse.
- **Physical mimics**: cardiovascular, respiratory, endocrine presentations; drug causes (hypoglycaemics, corticosteroids, caffeine, sympathomimetics); the "no history, no life event → suspect physical" rule.
- **Pharmacology table**: SSRIs/venlafaxine first-line; benzodiazepines reluctantly (oxazepam preferred, falls-delirium-incontinence risks); buspirone slow; beta-blockers contraindication-limited; neuroleptics minimal role.
- **CBT adaptations**: sensory, physical, cognitive tailoring; task-centred groups easier than group CBT.
- **Prevention window**: early intervention after stroke, MI, falls.
- **Indian exam corner**: "tension" somatic doorway; benzodiazepine-legacy conversion; family-delivered exposure; the after-50 rule in vignettes.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**Q1.** The model best describing the relationship among elderly stress-related and anxiety disorders (excepting OCD) is:
- (a) Discrete, mutually exclusive categories
- (b) Aspects of a general neurotic syndrome with extensive comorbidity and diagnostic instability
- (c) Subtypes of schizophrenia
- (d) Entirely physical diseases

**Answer: (b).** The chapter's classification stance: with OCD standing apart as distinct and stable with different aetiology.

**Q2.** Agoraphobia first developing after the age of 65 is most commonly:
- (a) Panic-driven, as in younger adults
- (b) A consequence of an alarming experience of physical ill health
- (c) A schizophrenia prodrome
- (d) Always malingering

**Answer: (b).** The geriatric pattern: illness-triggered avoidance; hence the prevention window in stroke, MI and fall aftercare.

**Q3.** Obsessional symptoms appearing for the first time after age 50 warrant investigation for:
- (a) Nothing: normal ageing
- (b) Dementia or a space-occupying lesion (or recognition as part of an affective disorder)
- (c) Schizophrenia in all cases
- (d) Simple tension

**Answer: (b).** First-onset obsessions in old age are rare and organic until excluded, the after-50 rule.

**Q4.** Which benzodiazepine is least problematic in elderly patients because of its short half-life and absence of active metabolites?
- (a) Diazepam
- (b) Nitrazepam
- (c) Oxazepam
- (d) Chlordiazepoxide

**Answer: (c).** Oxazepam's profile minimises accumulation and its delirium-incontinence-falls consequences; long-term use remains to be avoided regardless of agent.

**Q5.** Depression comorbid with anxiety in the elderly predicts:
- (a) Better antidepressant response
- (b) Poorer response to antidepressant treatment with greater likelihood of relapse and recurrence
- (c) No difference
- (d) Spontaneous cure

**Answer: (b).** The prognostic drag of comorbidity, one reason to assess the depressive component explicitly before planning treatment.

**Q6.** The prevention opportunity highlighted by the chapter for elderly neurotic disability lies in:
- (a) Annual psychotherapy for all elders
- (b) Early intervention after strokes, heart attacks and falls
- (c) Universal benzodiazepine prophylaxis
- (d) Avoiding all medical care

**Answer: (b).** The physical-illness association makes event follow-up the practical prevention point, screening one question at every post-event review.
