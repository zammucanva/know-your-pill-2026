# Alzheimer's disease and dementia

## 1. In one line + overview

**Tagline:** Dementia is a progressive loss of thinking and daily function big enough to change a person's life; Alzheimer's disease is its commonest cause; a slow, years-long shrinking of memory that currently cannot be cured but can be treated, supported and planned for.

**Overview, in plain words:** Everyone forgets keys; that is not dementia. Dementia begins when a person **cannot manage the day they used to manage** (the accounts go wrong, the familiar route becomes strange, the same question returns ten times a day) and it gets slowly worse over **months and years**, not days. Alzheimer's disease accounts for roughly **6 in 10 dementias worldwide**. It rises steeply with age: uncommon before 60, affecting perhaps 1 in 10 people over 65 and a larger share of those over 85. It is a disease of the brain's cells and connections, not "just old age", and never a punishment or a curse. Two truths to hold together: **there is no cure yet**, and **there is a great deal that medicine and family can still do**; symptomatic medicines that buy function, programmes that keep people walking and socially alive, planning that protects money and dignity, and support for the real treatment team, which in India is almost always the family.

## 2. Learning objectives

After working through this note, you should be able to:

1. Define dementia in one sentence and distinguish it from normal ageing, mild cognitive impairment, delirium and depression.
2. Describe the natural course of Alzheimer's disease across early, middle and late stages.
3. Explain amyloid and tau in plain words, and why "plaques and tangles" do not tell the whole story.
4. List the risk factors, separating the non-modifiable from the modifiable (and name the "life-course" view).
5. Assemble a practical diagnostic workup: history, cognitive testing, bloods, imaging, and know what biomarkers add.
6. Use the four antidementia drug groups with realistic expectations, doses and monitoring.
7. Manage the behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD) without defaulting to sedation.
8. Advise an Indian family on caregiver support, legal and financial planning, and available services.

## 4. Epidemiology

### Global

- With population ageing, the estimate most quoted by WHO is **around 55–57 million people living with dementia worldwide**, projected to roughly triple by 2050 as more countries survive into the high-risk ages.
- Prevalence roughly **doubles every 5–6 years after age 65**: about 5–8% at 65+, well above 20% after 85. More women than men live with dementia, largely because women live longer.
- Alzheimer's is 50–70% of all dementia; vascular dementia is second; dementia with Lewy bodies and frontotemporal dementia (both separate notes) follow.
- Dementia is now among the leading causes of disability and dependence in older people, and one of the costliest chronic conditions for families and health systems.

### India

- The **Dementia India Report 2020 (ARDSI/ALZ order of magnitude)** estimated about **8.8 million Indians above 60 with dementia**; projected to rise steeply by 2050 as life expectancy grows. (The older 2010 report estimated ~3.7 million; methods differ.)
- The landmark **10/66 Dementia Research Group studies in rural Tamil Nadu and Kerala** found dementia prevalence in elderly Indians **comparable to, and in some sites higher than, high-income countries**, demolishing the old belief that Indian families somehow "immunise" against dementia.
- The **National Mental Health Survey of India 2015–16** found geriatric mental morbidity high and treatment nearly absent; the treatment gap for dementia is commonly quoted in the **90% range** in community terms.
- Care is overwhelmingly home-based: fewer than a fraction of a percent of affected persons access day-care or residential services; the median primary carer is a daughter or daughter-in-law, often with no respite.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**Non-modifiable:**
- **Age**: the strongest risk by far; Alzheimer's is rare before 60 unless there is a genetic syndrome.
- **Family history**; one first-degree relative with late-onset disease roughly doubles risk; several affected relatives raise it more.
- **Genetic:** *APOE ε4* (the common risk variant for late-onset disease: risk roughly 2–3× with one copy, ~10–15× with two, though many carriers never develop dementia); rare autosomal-dominant families with mutations in *APP, PSEN1, PSEN2* cause onset in the 40s–50s, under 1% of all Alzheimer's.
- **Down syndrome**: by the fourth decade, the extra chromosome 21 gene dose produces classic Alzheimer's pathology; plan for it.
- **Sex:** women live longer, so more women are affected; some evidence also suggests their risk per year lived post-menopause is somewhat higher.

**Modifiable / life-course (the empowering list):**
- Midlife **hypertension**, **diabetes**, **obesity**, **smoking**, excessive **alcohol**; later-life **physical inactivity**, **social isolation**, **depression**, **untreated hearing loss**, **air pollution**, and low early-life **education**.
- The "life-course" synthesis (Lancet Commission style) suggests a **large minority of dementia risk, potentially around 40–45%**, is theoretically modifiable; the honest caveat: reducing these risks shifts population odds, it is not a personal guarantee.
- **Head injury** with loss of consciousness raises later risk, relevant to contact sports and road-traffic injury in India.
- Indian context: rapidly rising diabetes and hypertension, widespread uncorrected hearing loss, rising air pollution, and mass rural-to-urban migration of younger people leaving elders isolated, all push the future caseload up. Conversely: physical work, lifelong social engagement, and cognitive reserve from literacy appear protective in Indian cohort data.

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: a fire that starts in the memory room.** Two abnormal proteins build up. **Amyloid-β** clumps between cells into sticky plaques; **tau** twists inside neurons into tangles that strangle their transport systems. The process starts silently in the **hippocampus**, the brain's memory gate, and spreads along connected networks like a slow forest fire: first memory, then language, then visuospatial function, then the frontal lobes that hold judgement and behaviour, and finally the motor and swallowing centres. This order of spread is why the *symptoms* arrive in such a predictable sequence.

**Story 2: the chemical that fades.** A small cluster of cells deep in the base of the brain (the nucleus basalis of Meynert) supplies acetylcholine to the cortex, the chemical of learning and attention. These cells die early in Alzheimer's. The memory medicines do not douse the fire; they **squeeze the remaining acetylcholine harder** (cholinesterase inhibitors stop its breakdown). That is a genuine but modest gain, months of preserved function, not a reversal.

**Story 3: the brain fighting back, and losing slowly.** The brain's immune cells (microglia) clear amyloid for years. There is a long silent stage (amyloid is visible on scans **10–20 years before symptoms**) while the system compensates. Dementia begins when reserve runs out: the brain has been shrinking quietly, and the moment it can no longer compensate, decline becomes visible and steadily progressive. This is also why intellectual reserve (education, bilingualism, complex work) delays the *symptoms*, though not the underlying disease.

## 7. Symptoms

**Early (the family usually notices first, and the patient covers up):**

- Repeating questions; conversations loop. Forgetting recent events while old memories stay vivid.
- Misplacing objects in odd places (keys in the fridge); losing the way on familiar routes.
- Word-finding trouble: names of people and things slip; speech becomes vague ("that thing for that").
- Poor handling of money: paying twice, falling for scams, difficulty with change at a shop.
- Personality subtleties: less initiative, less interest in hobbies, irritability when challenged, defensive jokes to hide slips.
- **Insight is patchy and often bravely denied**, the person hides mistakes before anyone else sees them.

**Middle:**

- Disorientation to time then place; mixing day and night; wandering and getting lost in one's own lane.
- Apraxia: cannot sequence dressing, cooking, shaving, using a phone; asks for objects that are in hand.
- Suspicion and misidentification: accuses family of stealing, does not recognise a mirror image, asks for dead relatives.
- BPSD: behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia: agitation, restlessness, repetitive calling out, sleep reversal, hoarding, aggression during personal care.
- Incontinence appears; personal hygiene needs prompting then doing-for.

**Late:**

- Speech reduces to phrases, then words, then muteness.
- Recognises few; needs full feeding, toileting and turning; eventually bed-bound.
- Swallowing fails; aspiration pneumonia and sepsis close the final chapter; 8–12 years is the typical span from diagnosis, with wide variation.

**Anosognosia** (the medical word for "not knowing one is ill") is common and is not stubbornness; damaged self-monitoring circuits cannot see the deficit.

## 8. Diagnosis

### The logic of DSM-5 / ICD-11 (paraphrased)

DSM-5 speaks of **major neurocognitive disorder (dementia)** as: a noticeable decline from the person's own baseline in one or more thinking domains (memory, language, executive function, attention, visuospatial or social cognition) confirmed by testing and by daily-life loss of independence; not occurring only in delirium; not better explained by another brain disease or substance. **Probable Alzheimer's disease** is declared when the picture is insidious onset, gradual progression, and the lost domains follow the typical Alzheimer's pattern, with no other explanation found. ICD-11 carries the same architecture under its own code structure.

### How it is actually diagnosed (mostly clinical)

- **Collateral history is the spine of the diagnosis**: onset, pace, what was lost first, driving, money, medicines, alcohol, hearing and vision, mood, sleep. Always interview the family separately.
- **Bedside cognitive testing:** the informants' questionnaires (e.g. IQCODE-style history from relatives) and brief tests such as **MMSE or MoCA** (named here; items not reproduced; MoCA is more sensitive for early disease; Hindi and other Indian-language adaptations exist and should be used for non-English speakers). Scores stage severity; the *pattern* of deficits tells you the subtype.
- **Functional yardstick:** what can he still do without help? (This is what "major" vs "mild" hinges on.)
- **Bloods to exclude the treatable mimics, in every new case:** thyroid function, vitamin B12 (folate), complete blood count, sugar, kidney and liver function, calcium, and in risk-appropriate cases: VDRL (syphilis), HIV. This panel is cheap in India and catches the reversible impostors.
- **Imaging:** structural CT/MRI to exclude a tumour, subdural collection, hydrocephalus and vascular burden, and to look at hippocampal volume. A *normal-looking* brain does not exclude Alzheimer's; early scans often look near-normal.
- **Biomarkers (tertiary centres):** CSF amyloid/tau ratios and amyloid or tau PET; in practice they are mainly used in younger or atypical cases, and increasingly to qualify patients for anti-amyloid antibodies, very expensive and still debated, and essentially unavailable in routine Indian public care.
- **New-stage terminology to know:** *MCI / mild neurocognitive disorder*, complaints with test deficits **but independence preserved**; each year a fraction (roughly 10–15%) of MCI cases convert to dementia; MCI is the stage where prevention advice is most worth giving.

### Differentials: the must-know table

| Mimic | Clue that it is NOT Alzheimer's | Quick action |
|-------|--------------------------------|--------------|
| Depression ("pseudodementia") | Onset in weeks, "I don't know" answers, poor effort on testing but intact when coaxed; often says memory is terrible | Treat depression, re-test in 2–3 months |
| Delirium | Sudden, fluctuating, inattention, illness/drug trigger | Full delirium workup (see delirium note) |
| Normal ageing | Slower recall but functions independently; recognises "I forgot where I parked" vs "I forgot I parked" | Reassure; no disease label |
| Vascular dementia | Stepwise course, hypertension/strokes, early gait and slowing | See its own note |
| Hypothyroidism / B12 deficiency | Slow thinking with the systemic picture, recent onset | Test and treat |
| Normal-pressure hydrocephalus | Gait disorder first, then urinary incontinence, then cognition | MRI; neurosurgical opinion |
| Frontotemporal dementia | Behaviour or language first, memory preserved, onset < 65 | See its own note |

## 9. Management

### (a) What to tell the family in the first month (the real foundation)

- Name the illness honestly; correct the "just old age" and "madness" beliefs.
- Register the illness, not just the medicine: a written plan, a named carer roster, a safety review (gas, stove, wandering, money), and legal steps **while insight allows**.
- Write down the **cognitive reserve advice**: daily walking, music, prayer, social contact, fixed routines; the life the person still has slows the slide.

### (b) Symptomatic medicines (realistic framing)

- **Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors**: donepezil (start 5 mg at night, may rise to 10 mg after 4–6 weeks), rivastigmine (oral or patch), galantamine. Use in mild–moderate disease. Expected effect: a modest stabilisation, months of retained function; a meaningful minority show clear improvement in daily activity. Not a cure; disease continues underneath.
- **Memantine** (start 5 mg, titrate to 20 mg/day): add or switch in moderate–severe disease; helps cognition a little and agitation in some; well tolerated in elderly kidneys.
- **Monitoring:** reassess cognition, function and behaviour every 3–6 months. If the drug has clearly stopped helping in late-stage disease (swallow failure, muteness), tapering is ethical and honest.
- **Avoid:** long-term antipsychotics for BPSD (mortality and stroke signal), and anything anticholinergic (bladder drugs, first-generation antihistamines, tricyclics); they directly oppose what little acetylcholine remains.
- **New anti-amyloid antibodies** (lecanemab and relatives): modest slowing of decline in trials, with brain swelling/bleed risk; presently costly and beyond routine practice almost everywhere; know their existence for exams, not your clinic.

*India note: generic donepezil 5 mg and 10 mg and memantine are inexpensive; donepezil roughly ₹150–400/month, memantine similar (approx, 2026, varies by brand/state/scheme); rivastigmine patch is far costlier and rarely needed first. Where cost blocks even this, prioritise the free things: routines, walking, treating hearing and vision, treating pain and constipation; these often help behaviour more than tablets.*

### (c) BPSD: treat the trigger, not just the symptom

- Work through the mnemonic backwards: **P**ain, **F**aecal impaction, **U**rinary infection, **H**unger/thirst, **E**nvironment (noise, dark, new room), **S**leep. In dementia, behaviour *is* the language of physical discomfort.
- Non-drug first: same carer, same routine, bright days and dark nights, music, walks, one instruction at a time, do **with** not **to**; redirect rather than argue.
- If a drug is truly needed: smallest dose, fixed review date; short-term quetiapine or risperidone in low dose has the best pragmatic evidence, and only if the danger or distress outweighs the risks; antidepressants (SSRIs) if depression drives the picture.
- Sleep reversal: daytime light, activity, no daytime napping in chairs; avoid sedative cocktails.

### (d) The diseases that travel with it: keep treating them

Blood pressure, diabetes, lipids, hearing, vision, dental, feet, and constipation. Vascular risk control protects the brain from a *second* illness, and "mixed dementia" is the rule in old brains, not the exception.

*India note: nearly every family asks for a "brain tonic" or "memory injection". Say clearly: none of the marketed tonics, high-dose multivitamin combos or ginkgo products has convincing evidence for established Alzheimer's. That saves the family money and keeps trust in the medicines that do work.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **Guidelines actually used:** Indian Psychiatric Society guidance and WHO dementia knowledge frameworks guide practice; the **Dementia India Report (ARDSI)** drives national policy conversation; the **National Programme for Health Care of the Elderly (NPCHCE)** carries the government mandate.
- **Where patients present:** first to a family physician or a neurologist, then often a psychiatrist for the behaviour, or a faith healer first. The path to diagnosis is typically 2–3 years long, families normalise the early stage as "old age" until wandering or accusations force a consultation.
- **Private practice reality:** those who can pay get MRI and a neuropsychology battery; those who cannot often get a label without even the basic bloods. Push for the minimum: thyroid, B12, sugar, and a documented cognitive score, cheap and decisive.
- **Caregiver reality:** one daughter-in-law, 24×7, with no training, no respite and rising depression. Every prescription should include a **carer check-in**; the caregiver's health is the patient's health. ARDSI (Alzheimer's and Related Disorders Society of India) runs day-care and family support chapters in some cities, point families to the nearest one.
- **Money and law:** while the patient still has insight: joint bank mandates, a trusted-mandate arrangement, nominee updates, a written family agreement, and, under the Mental Healthcare Act 2017, future supported decision-making and advance directives can be discussed. Nomination and access problems, not medicine, are the commonest late-stage crises.
- **End-stage care:** tube feeding is frequently chosen out of guilt or pressure; counsel honestly that hand-feeding with careful positioning carries comparable comfort and dignity in advanced dementia.
- **Primary care role:** the MBBS doctor is usually the first and only doctor many families ever see, capable of the clinical diagnosis (collateral history + brief test + basic bloods), starting donepezil where appropriate, treating BPSD triggers, and carrying the family.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: the teacher who stopped marking homework.** A 71-year-old retired headmistress came with her son: two years of repeating questions, the household accounts drifting wrong, and, the precipitant, being found twice near the market, unable to explain how to reach home. She joked that at her age anyone forgets; her MMSE pattern showed early Alzheimer's-typical deficits; thyroid, B12 and sugar normal; MRI showed early hippocampal atrophy. Started on donepezil 5 mg, the family began a fixed daily routine, a walking group at the temple park, and ID card with address. At 18 months she was managing personal care and the accounts had moved to the son; the illness continued, but the household had reorganised around it without crisis.

*Teaching points: family-recognised functional decline beats clinic testing; basic bloods in every case; donepezil plus routine plus safety planning is the realistic package.*

**Case 2: "depression" that was Alzheimer's, and Alzheimer's that became delirium.** A 68-year-old widower was treated for six months for "depression", poor appetite, withdrawal, sitting all day. On review his testing revealed he had stopped *trying*, but errors appeared when attention was coaxed; collateral history revealed two years of getting lost while driving. Treated with an SSRI plus structured activity, mood brightened but memory continued to slide; the diagnosis of Alzheimer's was made, mild stage. Six months later his son called: "he has gone mad overnight, he sees snakes and does not know me." A urine test and sodium check revealed infection and mild hyponatraemia, delirium on the dementia. Both treated, he returned to his dementia baseline in five days.

*Teaching points: pseudodementia may be the first face of a real dementia, re-test after treating mood; any sudden change in a dementia patient is delirium until proven otherwise; and the two classic reversible triggers in India are urinary infection and sodium disturbance.*

## 13. FAQ

**"Is this just old age?"**
No. Age is the biggest risk factor, but the disease is not a normal part of ageing, plenty of 90-year-olds think clearly. Dementia is a specific illness of brain cells, and it deserves diagnosis like any illness.

**"Am I going to get it because my father has it?"**
For the common late-onset form, family history only roughly doubles the odds, and much of a person's risk can be reduced by exercise, blood pressure and sugar control, not smoking, treating hearing loss and staying socially connected. Only the rare early-onset familial form (under 1% of cases) is inherited in a clear-cut pattern, and genetic counselling can be offered there.

**"There is a memory medicine, will it cure him?"**
No, and it is kinder to say so clearly. It can hold function steadier for months and help some people manage daily activities longer. The disease continues underneath. The routine, walks, and your care do at least as much.

**"He accuses me of stealing his money. Does he hate me?"**
This is the illness, not your relationship. Damage in the memory and monitoring areas fills the gaps with fear, and the person nearest at hand gets blamed. A calm "let's look together", locked cupboards and shared ledgers reduce these episodes.

**"Should we hide the diagnosis from her?"**
As far as possible, no. In the mild stage, she can still plan: money, property, wishes for later care. Concealing the diagnosis takes away her right to arrange her own life while she still can.

**"He wants to wander in the night and we cannot sleep."**
First check the reversible drivers (pain, full bladder, constipation, infection, medicines), then make days active and bright and nights boring and dark; keep a night lamp and a floor mattress to prevent falls; an ID card or tag with a phone number is essential.

**"Is there any test that proves it is Alzheimer's?"**
Clinically: the pattern of decline plus exclusion of mimics. CSF and PET tests and new blood markers exist, but in Indian practice they are used mainly for young or unusual cases, because the clinical route plus basic tests is reliable enough for management.

**"Till what stage should we give the medicines?"**
While they still help, measured by stability in daily function, and a reasonable time beyond. When the illness is very advanced and swallowing is failing, stopping them is not giving up; it is honest care.

**"Is it better to tube-feed him when he stops eating?"**
Hand-feeding slowly, with positioning and smaller boluses, is often as safe and more dignified; feeding tubes in advanced dementia do not clearly prevent aspiration or prolong comfort. Discuss this before the crisis, not during it.

**"We are a small family and both of us work. What can we do?"**
Build the roster now: neighbours, day-care where available (ARDSI chapters in some cities), paid attendant for the heaviest hours, and a doctor visit schedule. Accept help; carer breakdown is the commonest reason families finally hospitalise a stable patient.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **Mnemonics to keep:** reversible dementia screen *DEMENTIAS* (Drugs, Emotional [depression], Metabolic [thyroid, B12], Eyes/ears/Nutritional, Tumour/trauma, Infection [syphilis, HIV], A [anaemia, HIV], build your own reliable version); BPSD triggers as *PAIN-FAECES-Urine-Home-Sleep* habits.
- **Highest-yield distinctions:** cortical (amnestic, aphasic, apraxic, Alzheimer's, FTD) vs subcortical (slow, apathetic, depressed-front, vascular, parkinsonian) dementia; pseudodementia vs early dementia (onset speed, "don't know" vs "near-miss" answers, effort vs capability); MCI vs dementia (independence is the hinge).
- **Classic traps:** (1) diagnosing dementia during a delirium; (2) missing depression in the elderly because "she has dementia anyway"; (3) forgetting B12 in a vegetarian elderly patient; (4) prescribing anticholinergic bladder drugs to someone on donepezil, directly opposing chemicals; (5) normal MRI does NOT rule out Alzheimer's.
- **Viva favourites:** hippocampal atrophy on MRI; nucleus basalis of Meynert and the cholinergic hypothesis; APOE ε4; the double-every-5-years prevalence rule; 10/66 study findings in India; caregiver burden as the "second patient"; why antipsychotics carry a mortality warning in dementia.
- **Indian exam corner:** Dementia India Report figures and the treatment gap; NMHS 2015–16 geriatric findings; Mental Healthcare Act 2017 advance directives; NPCHCE; the vegetarian B12 angle; and the ethics of restraint and tube feeding in advanced dementia.
- **Rehearse one line:** "Dementia is a clinical diagnosis: collateral history + objective impairment + loss of function + exclusion of the reversible mimics."

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. The neurotransmitter deficit most closely linked to Alzheimer's symptoms is:**
A. Dopamine
B. Serotonin
C. Acetylcholine
D. GABA

**Answer: C.** Cortical acetylcholine is lost early (nucleus basalis of Meynert); this is exactly what cholinesterase inhibitors act on.

**2. A patient's family reports 2 years of gradual memory decline and getting lost; bloods and MRI are normal. The most likely diagnosis is:**
A. Delirium
B. Depressive pseudodementia
C. Mild cognitive impairment progressing to Alzheimer's dementia
D. Normal ageing

**Answer: C.** Two years of progressive functional decline with normal mimic-screening is the classic presentation; "normal ageing" never takes away independence.

**3. Donepezil works by:**
A. Blocking NMDA receptors
B. Inhibiting the enzyme that breaks down acetylcholine
C. Dissolving amyloid plaques
D. Replacing lost dopamine

**Answer: B.** Cholinesterase inhibition preserves remaining acetylcholine; memantine is the NMDA antagonist.

**4. The strongest single modifiable protective factor for late-life dementia from the life-course view is:**
A. Ginkgo supplements
B. Regular physical activity and vascular risk control
C. Daily vitamin megadoses
D. Avoiding all medicines

**Answer: B.** Exercise and vascular risk control carry consistent evidence; tonics and megadose vitamins do not.

**5. A dementia patient becomes acutely confused at night, worse over hours, with fluctuating attention. First diagnostic thought:**
A. The dementia has advanced
B. Delirium: search for the cause
C. Schizophrenia onset
D. Start an antipsychotic immediately

**Answer: B.** Sudden-onset fluctuation is delirium until proven otherwise; treat the urinary, infection, metabolic or drug trigger.

**6. In an elderly vegetarian with slow cognitive decline, one mandatory test is:**
A. Serum vitamin B12
B. Serum copper
C. Genetic testing for APOE
D. Lumbar puncture

**Answer: A.** B12 deficiency is common, cheap to detect, and fully reversible if caught, a mandatory exclusion in Indian vegetarian diets.
