# Alcohol use disorders

## 1. In one line + overview

**Tagline:** Alcohol use disorder is a **treatable brain disease of "more"** (more than intended, more often, with more damage) whose emergencies (withdrawal seizures, delirium tremens, Wernicke's) kill people, whose quiet damages (liver, marriage, driving) break families, and whose treatment works best when **medicines that reduce relapse are combined with a plan that rebuilds the days, the cues and the pain underneath.**

**Overview, in plain words:** In India roughly **one in three adult men drinks**, and about a third of current drinkers drink at harmful levels, which makes alcohol the largest single cause of preventable psychiatric and medical disability in Indian men. The disorder sits on a spectrum: **hazardous use** (risk without dependence yet), **harmful use** (damage present, control intact), and **dependence** (the brain recalibrated: tolerance, withdrawal, craving, loss of control). The illness announces itself in disguises ("sleep problem", "liver complaint", "family dispute", "gastritis") because nobody walks in saying "I drink too much". Four clinical emergencies define the sharp end: **withdrawal syndrome, withdrawal seizures, delirium tremens and Wernicke encephalopathy**; the chronic end is liver disease, neuropathy, cognitive decline and the quiet erosion of everything the person valued. Treatment today is genuinely effective at every stage: ten-minute brief interventions for hazardous drinking, safe detoxification protocols for dependence, **relapse-prevention medicines (naltrexone, acamprosate, disulfiram)** that roughly double abstinence odds, and family-anchored psychosocial recovery; the Indian family being both the risk terrain and the treatment infrastructure. The role of the clinician is not to moralise the drinking but to treat the illness with the same seriousness as diabetes, and to say plainly, early, that **this condition is one where you can help the person get their life back.**

## 2. Learning objectives

1. Classify the spectrum: hazardous, harmful, dependent use, with one Indian example each.
2. Take a shame-free, quantified alcohol history in concrete units (per week, in quarters/pints) and score it against screening tools (AUDIT-style, named).
3. Run the detoxification decision: who detoxes at home, who needs a centre, who needs inpatient care.
4. Manage alcohol withdrawal with a symptom-triggered benzodiazepine protocol, thiamine-first discipline, and the red flags for delirium tremens.
5. Prescribe the three relapse-prevention medicines (naltrexone, acamprosate, disulfiram) with mechanisms, contraindications, India costs and monitoring.
6. Recognise the long-range complications: liver, pancreas, neuropathy, myopathy, cardiomyopathy, Korsakoff amnesia, alcohol-related dementia, and the indirect epidemics (violence, road deaths, tuberculosis delays).
7. Build the psychosocial and family plan: contract, cue-map, relapse-care plan, groups, and treating the co-travelling depression/insomnia/trauma.
8. Deliver a brief intervention in ten minutes, the highest-yield skill in Indian medicine for this condition.

## 4. Epidemiology

### Global
- WHO's recent figures: about **400 million people** live with alcohol use disorders worldwide; alcohol contributes to over **2.6 million deaths a year**; through injuries, liver disease, cancers, cardiovascular causes and infections.
- Lifetime risk is male-predominant globally; the global burden has been shifting toward lower- and middle-income countries where consumption is rising while policy lags.

### India
- National survey lineage: about **a third of adult men** are current drinkers; roughly **a quarter to a third of current drinkers** are hazardous or dependent; female drinking is far lower in surveys but rising in metros and probably under-reported.
- The "pattern" problem: Indian drinking is often **low-frequency/high-intensity** (irregular binges, not daily wine) so unit-counting must be calibrated to quarters, pints and "party" days.
- Consequence profile: alcohol drives a huge share of Indian male deaths through road injuries, violence, liver disease and tuberculosis treatment disruption, the patient rarely connects these.
- Infrastructure: government and NGO de-addiction centres, district hospitals with psychiatry, NASHA Mukti helpline (14446) and tele-MANAS linkage; the genuine bottleneck is trained human power.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

- **Genetic/biological:** heritability roughly 50–60%; the ALDH2 flushing variant (present in a large fraction of Indians) makes drinking aversive, partially protective, but not an immunity; male sex; early-onset use; conduct/ADHD history.
- **Psychological:** relief-drinking (anxiety, insomnia, shame, grief, trauma, dosely linked to ACEs); trait impulsivity; positive expectancies ("confidence", "sleep"); depressive episodes driving self-treatment.
- **Social/environmental:** availability and price (quarter-bag economies); peer norms and male-bonding drinking culture; occupations with cash, travel and separation (transport, hotel, army, migrant labour); family drinking models; unemployment and debt spirals; the cycle of drink-domestic-violion-drink-more.
- **Course:** mean age of dependence onset in Indian series is the mid-30s, with 10–15 years from regular use to presenting complication; the liver and the family usually present before the psychiatry.

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: the braking system that remodels.** Alcohol's acute action is to **press the brain's brake (GABA)** and **ease the accelerator's friction (glutamate)**, calm and disinhibition. With chronic flood, the brain remodels: fewer brake receptors, more accelerator capacity, so sober feels strung-out (tolerance), and stopping flips the balance: brakes gone, accelerator free, **the withdrawal syndrome** of tremor, sweating, anxiety and (dangerously) seizures and delirium.

**Story 2: the two-stage thermostatic crash.** In early abstinence there is a first storm (days), then a long grey phase (weeks–months), post-acute withdrawal (PAWS): flat mood, irritability, poor sleep, anhedonia, the reward thermostat still re-setting. Families call it "he's stopped but he's not back"; unprepared patients call it "life without drink is joyless", and relapse is born. Naming this phase and dating its end (typically 3–6 months) is one of counselling's strongest levers.

**Story 3: the nutrient famine and the liver's decay.** Alcohol gives calories without nutrition and blocks thiamine use: the malnourished drinker drifts toward the Wernicke–Korsakoff edge (see the amnesia note). Meanwhile the liver converts chronic alcohol into fat, inflammation and fibrosis; the LFT trajectory is the somatic biography of the drinking history; GGT and CDT are the honest informants of recent consumption.

## 7. Symptoms

**Dependence cluster:** morning drinking; drinking alone; maintaining a stock; tremor relieved by a drink; failed cut-downs; narrowing of life around drinking; **"the first drink problem", one drink reliably triggers the search for the next several.**

**Withdrawal ladder (time from last drink):**
- **6–12 h:** tremor, sweating, nausea, anxiety, insomnia (the "shakes").
- **12–24 h:** visual hallucinations sometimes (alcoholic hallucinosis, clear sensorium, quiet voices/vision).
- **6–48 h:** withdrawal seizures, brief generalised, no aura, about a quarter risk if untreated.
- **48–96 h:** **delirium tremens**, confusion, vivid hallucinations, fever, wild autonomic arousal (tachycardia, hypertension, sweating), the killing stage; mortality 1–5% with treatment, far higher without.

**Wernicke encephalopathy** (the emergency that demands treatment independent of the liver's drama: confusion, eye-movement paralysis or nystagmus, ataxia) any one sign in a malnourished drinker = parenteral thiamine **before any glucose**.

**Chronic harm inventory (by system):** liver (fatty → hepatitis → cirrhosis), pancreatitis; gastritis and cancers (mouth, throat, food pipe); peripheral neuropathy, myopathy; dilated cardiomyopathy; hypertension; **dementia spectrum** (alcohol-related dementia and the Korsakoff hole; see those notes); thiamine-deficiency states; immune decline (TB recovery sabotaged); sexual dysfunction and testosterone fall; injury and violence burden.

## 8. Diagnosis

### The logic of DSM-5 / ICD-11 (paraphrased)
The **single, severity-graded disorder** (see the overview note for the four-cluster logic) applied to alcohol: impaired control (more/longer/failed rules/craving), social impairment, risky use (including drinking while driving or with liver disease), and pharmacology (tolerance, withdrawal). ICD-11 dependence centres on the drive-control-harm-tolerance-withdrawal core; "harmful use" persists as the damage-without-dependence category. Two clinical words to retire: "alcoholic" as an identity label; "willpower failure" as an explanation.

### Assessment package
- **AUDIT** (ten questions: named, not reproduced) as the spectrum screener; AUDIT-C for busy OPDs; blood markers (GGT, CDT, LFTs) as corroboration, not as the diagnosis.
- **Quantified history in Indian units:** quarters, pints, "₹ per day"; days drunk per week; drinking companions and settings; morning drinking; last drink (for withdrawal timing); longest dry period and its ending (the relapse map).
- **The relief question:** "What does it do for you, sleep, tension, anger?": writes the psychosocial prescription before you leave the room.
- **Examination:** tremor, sweats, spider naevi, liver edge, jaundice, palmar erythema, peripheral neuropathy signs, gait (cerebellar), memory screen (the amnesia note), eye movements (Wernicke).
- **Investigations:** FBC, LFT, GGT/CDT, sugar, renal, electrolytes (magnesium, potassium, sodium, withdrawal destabilises all three), and imaging as clinically indicated (pancreatitis, head injury after falls, subdural in the elderly drinker, see the TBI note).

### Differential notes
| Look-alike | Key to it being the other thing |
|------------|----------------|
| Primary depression with heavy drinking | Which came first, and does the mood clear by week 3–4 of abstinence? |
| Bipolar with alcohol misuse (very common pairing) | Episodic pattern predating drinking; screen mania symptoms before SSRI (see the bipolar note) |
| Panic disorder | Morning "anxiety" that disappears with the first drink is withdrawal, not panic |
| Psychotic disorder | Quiet hallucinosis with intact clear sensorium in withdrawal vs delusional system of schizophrenia |
| Cirrhosis-first presentations | Ask the three quantified-history questions of every "liver patient" |

## 9. Management

### (a) Match response to stage
- **Hazardous/harmful without dependence:** brief intervention (FRAMES-style, ten minutes, documented), the single highest-yield action in Indian medicine for this condition; drinking-diary + feedback of GGT and units + menu of options.
- **Dependence:** the five-floor plan below.

### (b) Safe withdrawal: the detoxification decision
- **Outpatient/home detoxification** is appropriate for mild-to-moderate predicted withdrawal: brief history of drinking, no past seizures/DTs, no severe liver disease or major medical comorbidity, reliable family supervision, and daily contact.
- **Inpatient/centre detoxification** for: past withdrawal seizures or DTs, concurrent illness, pregnancy, psychiatric instability, homelessness, polysubstance (especially benzodiazepines), and failed home attempts.
- **Core protocol logic:** a benzodiazepine with a validated withdrawal scale to trigger doses symptomatically (chlordiazepoxide or diazepam where liver function allows; lorazepam in significant liver impairment), thiamine first and throughout (parenteral initially in malnourished), magnesium/potassium correction, hydration, glucose only after thiamine, and quiet supervision. Red flags for escalation: seizure, fever, hallucinations, confusion, autonomic storm, transfer.
- **Benzodiazepine's exit:** taper off over about a week (symptom-triggered regimens usually finish sooner); the danger is substituting one dependence for another, deliberate, short, documented.

*India note: the whole detoxification medicine kit (chlordiazepoxide, thiamine ampoules, multivitamins) costs under a few hundred rupees; the scarce commodities are supervision days and family discipline, which is why the family-contract visit is the real detoxification infrastructure.*

### (c) Relapse-prevention pharmacotherapy: the under-used treasure
- **Naltrexone (50 mg/day):** opioid-receptor blockade that mutes the reward of drinking and reduces heavy-drinking days; best evidence as the general first choice; contraindications: opioid use (including codeine/tramadol misuse; check!) and hepatitis/hepatic failure; expensive historically but now affordable as generics (approx ₹800–1,500/month, 2026, varies).
- **Acamprosate (666 mg three times daily):** restores inhibitory balance; best evidence in abstinent-motivated patients; renal dosing; cost and thrice-daily load in India; gentle side-effect profile.
- **Disulfiram (250–500 mg/day):** blocks aldehyde breakdown so that drinking produces flushing, vomiting, palpitations, an aversive commitment device; only works as supervised medicine: the **family-held** model (a named relative administers daily, court-appointment-style) is its honest Indian use; teach the reaction protocol; avoid in severe heart disease, psychosis, pregnancy.
- Newer/exam names: **nalmefene** (as-needed heavy-drinking reducer, used in Europe), **topiramate** (off-label evidence), and baclofen for some, name-awareness for vivas.
- **No "safe" alcohol during recovery**: the plan is built around zero, lapses planned-for-not-normalised.

### (d) Psychosocial recovery
- Motivational interviewing opens the door; relapse-prevention CBT maps personal cues (people, places, paydays, moods) and skill-builds alternatives; group fellowships (AA-adjacent and faith-based recoveries: temple/church/ashram programmes work for those who accept their idiom) supply the crucial identity shift from "drinker among abstainers" to "recovering person among peers".
- Structure engineering: work, exercise, evening plans; idle afternoons are the relapse capital of India (see the overview note).
- **Family contract, written:** who holds medicines (relative + one pharmacy), money rules (no cash days; UPI limits), relapse-response plan (calm, fast, call the doctor first, not the neighbourhood), and one protected relationship activity per week that is not about the drinking.
- Treat the riders: depression after week 3–4 if it persists, insomnia (sleep hygiene; melatonin; mirtazapine where depression co-travels), PTSD where trauma antedated, and always tobacco (co-dependence; see the nicotine note).

### (e) The complications track
Parallel medical follow-up for liver, pancreas, neuropathy, TB adherence; vaccination (hepatitis B); nutritional rehabilitation; and cognitive re-assessment at 3–6 months (the alcohol-related dementia and amnesia notes carry the cognitive programmes).

## 10. Indian practice

- **Legal/policy frame:** state-level prohibition patchwork, excise pricing, NDPS not applicable to alcohol (state acts instead), and the national programme resources (de-addiction centre scheme, NASHA helpline 14446, tele-MANAS linkage).
- **Presenting disguises to expect:** gastritis, insomnia, "nerves", liver check-ups, domestic-violence referrals, work-performance letters; screen behind all of them with the two shame-free questions.
- **The family-anchored model:** in India, a supervised disulfiram or a family-held naltrexone bottle achieves what Western evening-clinic arrangements cannot; build the contract in the first week, not the third relapse.
- **Pharmacy realities:** codeine and tramadol co-misuse is common and interacts with naltrexone; ask about it before prescribing; the same pharmacy rules the family has set.
- **Workplace brief interventions:** employer letters converting termination to medical leave; the transport industry's mandatory rest-day structure as a treatment ally.
- **Primary care role:** ten-minute brief intervention, safe home detoxification, family contract, naltrexone prescribing and quarterly follow-up, all within the district doctor's scope; centre referral only for risky detox and refractory relapse.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: the quarter-a-day, forty-year-old with "gastritis".** A 42-year-old mill worker presented for gastritis; screening found 21 quarters per week, morning tremor for two years, a seizure "once at a wedding", and a GGT six-fold raised. Detoxification: home-based (family supervision; no DT history in fact, but the seizure history made it borderline), day-1 chlordiazepoxide with symptom-triggered dosing, thiamine before any drip, daily phone contact for five days; relapse-prevention: naltrexone (tramadol check negative), family contract (wife holds medicines; one protected evening walk per week), cue-map (the dhaba on pay-day, the brother-in-law who drinks), and a written relapse plan. Two lapses in year one (payday; Diwali), both survived without dropout; GGT normalised; the "gastritis" never returned.

*Teaching points: the disguise; the seizure history as the detox decision; thiamine-first; naltrexone with the tramadol check; and the contract written early enough to hold.*

**Case 2: the DT that was almost called "psychosis".** A 55-year-old man, three days into an involuntary abstinence (family locked the house for a wedding), was brought "raving, seeing snakes, not recognising his sons". Casualty was about to treat "acute psychosis". The timeline (last drink 60+ hours before, heavy daily use for 15 years, prior shaking episodes) plus vitals (fever, pulse 128, sweating) gave delirium tremens; chlordiazepoxide loading, thiamine, magnesium and careful nursing resolved it over three days. Wernicke screening after stabilisation caught horizontal nystagmus, and memory testing showed a patchy hole (the amnesia note's programme followed).

*Teaching points: the 48–96 h DT window; autonomic storm separates it from functional psychosis; family-enforced abstinence is a classic trigger; the glucose-before-thiamine trap lives in exactly this scenario.*

## 13. FAQ

**"He drinks only on weekends. Is that a problem?"**
Count the units, not the calendar: binge patterns (a full quarter in an evening) carry the injury risk of daily drinking. If he cannot stop mid-binge, or the weekend needs the drink to feel normal, it is already on the spectrum.

**"Can he just cut down instead of stopping?"**
For hazardous drinking, cut-down with structure genuinely works. For dependence, the brain's "first drink problem" usually makes moderation a revolving door; the evidence-backed road is abstinence with relapse-prevention medicines.

**"Is disulfiram a cure?"**
No, it is a commitment device: it turns a slip into an unpleasant lesson, which buys time for the real treatment (rebuilding days and treating the pain underneath). It works only when the family supervises it daily.

**"He has stopped drinking but is irritable and flat. Why?"**
That is the long reset of the reward system, post-acute withdrawal, typically lasting weeks to months, with a dated end. Naming it and planning around it prevents the "life is joyless without it" relapse.

**"Do the memory tablets help his forgetfulness?"**
First identify which alcohol damage it is: thiamine-deficiency amnesia (the hole), alcohol-related dementia (the thinning) or early recovery fogging; each has its own note and its own management; tonics do not treat any of them.

**"Should we hide it from the marriage/alliance talk?"**
Concealment of a current dependence risks both the health and the marriage. The honest framing: "a treatable medical condition, under treatment, with family support", treated illness discloses better than discovered illness.

**"What is our job at home?"**
Five written items: hold the medicines, control the cash rules, keep one protected activity, respond to lapses calmly and fast, and look after your own health; the family is the treatment infrastructure, and the doctor is its engineer.

**"Will the liver recover?"**
Fat and inflammation largely reverse with months of abstinence; established cirrhosis does not reverse but stabilises with sobriety. The liver's biography is written by the drinking years; the next chapters are yours to choose.

**"Is beer safer than whisky?"**
The dose of ethanol is the dose of harm; the vessel matters little. "Light" drinks make heavy totals easy.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. The most dangerous window for delirium tremens after the last drink is:**
A. 0–12 hours
B. 48–96 hours
C. 7–10 days
D. Immediately

**Answer: B.** Tremor at 6–12 h, hallucinosis at 12–24 h, seizures 6–48 h, the DT storm at 48–96 h.

**2. The correct first medication for a malnourished dependent drinker requiring IV fluids is:**
A. Glucose drip immediately
B. Parenteral thiamine before any glucose-containing fluid
C. Haloperidol first
D. Naltrexone

**Answer: B.** The B1-before-D5 rule; glucose metabolism consumes thiamine and can tip the patient into Wernicke's.

**3. Naltrexone's critical prescribing check in India is:**
A. Liver function and concurrent codeine/tramadol/opioid use
B. Skin colour
C. Blood group
D. Family history of diabetes

**Answer: A.** Opioid-receptor blockade precipitates withdrawal in opioid users (codeine/tramadol included; common Indian co-misuse) and hepatitis/hepatic failure are contraindications.

**4. "He has stopped for 3 weeks but says life feels grey and joyless." The best explanation and action:**
A. Major depression: start an SSRI today
B. Post-acute withdrawal phase: name it, date its typical end, plan around it
C. He needs a small drink to rebalance
D. Personality change

**Answer: B.** The reward thermostat resets over weeks-months; early SSRI is reserved for persistent syndromal depression beyond ~4–6 weeks or danger.

**5. Disulfiram's honest Indian model of use is:**
A. Self-administered as needed
B. Daily, family-supervised: a commitment device within a written contract
C. Monthly injection for craving
D. First-line for everyone

**Answer: B.** Its aversive mechanism only works when administration is supervised; the family-held model is its realistic deployment.

**6. The single highest-yield screening action in Indian general practice is:**
A. Annual liver ultrasound for everyone
B. Two shame-free questions (tolerance; used-more-than-meant) behind presenting complaints
C. Random blood alcohol
D. Waiting for the patient to disclose

**Answer: B.** Presentations disguise (gastritis, insomnia, "nerves"); brief shame-free screening catches the spectrum years earlier than self-disclosure.
