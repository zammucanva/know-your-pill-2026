# Child Adversity Contexts — Adoption & Foster Care, Parental Illness, and Childhood Bereavement

## 1. In one line + overview

**Three adversities that are not disorders but shape everything: children placed in new families, children living with ill parents, and children who lose them; with risk that is real, resilience that is common, and interventions that are mostly about communication and parenting.**

This note combines three Oxford chapters on childhood adversity contexts. The adoption/foster-care chapter (Thoburn) shows family placement as both solution and risk-generator, organised on six dimensions (age at placement, prior disturbance, attachments, placement history, type of adoption, cultural match), and shows adopted children *outperforming* the adverse environments they left while carrying slightly elevated emotional and behavioural risk, with identity as the lifelong theme. The parental-illness chapter (Ramchandani, Stein & Murray) demonstrates that most parental disorders (depression, schizophrenia, eating disorders, substance misuse, anxiety, cancer, HIV) raise child risk through four mechanisms (impaired parenting, family discord, direct symptom involvement, and bidirectional child-to-parent effects), with resilience as the standing counterweight. The childhood-bereavement chapter (Black & Trickey) delivers the developmental logic: children under 7 cannot fully grasp death's permanence, one in five bereaved children needs specialist help, traumatic deaths jam the mourning machinery, and a brief preventive family intervention works.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Use the six dimensions of family placement to structure risk assessment for any adopted or fostered child.
- Summarise outcomes for infant-placed vs later-placed children (including the ~5% adoption breakdown figure and the Swedish inter-country cohort findings).
- Name the four mechanisms by which parental illness transmits risk to children, with an example of each.
- Describe disorder-specific risks: parental depression (three-fold offspring depression), schizophrenia, eating disorders (mealtime conflict and infant weight), substance misuse, anxiety (behavioural inhibition), cancer (communication as the pivotal variable), HIV.
- Explain childhood grief developmentally: the under-7 understanding threshold, viewing the body, funerals, adolescent guilt.
- Manage traumatic bereavement (treat PTSD before mourning can proceed).
- Deliver the brief preventive family intervention after parental death, and the child-focused techniques (memory boxes, letters, rituals).
- Apply the Indian kinship-care default, disclosure norms and ritual architecture to each context.

## 4. Epidemiology

**Family placement.** "Success" rates vary from below 50% to 95% depending on measures, a warning about the evidence base. About **5% of infant-placed adoptees leave before 18** ("adoption breakdown"); ~80% of adopters and adoptees report broad satisfaction. In a Swedish cohort of ~6,000 inter-country-adopted adults, more similarities than differences with peers, but elevated psychiatric problems, substance abuse and suicide. For later placements: 8 years on, 19% of one English sample of children placed over age 5 had left their placements, and only just over half of continuing placements were "continuing/happy". **Parental illness.** ~13% of women have postnatal depression; parental depression's offspring risks are the best-quantified; up to 40% of some sub-Saharan antenatal clinic attenders are HIV-positive. **Bereavement.** In industrialised countries 1.5–4% of children lose a parent in childhood; in some developing countries, up to **21%**, with HIV responsible for up to three-quarters of such deaths.

**India.** The Indian numbers run through different channels: massive kinship absorption of orphaned children (the joint family as default child-welfare system), CARA-regulated adoption with its own waiting lists, high parental illness burden (TB, cancer diagnosed late, HIV in concentrated epidemics), and road-traffic and disaster deaths creating the sudden-death bereavements the chapter flags as highest-risk. Childhood bereavement in India is thus commoner than Western epidemiology suggests, and mostly carried by extended families with little professional input.

## 5. Causes & risk factors: the risk architecture

**For placed children:** pre-placement maltreatment and neglect; institutionalisation; anxious, ambivalent or avoidant attachments then lost; sibling separation; multiple carer changes; age beyond 6 months at placement (the risk gradient); "drift": placement delayed by contested legal proceedings; transracial/inter-country placement adding loss of cultural, ethnic and racial ties (Brodzinsky: for later-placed children loss is overt and sometimes traumatic; for infant-placed children it is covert, emerging as the child comprehends it); temperament (the "born worrier" carries "what was not worth keeping about me"; the resilient child shrugs off even mediocre substitute parenting).

**For children of ill parents:** genetics plus environment plus gene-environment interaction; impaired parenting (the depressed mother less vocal, positive, spontaneous; more negative, intrusive, less communicative); marital discord (a key mediator, possibly more proximal than the depression itself); socio-economic disadvantage clustering; direct symptom involvement (the child incorporated into a delusion or obsession, rarer); and **child-to-parent effects** (infant irritability and poor motor control at 10 days predict later maternal depression; the arrow runs both ways).

**For bereaved children:** developmental level (concept of death incomplete before 7); learning disability (higher risk: cognitive difficulty with death's components plus dependency); traumatic circumstances (witnessing horrific death; suicide or homicide deaths with media interest, disfigured or delayed bodies, withheld mementoes); compromised surviving parenting; pre-death adversity (hard to separate the loss from what preceded it); social support collapse when support systems find the death unbearable.

## 6. What happens in the mind: three short stories

**The identity story (adoption).** The adopted child carries two extra hurdles on the road to maturity: separation-and-loss, and making sense of an adoptive identity. Around age 11 the risk of maladjustment peaks and then declines into later adolescence; in adulthood, even among the broadly satisfied, some carry a continuous or episodic unease around identity and the question of why their birth parents "gave them up", and when serious adult problems emerge, adoptive identity often underlies the presenting symptoms. Openness has become the modern answer: total-severance closed adoption was a 20th-century experiment (UK secrecy from 1958; Alabama sealing records until 1991) now largely dismantled by access-to-information legislation and open adoption contact.

**The communication story (parental illness).** What damages children of ill parents is rarely the diagnosis itself; it is what the illness does to the interaction: withdrawal, unavailability, harshness, conflict, or silence. The parental-cancer finding is the cleanest proof: children's anxiety tracks **whether they are told about the illness and how well**; informed children are less anxious than uninformed ones. Communication is not an optional kindness; it is the mechanism of protection.

**The mourning-machinery story (bereavement).** To mourn, the child must summon the image of the dead person. If that image is a terrifying picture from a witnessed death (or re-invokes the helplessness and terror of the moment) the child avoids the image, and the grief cannot proceed. This is why traumatic bereavement is treated in sequence: post-traumatic symptoms first, mourning after. For ordinary deaths, the machinery runs on communication, reminiscing, expression of feeling and making sense, the four therapeutic elements.

## 7. Symptoms: the clinical pictures

**Placed children: short-term.** English infants with good early care placed quickly settle without obvious stress; Romanian institutionalised infants placed in early months also settle well; children placed after 6 months show stress reactions to attachment loss or adverse-reaction effects of prior maltreatment. **Middle years:** higher risk of social, emotional and behavioural difficulties than matched non-adopted peers (especially boys), inability to settle, restlessness, lying or fantasising, peer and teacher difficulties, low self-esteem and insecurity, peaking around 11 and declining into later adolescence. **Later-placed children** bring the pre-placement problems plus institutionalisation effects (for inter-country adoptees), attachment disturbances, and the consequences of multiple moves. **Adults adopted as infants:** healthier, higher IQ, less criminality, fewer psychiatric symptoms than peers from the environments they were born into; broadly similar to those raised by comparable birth parents; with the Swedish-cohort caveat of elevated psychiatric disorder, substance misuse and suicide. **Relinquishing parents:** moderate distress to long-term grief that may affect children born later; some relinquishing parents are themselves children.

**Children of ill parents: by disorder.** *Depression (either parent: paternal depression now has independent evidence)*: three-fold major depression in offspring plus anxiety disorders, substance dependence, social and physical-health impairment extending into adulthood; postnatal depression: infant emotional/behavioural risk, possible cognitive effects (inconsistent), with developing-country evidence of infant physical-health risks (poor growth, diarrhoeal illness); adolescence brings mood and anxiety disorders. *Schizophrenia*: greatly increased offspring schizophrenia risk; obstetric complications; childhood attentional problems that persist and mark later risk; social difficulties with peers and teachers; adolescent social-relationship and thought-disorder problems; adult schizotypal behaviour: much of the non-cognitive burden tracing to family disruption (hospital admissions, employment and relationship difficulties) rather than to "schizophrenogenic" communication. *Eating disorders*: infancy risk of failure to thrive (anorexia history); intrusive mealtimes with negative emotion and conflict, less infant autonomy, infant weight inversely related to mealtime conflict; middle childhood: self-evaluation by body shape and weight, dietary restriction. *Alcoholism/substance misuse*: FAS (5.9% of alcoholic women's births vs 0.01-0.03% of all births); impaired cognitive and social development; ADHD/attention/impulsivity (the most consistent finding); aggression, fewer friends, criminality risk; elevated risk of the same substance misuse in adolescence (with resilience the norm); parenting deficits of neglect and harsh discipline; divorce, marital conflict, assortative mating. *Anxiety*: considerable specificity of familial transmission (two-fold risk); temperamental behavioural inhibition and autonomic reactivity (increased startle in infancy); parental over-protection limiting skill development; social-phobia modelling; possible intrauterine HPA-axis effects of antenatal anxiety. *Cancer*: adolescent children show elevated emotional disturbance (younger children inconsistently); problem-focused coping adaptive, emotion-focused coping (venting, denial, apathy) predictive of anxiety and depression. *HIV*: school-age children of affected mothers (externalising and internalising problems, lower social skills, academic difficulties) with maternal depression as a plausible key mechanism.

**Bereaved children: by developmental stage.** *Young children*: anxiety or depressive reactions, often somatic (regression in achieved control, anorexia, insomnia), but cannot distinguish temporary from permanent loss; no full understanding of death before 7 (though 4-year-olds can understand with help). *Pre-pubertal schoolchildren*: can be helped to comprehend death's reality; viewing the body (where culturally normative and the body is not mutilated) may reduce misconceptions; attending the funeral appears (clinically, though not scientifically) to help grieving. *Adolescents*: the death may strike mid-separation, mid-conflict, guilt and anger; suicidal feelings more likely acted on within a depressive reaction; can sustain sad affect and grieve directly, but also react with behavioural and academic difficulties. *Children with learning difficulties*: higher risk (conceptual difficulty + dependency). *Traumatic bereavement*: PTSD risk from witnessed deaths; suicide/homicide deaths add traumatic content, media interest, and broken support systems. *Other losses*: grandparent deaths (devastating where caregiving), sibling death (high morbidity, mitigated by preparation and ritual participation; adolescents may deny finality), friend/pet/home losses (usually non-pathological with supported parents, but adolescent friends' suicide: elevated depression in controlled study, though not elevated attempted suicide).

## 8. Diagnosis

- **For placed children:** assess on the six dimensions (age, prior disturbance, attachments, placement history/duration, adoption type and cultural match), this framework tells you where the risk sits and what the differential should include (attachment disorders, consequences of maltreatment, ADHD, the institutionalisation overlap, PTSD, identity issues in adolescence).
- **For children of ill parents:** do not diagnose the child without mapping the family; the child's disorder may mirror the parent's but the range is broad (social, emotional, cognitive, physical). Screen specifically for the mechanisms: parenting capacity, marital discord, socio-economic strain, symptom involvement. In parental cancer: assess what the child has been told.
- **For bereaved children:** remember parents under-report child symptoms relative to children's own reports, **interview children individually**. Look for the diagnosable disorders (depression, anxiety, PTSD) that cross thresholds; remember DSM's ~2-month window before major depression is diagnosed in bereavement, and ICD-10's adjustment-disorder/Z-code framing; assess trauma exposure and the imagery-blocking mechanism; check the surviving parent's functioning (children's outcome rides on it).
- **The resilience audit:** in all three contexts, most children do *not* develop disorder; assessments should document protective factors (a well parent, high IQ, social support, communication, preparation) as deliberately as risk factors.

## 9. Management

**Placed children.** Preparation and matching (the "keep them out of care at all costs" attitude produces ill-planned placements that break down); facilitating good contact between birth parents, foster carers and the child; multi-agency support before, during and after placement; for time-limited placements: temporary, emergency, assessment, treatment and bridging functions, returning to the same foster carers where possible; treatment foster care (specially recruited, trained, supported carers) with multi-systemic programmes showing better outcomes than service-as-usual for troubled children. Reframe "overstaying" as success where the task-centred family becomes a secure base into adulthood.

**Children of ill parents.** Treat the parental illness *and* the parenting interface: family-perspective working; social support for the family; in parental cancer: assist parents to recognise and cope with children's distress, communicate about the illness (informed children are less anxious), support problem-focused coping; in HIV: identify and treat maternal depression as the caregiving mechanism; in substance misuse: enhance social supports, treat the family system; in anxiety: work with parental over-protection and modelling.

**Bereaved children.** The evidence-backed package: a **brief family intervention around 2 months after parental death reduces children's morbidity at 1 year** (Black & Urbanowicz); Schut & Stroebe's conclusion: children are a special case, *likely to benefit from primary intervention* (open to all bereaved children, not only complicated cases), unlike adults where targeting complicated grief works better. Support the widowed parent's grief and mourning with practical help (child care, financial advice), as important as counselling the child; support and guide other adults (teachers, religious leaders). Therapeutic elements: **promotion of family communication about the dead parent; promotion of mourning through reminiscing; appropriate expression of feelings; making sense of the death**. Child techniques: art and storytelling, letters to the deceased, memory boxes, rituals (candles, balloons), representing aspects of the person, opening-up games, role play, deliverable by carefully selected, trained, supervised volunteers. Give young children straight biological explanations of what death means; help them recognise and cope with sad affects in themselves and the surviving family. **Follow-up appointments** after time-limited interventions (problems can emerge later); **preparation for an expected death** lowers later anxiety. For traumatic bereavement: treat PTSD first; bereavement counselling before trauma treatment does not work. Children meeting disorder thresholds get disorder-specific treatment (depression, anxiety, PTSD chapters).

## 10. Indian practice

- **Kinship care is the Indian placement system.** The joint family absorbs orphaned and separated children, often well (continuity of attachment, culture, identity) but informally, without assessment: screen these children for the same risks (pre-loss adversity, caregiving quality, favouritism and exploitation within the host household, school continuity). CARA adoption is the formal route; the note's six-dimension framework applies to both.
- **Stigma shapes everything in parental illness.** HIV and (still) TB and leprosy carry family-level stigma that suppresses exactly the communication shown protective: the child knows the parent is "sick" but not what is happening, is excluded from hospital visits, and overhears whispered half-truths, the uninformed-child anxiety pattern. The clinical task is permission-giving for honest, age-appropriate disclosure.
- **Bereavement's ritual wealth.** Indian death rituals (viewing, cremation, the 13-day rites, yearly shraddha) are precisely the community participation the Western literature finds protective; children are usually included, mourning is communal, and the calendar of remembrance continues. Preserve and use it; the clinical additions are the individual child interview, memory work, and the traumatic-death exception where the witnessed image blocks mourning.
- **The suicide/homicide layer.** NCRB-context suicide deaths and accidental deaths arrive with the complicating features the chapter lists: stigma, police procedures, delayed or disfigured bodies, media. Expect the PTSD-first rule to apply especially often.
- **The surviving-parent package is deliverable anywhere.** Practical support to the widowed parent (MGNREGA/survivor pensions, school fee waivers), one clinic visit at ~2 months for the brief family intervention, teacher sensitisation; none of it requires infrastructure that Indian districts lack.
- **Costs (approx 2026).** CARA adoption costs are regulated and modest; the interventions in this note are conversation- and time-based; the scarce commodity is trained counselling capacity, which Tele-MANAS and NGO bereavement programmes partially supply.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: The talkative boy with the silent house.** A 12-year-old is referred for school refusal and headaches eight months after his father's death from a road accident (which he did not witness). His mother, controls his widowed mother (sad, exhausted, managing two jobs) has kept all mention of the father out of the house ("so no one becomes sad"). He sleeps poorly, keeps the father's mobile phone hidden in his school bag, and has begun telling classmates his father is "abroad". Assessment: individually interviewed (mother under-reported his symptoms), he speaks openly: he does not know how his father died, whether he suffered, or what happens now. Formulation: inhibited mourning inside a communication-closed home, not yet a disorder, but squarely in the one-in-five referral band. Management: the brief family intervention, a session with mother and son together; permission to open the father's box of belongings; the biological explanation of death; his questions answered; teacher informed (his class had been told nothing); memory box, letter to father, and lighting a lamp on the monthly death-day per family custom; follow-up at 3 and 6 months. Teaching points: parents under-report, interview the child; the closed-communication pattern is the treatable mechanism; "abroad" fantasies signal unprocessed reality, not lying; the ritual calendar became the therapy's ally.

**Case 2: The girl who watched.** A 9-year-old saw her mother die in a kitchen fire. Three months later she has nightmares, refuses the kitchen, startles at smoke, and "cannot see her mother's face" when she tries, she sees the fire instead. The extended family presses for the final religious rites to "complete" the mourning; she deteriorated after attending them. Formulation: traumatic bereavement with PTSD; the mourning machinery is blocked by the terrifying image; rituals alone cannot process what the trauma holds. Management: trauma-focused CBT for the PTSD first (gradual imaginal exposure to the memory), with grief work introduced once the intrusive images have lost their charge; the family counselled that this sequence, not ritual avoidance or ritual forcing, is the evidence-based path. Teaching points: the image-blocking mechanism in clinical form; treat trauma before mourning; family rites are protective in ordinary deaths but cannot substitute for trauma treatment in witnessed deaths.

## 13. FAQ

**"We are adopting, what should we worry about?"**: The six dimensions tell you: the child's age, what came before, the attachments made and lost, and the match. Infant placements carry the lowest risk; older placements bring the child's history with them. Most adopted children do well (better than they would have in the circumstances they came from) with a slightly raised risk of emotional and behavioural difficulties, and identity questions to accompany them for life.
**"Should we tell the children about my cancer?"**: Yes, in age-appropriate detail: children who are informed and can talk with their parents about the illness are measurably less anxious than children left guessing; imagination is worse than information.
**"He was only four, does he really understand that his grandfather died?"**: Not fully: children build the complete concept of death (irreversibility, universality) only by about 7, though 4-year-olds can understand much with help. Give simple biological explanations, let them see mourning adults, and expect the questions to repeat.
**"Should the children attend the funeral?"**: Generally yes (clinical experience supports it), and where your culture includes viewing the body and the child wants to, that too, but never with a mutilated body, and always with preparation and an available adult.
**"Her friend took her own life, is my daughter at risk of suicide now?"**: Adolescents bereaved by a friend's suicide show more depression than peers in controlled studies, but not more attempted suicide. Take the sadness seriously, keep communication open, and watch for the depression that warrants treatment.
**"Since my husband died, my son says his father is 'on a trip'. Is he lying?"**: He is managing an unfinished comprehension. Answer his questions with simple biological truth; the fantasy dissolves as understanding and grief proceed.
**"The relatives want the final rites done quickly, but my son is worse since the last one."**: If the death was witnessed or violent, rituals may be landing on untreated trauma; the sequence is trauma treatment first, mourning work after.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **Brodzinsky's contrast**: overt/acute/traumatic loss in later-placed children vs covert/emergent loss in infant-placed children.
- **The four mechanisms** of parental-illness transmission, the generic viva answer.
- **Numbers to quote**: 3-fold offspring depression (parental depression); 13% postnatal depression; FAS 5.9% of alcoholic mothers' births; 1 in 5 bereaved children needing specialist referral; 1.5–4% industrialised / up to 21% developing-country parental orphanhood.
- **The bereavement-management ladder**: support the surviving parent → promote communication/reminiscing/expression/sense-making → child techniques → follow-up; PTSD first for traumatic deaths.
- **Schut & Stroebe's targeting rule**: adults, target complicated grief; children: primary (universal) intervention.
- **DSM/ICD framework**: bereavement V62.82 vs 296.2 (~2-month window); ICD-10 adjustment disorder F43.2 / Z63.4 (6 months).

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**Q1.** For children adopted as infants compared with children raised by birth parents in similar circumstances, the research consensus is:
- (a) Markedly worse outcomes across all domains
- (b) Broadly similar, with a slightly elevated risk of social, emotional and behavioural problems (and lifelong identity themes)
- (c) Identical outcomes in every domain
- (d) Uniformly superior outcomes

**Answer: (b).** Against the adverse environments they left, adoptees do better (health, IQ, criminality, symptoms); against matched birth-parent homes, a modest excess of emotional/behavioural difficulty, peaking around age 11, with adoptive identity as the persistent undertone.

**Q2.** In parental cancer, the variable most clearly linked to children's anxiety levels is:
- (a) The cancer's histological type
- (b) Whether children are informed about the illness and the quality of communication with parents
- (c) The parent's hospital's reputation
- (d) The child's blood group

**Answer: (b).** Informed children show lower anxiety than uninformed children; communication is the protective mechanism, not a procedural detail.

**Q3.** A 6-year-old whose mother died suddenly keeps asking when she is coming back. The developmental explanation is:
- (a) Psychotic denial requiring antipsychotics
- (b) Incomplete concept of death: full understanding of irreversibility and universality ordinarily develops by about 7 years
- (c) Manipulative attention-seeking
- (d) Early bipolar disorder

**Answer: (b).** Give simple biological explanations and repeated honest answers; the comprehension matures with mourning, not in spite of it.

**Q4.** A child witnessed her father's violent death. Six months later she has intrusive images and avoids all reminders. Bereavement counselling has failed. The next step is:
- (a) More intensive bereavement counselling
- (b) Trauma-focused treatment of PTSD first; mourning proceeds once the traumatic imagery is processed
- (c) Immediate ritual completion only
- (d) Reassurance and no treatment

**Answer: (b).** The image-blocking mechanism: trauma treatment precedes grief work, the Oxford chapter's explicit sequencing rule.

**Q5.** The controlled study of a brief family intervention 2 months after parental death found:
- (a) No effects at any time point
- (b) Reduced children's morbidity at 1 year, with differences no longer significant at 2 years
- (c) Worsened outcomes
- (d) Effects only in adults

**Answer: (b).** Symptom relief in short and medium term justified the intervention; children are a special case likely to benefit from primary (universal) intervention.

**Q6.** Which of the following is one of the four mechanisms by which parental illness transmits risk to children?
- (a) The child's school board
- (b) Parental illness interfering with parenting and parent-child interaction
- (c) The season of the child's birth
- (d) The child's birth order only

**Answer: (b).** With family/environmental discord, direct symptom involvement (delusions/obsessions incorporating the child), and bidirectional child effects, the complete four-mechanism answer.
