# Diagnosis & Classification — DSM/ICD Logic

## 1. In one line + overview

**Psychiatric classification is a negotiated engine built on unknown aetiology: descriptive rather than theoretical, operationalised for reliability, balancing the competing needs of clinicians, researchers, educators and statisticians, and its two great systems, ICD and DSM, differ in purpose (statistics vs nomenclature), impairment rules and structure, which is why the same patient can carry different labels in each.**

The Oxford chapter (First & Pincus) opens with psychiatry's diagnostic predicament: aetiology unknown for most disorders, reliance on reports and observation, laboratory tests largely irrelevant until recently, and historical records unreliable, producing "a long and rich history" of classification efforts. It then defines the territory (diagnosis as name and process; disease vs disorder vs illness vs sickness; classification as systematic grouping), asks the framing question, **"whose needs is the classification primarily intended to address?"** (clinicians wanting treatable categories; researchers wanting homogeneity; educators wanting structure; public-health administrators wanting statistics; critics wanting less stigmatizing misuse), and lists the four unresolved conceptual issues (descriptive vs theory-based; pathology vs normalcy; categorical vs dimensional; lumping vs splitting; plus the single- vs multiple-diagnosis hierarchy). The historical spine: from the 1855 causes-of-death nomenclature through ICD-6's first mental-disorders section (26 categories; only five countries adopted it), the operationalised-criteria revolution (the **Feighner criteria** with their 1,650 citations; the RDC), **DSM-III's** descriptive-athoretical breakthrough with its multiaxial system, the ICD-10 family of documents (Blue Book; research criteria; primary-care version) with its unprecedented 55-country field trials, and DSM-IV's meta-analytic empirical revision process. The systems' comparison: ICD as classification-for-statistics (validity no criterion for inclusion) vs DSM as sanctioned nomenclature (utility plus data); the **impairment difference** (ICD symptoms-only, DSM's clinical-significance criterion, the New York snake-phobia example); ICD-10's F-chapter architecture (F0-F9, the third/fourth-digit coding, five-sixth digit course codings) and its subchapter logic (organic; substance; schizophrenia-schizotypal-delusional with the 1-month vs DSM's 6-month duration; mood; neurotic-stress-somatoform; behavioural syndromes; personality; and the rest).

## 4. The core ideas

### 4.1 Definitions, goals and the conceptual arena

**Diagnosis** is both the name of a disease and the process of determining it. **Disease** where objective pathology or presumed aetiology exists (pancreatic cancer, strep throat, Alzheimer's); **disorder** where aetiology is unknown and definition is syndromic (symptoms, history, sometimes laboratory findings), psychiatry's term; **illness** the individual's subjective awareness of distress; **sickness** the inability to perform social roles. **Classification**: placing diagnostic entities into systematic groupings by principles of similarity and difference, and depending on the principles, classifications differ radically. **The framing question, whose needs?** Clinicians (categorise as many help-seekers as possible; facilitate identification, treatment, prognosis, cause); researchers (homogeneous groups for treatment-testing and aetiology); educators (structure for teaching psychopathology and differential diagnosis); public-health administrators (track epidemiology, utilisation, costs); critics (diagnosis as reductionistic labelling of individual differences and social deviance, exposing people to stigma, at minimum, less misuse), with ICD-10's answer: **different products for different target groups**. The **conceptual issues**: descriptive vs theory-based (classification from aetiological theory (psychodynamic, behavioural, neurobiological) vs a descriptive heuristic); pathology vs normalcy (what assumptions distinguish "mental disorder" from normative behaviour, "caseness"); categorical vs dimensional (discrete bounded categories vs continua, and which dimensions, chosen how); lumping vs splitting (few broad heterogeneous vs many homogeneous categories); single vs multiple diagnosis (hierarchy where diagnoses trump one another vs encouraged comorbidity). Systems do not apply their own rules consistently, compromises everywhere.

### 4.2 The historical engine: operationalisation

The first international classification (1855) was a nomenclature of causes of death; adopted by WHO in 1948 as ICD-6, which included for the first time a classification of mental disorders (10 psychoses, 9 psychoneuroses, 7 character/behaviour/intelligence categories), but its gaps (no dementias, most personality disorders, adjustment disorders) left only five adopter countries, and DSM-I (1952) arose as the American alternative, adding glossary definitions for the first time. ICD-8 (1968; British-viewed glossary 1974) and DSM-II defined for US use. **The early-1970s revolution: explicit operationalized criteria for research**, the glossary definitions too vague to identify homogeneous study populations; researchers built their own. The first wide-range set: **Robins and Guze (Washington University, St Louis)**, the **Feighner criteria** (named for the paper's first author), 16 disorders with inclusion and exclusion criteria, "to provide common ground for different research groups so that diagnostic definitions can be amended constructively", and enormously useful, measured by **1,650 citations 1972–82 against the typical 2.1 per paper**. The expansion: the **Research Diagnostic Criteria (RDC)** for the NIMH collaborative depression psychobiology project, heavily used in mood and psychotic research. WHO's ICD-9 preparation (eight annual international seminars 1965–72; published 1978); the US dissatisfaction with ICD-9's research inadequacy and its omission of the demonstrated operationalisation innovation → **DSM-III (1980)**: Spitzer's leadership, 14 advisory committees, drafts circulated internationally, criteria mostly derived from RDC plus expert consensus; with reliability gains over DSM-II's glossary demonstrated in the large NIMH field trial. **The descriptive-ath theoretical principle**: criteria based on symptomatic presentation, not cause-theories, which allowed clinicians of all orientations to agree: the cognitivist, the neurobiologist and the psychodynamicist understand panic's cause differently, but all can agree on how a panic attack *presents*. **The multiaxial system**: separating (and thereby attending to) personality and developmental disorders (Axis II), physical conditions (III), stressors (IV) and adaptive functioning (V) from the florid presenting Axis I, institutionalising the biopsychosocial model. Initial psychoanalytic opposition notwithstanding, DSM-III became the common language, translated into 13 languages, adopted internationally by researchers. Its revealed inconsistencies and data-driven corrections (the prohibition on diagnosing panic disorder alongside major depression, overturned by family data showing either disorder in relatives of comorbid probands) led to **DSM-III-R (1987)**.

**ICD-10** (work from 1982, Sartorius chairing): designed deliberately as **several versions**, the statistical/glossary version (the treaty-mandated official classification), the **Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Guidelines (the Blue Book**, detailed definitions plus guidelines for clinicians), the **Diagnostic Criteria for Research** (stricter than the clinical guidelines, "usually starts in early childhood" becomes "should not be made if onset is after 30", because clinicians don't observe strict rules in daily work while research demands them), and the **primary-care version** (fewer categories, treatment guidelines). The field-trial scale: clinical guidelines in **194 centres in 55 countries**; International Revision Conference approval (1989); World Health Assembly approval for 1993 introduction. **DSM-IV** (work from 1988): continued the descriptive approach but added a **three-stage empirical review**, systematic literature reviews (rules from a methods conference), MacArthur-funded data reanalyses (existing datasets combined meta-analytically, answering some questions like the minimum panic-attack count, limited by dataset incompatibilities), and 15 NIMH-funded field trials; the whole process documented in the four-volume Sourcebook. **The coordination effort**: joint DSM-IV/ICD-10 meetings to minimise differences, but the timelines defeated identity (ICD-10 categories were settled by 1989 when DSM-IV work began): the systems ended "much more similar than DSM-III and ICD-9" with persisting small differences, some genuine outlook differences (the **1-month vs 6-month schizophrenia duration**), most without justification.

### 4.3 The systems compared: and the ICD-10 architecture

**Classification vs nomenclature**: ICD is set up as a classification for statistics; the coder needs an unambiguous category; the inclusion rule is **common international usage, with no implication of validity** (categories included in ICD-10, like mixed anxiety-depression, were rejected by DSM-IV for validity concerns); DSM is a nomenclature, inclusion implies APA sanction, clinical utility and an empirical database (unevenly: old categories "grandfathered"; from DSM-IV, new categories faced the higher standards). **The impairment difference**: ICD-10 disorders (dementia and phobias nearly alone among exceptions) are defined by symptoms alone, impairment indicated separately through the ICF; most DSM-IV criteria sets carry the **clinical-significance criterion** (clinically significant distress or impairment), included to set the threshold where symptoms alone (occurring in non-disordered people too) would produce false positives. The worked example: **the New Yorker with snake phobia**, never encountering snakes, no functional impact: not a mental disorder in DSM-IV; a snake phobia in ICD-10 (the fear/avoidance would be there if confronted). The rationale for DSM's position: descriptive symptoms are not specific to disorder (pneumonia is diagnosed by the bacillus regardless of function; psychiatric symptoms cannot be, absent pathology evidence, hence impairment/distress as the threshold). 

**The ICD-10 F-chapter structure**: psychiatric disorders occupy Chapter V, letter F, with the second digit giving the larger group and the third the special group (three digits = 100 possibilities; the fourth digit = 1,000, one-third used, designed for expansion); a fifth/sixth digit codes course or features; X/Y/Z codes add circumstances (suicide), symptoms and psychosocial factors; somatic comorbidity from the related chapters. Unique among ICD chapters, F carries short definitions plus inclusion and exclusion terms for every disorder (the diagnostic-reliability inheritance). The family: the Short Glossary (statisticians, insurance clerks), the **Blue Book CDDG** (the clinician's central text, many unclear cases unsuitable for research are described here), the research criteria, the multiaxial presentation (axis I all disorders, psychiatric coded alongside other medical; axis II disability, with WHO's DAS/WHODAS instruments; and the contextual-factors axis), and the primary-care version (roughly two dozen categories with flowcharts and treatment guidance).

**The F-subchapter logic** (the chapter's own tour): **F0 organic/symptomatic**, organic aetiology regardless of psychotic or non-psychotic presentation, "organic" not implying that other conditions lack cerebral substrate; **F1 substance**, all psychoactive-substance disorders in one place: the third digit identifies the substance (F10 alcohol), the fourth the syndrome (F10.3 withdrawal state), the fifth the qualifier (F10.31 with convulsions), intoxication, harmful use, dependence, withdrawal with/without delirium, psychotic, amnesic syndromes; **F2 schizophrenia, schizotypal, delusional**: the 1-month duration rule; **acute and transient psychotic disorders given particular attention for developing countries**, where short-lasting good-prognosis psychoses are frequent (an Indian-exam favourite with direct local relevance); **F3 mood**, all mood disorders together, the endogenous/neurotic distinction abandoned, neurotic depression (ICD-9 300.4) dissolved mostly into dysthymia (F34.1), recurrent mania coded bipolar regardless of depressions; **F4 neurotic, stress-related, somatoform**, many categories (dissociative disorders in seven subtypes), hysteria abandoned, stress/adjustment reactions by time and severity, **somatoform disorders of particular importance in developing countries**, neurasthenia retained (unlike DSM-IV); **F5 behavioural syndromes with physiological disturbances**: eating, non-organic sleep, sexual dysfunction, puerperal disorders; gender identity and sexual preference moved to F6; the **F54 psychosomatic code** (psychological/behavioural factors in diseases classified elsewhere, code alongside the somatic diagnosis); **F6 personality and behaviour**: the specific personality disorders (cyclothymic to F3 as cyclothymia; schizotypal to F21 in F2; emotionally unstable personality with impulsive and borderline types); plus the factitious-disorder innovation; **F7 mental retardation**, **F8 developmental**, **F9 childhood-onset**.

*(Modern updates, flagged as post-Oxford: DSM-5 (2013) dropped the multiaxial system, dimensional specifiers and cross-cutting measures appeared, and autism-spectrum reorganisation; ICD-11 (2022 operating) followed with further dimensional hybrids, personality disorder by severity-plus-trait, and complex-PTSD. The Oxford chapter's conceptual issues, categorical vs dimensional above all, predicted exactly these reform directions.)*

## 5. Why it matters clinically

- **The atheoretical compact** is what lets a multidisciplinary team share one diagnostic language; you need not agree about *why* the panic happens to agree *that* it does; that compact is classification's daily clinical achievement.
- **The impairment rule decides caseness**: DSM's clinical-significance criterion is the threshold between symptoms (common in everyone) and disorder, the anxiety-39.5%-to-3.2% lesson of the child-epidemiology note in classification form.
- **The ICD-DSM differences are examinable and practical**: the same patient is a snake-phobic in ICD-10 and healthy in DSM-IV; schizophrenia's duration gate differs (1 vs 6 months); Indian practice codes ICD.
- **The subchapter architecture is a mental map of psychiatry**: F0-F9 tells you where every condition lives, and the coding depth (fourth-fifth digits) is the clinical record's precision instrument.
- **The acute-psychotic provision** (F23) was written *for* developing countries, the classification acknowledging Indian and tropical presentations where brief good-prognosis psychoses dominate.
- **Operationalisation's heritage**: the Feighner-to-DSM-III lineage is why psychiatric research exists at all, reliable diagnosis preceding (and enabling) every subsequent evidence base the KYP library teaches.

## 6. India lens

- **India codes ICD**: the Coding in Indian records, exams, insurance and disability certification is ICD-10 (migrating to ICD-11); the DSM difference-knowledge matters for reading the international literature and for exam-diploma questions that test both.
- **The F23 acute-and-transient provision is India's chapter**: the WHO field trials that validated it drew heavily on developing-country data where the brief polymorphic psychoses (the acute-transient-psychosis note's territory) are frequent; the classification was built with Indian presentations in mind.
- **The somatoform-developing-country note**: ICD-10's own commentary flags somatoform disorders as of particular importance in developing countries; the somatic front door of Indian depression and anxiety is written into the classification's design rationale.
- **Neurasthenia retained**: ICD-10 keeps the category (DSM-IV dropped it), the weakness/fatigue-centred presentation common in Indian and East Asian clinics has a home in ICD that DSM denies.
- **The primary-care version is the Indian instrument**: the WHO's PHC version (few categories, flowcharts, treatment guidance) was designed for exactly the Indian district-health infrastructure; the DMHP and primary-care-psychiatry note's training curriculum is its natural deployment.
- **Exams and the conceptual issues**: categorical-vs-dimensional, the multiaxial logic, the ICD-DSM differences and the operationalisation history are standard Indian postgraduate and diploma essay questions; this note is that answer, sourced.

**FAQ**
*"Why do psychiatrists talk about disorders rather than diseases?"*: Disease implies known pathology or aetiology; psychiatry's conditions are defined syndromically, symptom patterns with distress and dysfunction, because their causes are mostly unknown; hence the honest term disorder, with the classification itself admitting this.
*"Which is right, ICD or DSM?"*: They serve different masters: ICD is a statistical classification (inclusion by international usage, no validity claim), DSM a sanctioned nomenclature (inclusion implies data); hence the same patient can be a case in one and not the other, the snake phobia with no functional impact. Indian records use ICD; the international research you read uses both.
*"Why does my DSM-diagnosed patient have six disorders?"*: DSM abandoned strict hierarchy for comorbidity (to communicate more diagnostic information), while encouraging multiple diagnoses; the dimensional critique and the overlapping criteria explain the pile-up, a known cost of the categorical compromise.
*"Is schizophrenia 1 month or 6?"*: In ICD-10, 1 month of symptoms; in DSM-IV, 6 months of illness (including prodrome), the classic genuine-outlook difference; the Indian record follows ICD's month.
*"Do these categories reflect brain reality?"*: Not yet: they are descriptive compromises awaiting aetiological validation; the modern moves (dimensional specifiers, RDoC-style frameworks, ICD-11's trait model) are the current attempts, exactly the conceptual issues the Oxford chapter laid out.
*"What was wrong with 'neurotic depression'?"*: The endogenous/neurotic split lacked validity: ICD-10 dissolved neurotic depression largely into dysthymia, ending the dualism, one of classification's cleanest deletions.

**Self-test MCQs**

**Q1.** The fundamental difference in purpose between ICD-10 and DSM-IV is:
- (a) ICD is for children; DSM for adults
- (b) ICD is a classification system for statistical purposes (inclusion by common international usage, no validity implication), whereas DSM is a diagnostic nomenclature (inclusion implies clinical utility and an empirical database)
- (c) ICD is European; DSM is pharmacological
- (d) No difference exists

**Answer: (b).** Classification vs nomenclature: explaining why mixed anxiety-depression could be in ICD-10 but fail DSM-IV's validity screen.

**Q2.** A New Yorker with marked snake fear who never encounters snakes and suffers no functional impact:
- (a) Has a disorder in both systems
- (b) Has specific phobia in ICD-10 (symptoms sufficient) but no mental disorder in DSM-IV (no clinically significant distress or impairment)
- (c) Has a disorder in DSM-IV but not ICD-10
- (d) Has schizophrenia

**Answer: (b).** The worked example of the impairment difference: DSM's clinical-significance criterion as the false-positive guard; ICD's orthogonal ICF for function.

**Q3.** The first widely used set of operationalized diagnostic criteria, cited 1,650 times in a decade, was:
- (a) The DSM-5 criteria
- (b) The Feighner criteria (Robins and Guze, Washington University, 1972)
- (c) The ICD-8 glossary
- (d) Hippocrates' humours

**Answer: (b).** The research-community response to unmeasurably vague glossary definitions, 16 disorders with explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria, later expanded into the RDC and incorporated into DSM-III.

**Q4.** DSM-III's landmark contributions included:
- (a) The aetiological theory of schizophrenia
- (b) The descriptive atheoretical approach (criteria by presentation, not cause-theory), explicit criteria, and the multiaxial system
- (c) The abolition of diagnosis
- (d) Psychoanalytic aetiology throughout

**Answer: (b).** The compact that let all orientations share one language, plus Axis II-V institutionalising the biopsychosocial evaluation.

**Q5.** In the ICD-10 F-chapter, the code F10.31 (alcohol withdrawal state with convulsions) illustrates:
- (a) Random numbering
- (b) The systematic depth: third digit = substance, fourth = syndrome, fifth = qualifier
- (c) A US-only code
- (d) A personality-disorder code

**Answer: (b).** The coding architecture: substance-by-syndrome precision designed for statistical and clinical discrimination, expandable to a thousand diagnoses at four digits.

**Q6.** ICD-10's special attention to acute and transient psychotic disorders was driven by:
- (a) European litigation
- (b) Their frequency in developing countries, where short-lasting good-prognosis psychoses are common, the classification built with those presentations in the field trials
- (c) Insurance companies
- (d) DSM-IV's demands

**Answer: (b).** The developing-country provision: directly relevant to Indian practice, where the brief polymorphic psychoses are common and the F23 block serves them.
