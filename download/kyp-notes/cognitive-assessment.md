# Cognitive Assessment — The Psychometrics of Mind Measurement

## 1. In one line + overview

**Cognitive assessment is measurement, and every measurement carries error: the reliable clinician knows the true-score equation, the reliability-validity discipline, the difference-score traps, and the domain batteries from Wechsler IQ to memory, attention and executive function, or misreads every number on the page.**

The Oxford chapter (Powell) opens with the axiom that cuts through all of it: assessment, testing, measurement is the evaluation of the individual in numerical or categorical terms under statistical and psychometric principles, **even diagnosis is a form of measurement** and should carry reliability and validity like any test. The teaching spine: **classical test theory** (the true score *t*, the observed score *x*, the error *e*, the basic equation *x = t + e*; the standard error of measurement; the reliability-validity tables); **the scales of measurement** (nominal, ordinal, interval) and their statistical consequences; **the standard-score logic** (z-scores, percentiles, the IQ mapping with its category bands); **the difference-score discipline**, the crucial clinical trap: a *reliable* difference (statistically reproducible: about 9 VIQ-PIQ points on WAIS-III) is not an *abnormal* difference (found in fewer than 5% of the population: about 22 points), confusing them generates "all manner of erroneous conclusions". Then the instruments: the **Mini-Mental State Examination** as screening (24-or-less with 9+ years education, ~10th percentile at 65+; never diagnostic alone); the **Wechsler scales** (WAIS-III-UK 16–89, WISC-IV 6–16.11, WPPSI; the subtest-index architecture with full-scale IQ reliability ~0.98, SEM ~2.5, the reliable/abnormal difference thresholds); the non-verbal alternatives (Progressive Matrices); **processing speed** (the concussion-sensitive tests, PASAT, Map Search); **attention** (Test of Everyday Attention); **memory** (Wechsler Memory Scale-III with its honest reliabilities 0.60–0.87 and practice effects up to 15 points; Children's Memory Scale; Rivermead Behavioural Memory Test; the individual classics: Rey Auditory-Verbal Learning, Rey-Osterrieth, Recognition Memory, Autobiographical Memory); **language** (Boston Diagnostic Aphasia Examination, Western Aphasia Battery, the brief screeners: Boston Naming, Graded Naming, Token Test, the Wechsler reading dimensions); **executive function** (the dysexecutive syndrome concept and its frontal and non-frontal causes); and the test-user's duties: reading the manual (the 13-item checklist of what a good manual contains), knowing the common test problems, and asking local psychologists what they use.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- State the measurement axiom and the classical equation (x = t + e) with the SEM-reliability relationship.
- Distinguish the five types of reliability (scorer, test-retest with the 0.8 threshold, parallel-form, split-half, internal consistency with the 0.7 floor) and the seven types of validity (face, content, concurrent, predictive, construct, factorial, incremental).
- Use the scales of measurement (nominal/ordinal/interval) and their permitted statistics.
- Convert scores to z-scores, percentiles and IQ equivalents, and use the category bands (deficient <2.5th; borderline 2.5–10th; low average 10–25th; average 25–75th; superior >90th).
- Apply the **difference-score discipline**: reliable (WAIS-III VIQ-PIQ ~9 points) versus abnormal (~22 adults, ~26 children), and the subtest abnormality threshold (11–12 points).
- Use the MMSE as a screen only (the education caveat), and the Wechsler architecture (subtests, index scores, reliability, SEM).
- Name the domain batteries and individual tests: speed (PASAT, Map Search, digit cancellation); attention (TEA); memory (WMS-III, CMS, Rivermead, Rey AVLT, Rey-Osterrieth, RMT, AMI); language (BDAE, WAB, Boston/Graded Naming, Token, WORD); executive (the dysexecutive syndrome).
- Read a test manual with the 13-point checklist; name the common test problems; state the non-English-testing caveat and the ecological-validity question.

## 4. The core ideas

### 4.1 Test theory: the discipline before the tests

**The axiom**: assessment is measurement, categories, scales, ability tests, psychophysiology, and diagnosis itself, all subject to reliability and validity. **Classical test theory**: tests aim to measure a real quantity (social anxiety, spelling, spatial memory), the *true score* t; the obtained score is the *observed score* x; every test carries error e; hence **x = t + e**: the most basic equation in psychometrics. Repeated testing would scatter observed scores around the true score with a standard deviation: the **standard error of measurement (SEM)**; a good test drives SEM toward zero; the relationship **SEM = SD√(1 − r)** where r is the test-retest reliability; perfect reliability (+1) gives SEM zero. **Reliability types** (the chapter's table): scorer/rater reliability (two judges agree; should be near perfect); **test-retest** (same result on separated occasions, as a correlation; *below 0.8 is dubious*); parallel-form (equivalent versions agree); split-half (odd vs even items, with the Kuder-Richardson mean of all splits); internal consistency (item-to-item correlation, α **not below 0.7**). **Validity types**: face (seems sensible, non-statistical, but without it patients see no point); content (covers all aspects: expert judgement); **concurrent** (discriminates criterion groups; correlates with kindred tests); **predictive** (forecasts future criteria: child IQ to adult occupation); the last two jointly *criterion-related*; **construct** (measures the hypothetical construct: convergent and divergent correlations); factorial (stable subfactors); **incremental** (improves actual decision-making, do neuropsychological results improve brain-injury detection?). Threat examples: a numeracy test so stressful that scores track anxiety; a social-comprehension test so culturally biased that scores track ethnicity.

**Scales and standard scores.** **Nominal** scales (labels for categories: marital status; group-splitting; χ² associations); **ordinal** (rank order without magnitude assumptions: non-parametric statistics); **interval** (fixed units: height, speed; parametric statistics): the chapter's tests aspire to interval level. **Central tendency** (mean, median, mode) gives the first normality hint; the **standard deviation** converts to precision: the observed score becomes **z = (x − m)/SD**, and z to a percentile; the 50th percentile being the mean. The IQ mapping (mean 100, SD 15) with the category bands: below the **2.5th percentile deficient** (IQ 70); 2.5–10th **borderline** (IQ 75–80); 10–25th **low average** (85–90); 25–75th **average** (90–110); 75–90th **high average** (115–120); above the 90th **superior**. Percentile logic also selects between groups: a token-test score at the 5th percentile for normals and the 63rd for dysphasics belongs to the dysphasic distribution.

**The difference-score discipline: the clinical trap.** Clinical practice compares the patient with *herself* (verbal vs spatial IQ; memory quotient vs IQ): **difference scores** with two independent concepts: *reliability* of the difference (unlikely to be chance; reproducible on retesting, on the WAIS-III, about **9 points** between verbal and performance IQ at the 95% level) and *abnormality* of the difference (**the percentage of the general population with a difference this size or greater**, 18% of adults differ by ≥10 VIQ-PIQ points; abnormality requires about **22 points in adults, 26 in children**, i.e. under 5% prevalence). "Failure to distinguish between these two leads to all manner of erroneous conclusions." Subtest scatter: it takes a subtest range of about **11–12 points** to be abnormal (under 5% of people). The general duty: read the manual, the chapter's table of what a good manual discloses: development history, construct and purpose, the standardisation sample, criterion-group data, age-range data, administration standard, scoring criteria, means and SDs, **reliability coefficients and how obtained, validity measures, the SEM, reliability AND abnormality of difference scores, subtest scatter data, criterion-group scores, unsuitable groups, and the ceiling and floor effects**.

### 4.2 The instruments: by domain

**General ability.** The **Mini-Mental State Examination**: brief, repeatable over time to gauge change; orientation, naming, language, memory, basic non-verbal skills; good middle-age-and-elderly norms; **a score of 24 or less raises the possibility of dementia in older persons with nine or more years of education** (24 ≈ the 10th percentile at 65+), but **only a screen: neither presence nor nature of cognitive impairment can be diagnosed on the MMSE alone**. The **Wechsler scales** carry the detailed assessment: WAIS-III-UK (16–89 years), WISC-IV-UK (6.0–16.11), WPPSI, with verbal subtests (vocabulary, similarities, digit span, arithmetic, information, comprehension, letter-number sequencing) and performance subtests (picture completion, block design, digit symbol/coding, matrix reasoning, symbol search, and the rest), index scores (verbal comprehension, perceptual organisation/reasoning, working memory/freedom from distractibility, processing speed), mean 100 SD 15, subtest mean 10 SD 3. **Full-scale IQ reliability ~0.98; SEM ~2.5 (all scores ±5 points); reliable VIQ-PIQ difference ~9 points; abnormal ~22; VCI-PRI reliable ~11, abnormal ~26.** The critical interpretive rule: **IQ is very stable and therefore insensitive to anything except gross brain damage; subtest analysis, under the difference-score discipline, does the sensitive work.** Where language disorder or non-English testing confounds: the **Progressive Matrices** (non-verbal inductive reasoning).

**Speed of processing.** Reasoning is not only accuracy but speed, sensitive to even **mild concussion**; the depressed-speed battery: choice-reaction-type tasks, **Map Search** and Telephone Search (visual targets under time pressure), digit-symbol/coding, the **PASAT** (paced auditory serial addition, each new digit added to the previous, a working-memory-plus-speed load with the classic 2.4-second pacing), digit-cancellation tasks, and the Adult Memory and Information Processing Battery's timed cancellations disentangling motor from mental slowing.

**Attention.** The aspects: focusing resources, focusing on the *right* aspect, sustaining, ignoring distraction, and dividing attention. The speed tests are attention tests by nature; digit span tests attention (any lapse produces a wrong answer); the **Test of Everyday Attention** (eight subtests; test-retest reliabilities over 0.83 for Map and Telephone Search; validity demonstrated by sensitivity to head injury and stroke).

**Memory.** Memory is a complex of processes (registration, storage, retrieval) across modalities (verbal vs spatial) and periods (short-term, long-term/learning), requiring battery assessment: the **Wechsler Memory Scale-III** (16–89; subtests spanning information/orientation, logical memory, faces, verbal paired associates, family pictures, word lists, visual reproduction, letter-number sequencing, spatial span, mental control, digit span; index scores auditory/visual immediate and delayed, recognition, general and working memory; **index reliabilities 0.60–0.87, rather low, with practice effects up to 15 points across 5 weeks; SEM 3.88–7.40, so true scores at best ±8 points**) and the **Children's Memory Scale** (5–16; reliabilities 0.76–0.91; practice effect 10–15 points over 2 months). The ecological alternative: the **Rivermead Behavioural Memory Test** (with child version) designed to reflect real-life memory tasks. Time-limited practice selects key subtests or individual classics: **Rey Auditory-Verbal Learning Test** (verbal learning, well researched), **Rey-Osterrieth Complex Figure** (visual memory), **Recognition Memory Test** (forced-choice words and faces), **Autobiographical Memory Interview** (remote personal memory).

**Language.** The full batteries: **Boston Diagnostic Aphasia Examination** and the closely related **Western Aphasia Battery**, auditory comprehension, oral expression, written understanding, writing; long to administer, hence the brief screeners: **Boston Naming Test** and **Graded Naming Test** (word-finding), **Token Test** (verbal comprehension), and the **Wechsler Objective Reading Dimensions** (reading and spelling ages, with the IQ-vs-reading difference-score abnormality for dyslexia identification).

**Executive function.** The construct: a supervisory system exerting executive control of attention; its deficit produces the **dysexecutive syndrome** (changes in volition, poor planning, disruption of purposive action, reduced efficacy of performance) most frequently from **frontal-lobe damage (the frontal-lobe syndrome is a dysexecutive syndrome) but also from other lesion patterns**; the chapter's table pairs dysexecutive features with the tests sensitive to them (cognitive estimates, fluency, planning/search tasks such as the Tower of London and Wisconsin family, and behavioural measures).

**Test-user duties.** Sources: Lezak's *Neuropsychological Assessment*, Strauss's *Compendium*, Mitrushina's *Handbook of Normative Data*. The common problems: small standardisation samples; unknown or unstable factor structures; poor theoretical adequacy; no criterion groups; vague scoring; absent difference-score data. The onus is on the user to gauge strengths and limitations. The **local caveat**: this account is of English-language tests; readers elsewhere should note the principles but **ask local psychologists what tests they use**.

## 5. Why it matters clinically

- **Every psychiatric number is a measurement with error**: the discipline (x = t + e; SEM; reliability thresholds) applies to rating scales, screening tools and diagnoses, not only to IQ.
- **The difference-score trap is a daily clinical error**: a 12-point VIQ-PIQ gap is reliable but perfectly normal; treating it as brain damage generates false neuropsychiatry; the 9/22-point rule prevents it.
- **The MMSE boundary** (24 with education) is a screen that diagnoses nothing, the commonest misreading of the commonest test.
- **Change-over-time needs practice-effect discipline**: memory-battery gains of 10–15 points on retest can be learning, not recovery.
- **IQ's insensitivity**: a normal full-scale IQ excludes only gross damage; the sensitive information lives in the subtests, the domains and the differences.
- **India**: the manual-discipline and the ask-local-psychologists caveat are the Indian clinician's daily reality, imported tests with Western norms, education gradients, and the bilingual assessment problem.

## 6. India lens

- **The education problem is Indian psychometrics' central issue**: MMSE and similar instruments carry education gradients far steeper in India's literacy distribution; an illiterate 70-year-old failing the orientation-reading items is not demented by that failure; literacy-adjusted norms (developed by Indian centres) and the education-stratified interpretation are mandatory, exactly the reliability-threat logic the chapter teaches (the test measuring education rather than cognition).
- **The bilingual testing problem**: assessing cognition in the patient's second language measures language, not cognition; the Rey-Osterrieth (language-reduced) and performance subtests carry the assessment, with the Matrices as the non-verbal anchor; the chapter's "ask local psychologists" is the Indian instruction; Indian neuropsychology batteries (the NIMHANS adaptations) exist and should be preferred to imported norms.
- **The difference-score teaching gift**: Indian trainees over-read single low scores (a 78 VIQ); the 9/22 and 11–12-point rules, the SEM (±5 points) and the practice-effect warnings convert test reports from oracle-readings into measurements.
- **The ecological-validity question**: Indian functional demands differ: a Rivermead-style everyday-memory logic, adapted to the household's real tasks (money-handling, route-finding, recipe-keeping), is the practical family-report supplement to batteries.
- **Costs (approx 2026)**: the imported test kits and their licences are the costly layer (major private centres only); the MMSE, Rey figures and verbal-learning lists are free; the scarce resource is trained neuropsychologists; district practice relies on the screening-plus-informant discipline with referral for the battery.

**FAQ**
*"The report says his IQ is 92, is that normal?"*: Yes, average band, but the fuller truth: the score carries about ±5 points of measurement error, and a normal full-scale IQ only excludes *gross* damage; the subtests, domains and differences carry the clinical information.
*"Her verbal IQ is 15 points below her performance IQ, surely that's brain damage?"*: No: that difference is statistically reliable but found in nearly a fifth of adults; abnormality (fewer than 5% of the population) needs about 22 points; confusing the two is the classic error.
*"Mother scored 22 on the MMSE, does she have dementia?"*: It raises the possibility (with her education level considered), but the MMSE is a screen: neither the presence nor the nature of impairment can be diagnosed from it; a full assessment, and the clinical picture, decide.
*"Her memory test improved by 12 points after treatment, recovery?"*: Possibly, but memory batteries show practice effects of 10–15 points on retesting, and some index reliabilities are modest; change-claims need the manual's retest discipline behind them.
*"Which memory test should we use?"*: The battery for the question (WMS-III or Children's by age; Rivermead for everyday relevance) or, under time pressure, the individual classics (Rey Auditory-Verbal for verbal learning, Rey-Osterrieth for visual memory) always against appropriate norms.
*"Can we test a patient who doesn't speak English fluently?"*: Only with caution and adaptation: language confounds the verbal tests; the non-verbal battery (Matrices, block design, figure tasks) carries the assessment, and local psychologists' validated translations and norms are the right tools (the chapter's own instruction).

**Self-test MCQs**

**Q1.** In classical test theory, the relationship between observed (x), true (t), and error (e) scores is:
- (a) x = t − e
- (b) x = t + e
- (c) t = x + e
- (d) e = x + t

**Answer: (b).** The observed score is the true score plus error, and the statistical aim of psychometrics is to drive the error term (the SEM) toward zero through reliability.

**Q2.** On the WAIS-III, a difference between verbal and performance IQ of about 9 points is:
- (a) Statistically reliable (reproducible) at the 95% level, but not abnormal, since 18% of adults differ by 10 or more points
- (b) Diagnostic of brain damage
- (c) Statistically unreliable
- (d) Impossible to obtain

**Answer: (a).** The reliable-versus-abnormal distinction: the chapter's most emphasised clinical lesson; abnormality needs about 22 points in adults (26 in children).

**Q3.** An MMSE score of 24 or less in an older person with nine or more years of education:
- (a) Diagnoses Alzheimer's disease
- (b) Raises the possibility of dementia (about the 10th percentile at 65+), a screen only, never diagnostic
- (c) Is within the normal range
- (d) Diagnoses depression

**Answer: (b).** The MMSE is a repeatable screening instrument; the presence and nature of impairment require full cognitive assessment.

**Q4.** A test-retest reliability coefficient below 0.8, and an internal-consistency coefficient (α) below 0.7, are respectively:
- (a) Excellent and excellent
- (b) Dubious and not acceptable
- (c) Irrelevant and irrelevant
- (d) Reversed: 0.8 for α and 0.7 for test-retest

**Answer: (b).** The chapter's two rule-of-thumb floors: reliability thresholds that make the SEM interpretable.

**Q5.** Full-scale IQ on the Wechsler scales (test-retest reliability about 0.98) is:
- (a) Highly sensitive to subtle brain damage
- (b) Very stable and therefore insensitive to anything except gross brain damage; subtest analysis under the difference-score discipline carries the sensitivity
- (c) Unreliable
- (d) Equivalent to the MMSE

**Answer: (b).** Stability is insensitivity: the aggregate hides what the profile's reliabilities and abnormalities reveal.

**Q6.** The dysexecutive syndrome: changes in volition, poor planning, disruption of purposive action, reduced performance efficacy:
- (a) Is caused exclusively by frontal-lobe damage
- (b) Most frequently follows frontal damage but may arise from other lesion patterns
- (c) Is a memory disorder
- (d) Is a culture-bound syndrome

**Answer: (b).** The supervisory-system construct: the frontal-lobe syndrome *is* a dysexecutive syndrome, but not the only route to it.
