# Frontotemporal dementia

## 1. In one line + overview

**Tagline:** Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) is a younger-onset dementia that attacks the **frontal and temporal lobes first**, so **personality, behaviour and language change before memory does**, and it is routinely mistaken for depression, marital breakdown, mania, obsessive–compulsive disorder or "a midlife crisis" for years before anyone thinks of the brain.

**Overview, in plain words:** A 55-year-old bank manager starts making off-colour jokes in meetings, buys things he cannot afford, eats from other people's plates, stops bathing, and no longer asks about his grandchildren. His memory is fine. His MRI is quietly striking: the frontal and temporal lobes are shrinking. This is FTD; a family of dementias that begins, on average, between **45 and 65**, making it one of the **commonest causes of young-onset dementia** (in younger groups it rivals or beats Alzheimer's). There are two main doors into it: the **behavioural door** (disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, compulsive habits, changed eating) and the **language door** (speaking effortfully and ungrammatically, or slowly losing the *meanings* of words). Roughly **a third to two-fifths of cases run in families**, the strongest familial signal of any common dementia, and some patients later develop, or develop alongside, motor neuron disease (ALS), which changes prognosis profoundly. There is no disease-modifying medicine yet: treatment is behaviour management, caregiver architecture, language support and foresighted planning. The diagnosis itself is a huge intervention; it converts "he has become a terrible person" into "he has an illness of the frontal lobes", which rescues marriages, inheritances and the family's ability to care.

## 2. Learning objectives

After working through this note, you should be able to:

1. Define FTD and its three clinical presentations: behavioural variant, semantic-variant language, and nonfluent/agrammatic language.
2. Recite the behavioural-variant feature clusters (disinhibition; apathy; loss of empathy; compulsive/stereotyped behaviour; hyperorality/dietary change; executive decline) in your own words.
3. Explain why FTD is the **great psychiatric mimic** of midlife, and list its five commonest misdiagnoses.
4. Describe the two underlying proteins (tau and TDP-43) and the three main familial genes.
5. Order and interpret the diagnostic workup: collateral history, frontal-weighted testing, MRI/FDG-PET, and the rule-out list.
6. Manage it without disease-modifying options: behavioural strategies, selective SSRIs, careful sedation only when needed, speech therapy, caregiver and legal planning.
7. Screen for and counsel about motor neuron disease overlap.
8. Advise genetic counselling appropriately for Indian families, including its practical limits.

## 4. Epidemiology

### Global

- Prevalence estimates are roughly comparable to early-onset Alzheimer's in the 45–64 band; in specialist young-onset dementia series, **FTD is the leading or second diagnosis**.
- Mean onset **45–65 (typical late 50s)**; about 20 years earlier than typical Alzheimer's, which is precisely why its social blast radius (jobs, marriages, children still at school) is so much wider.
- Behavioural variant is the commonest presentation; language variants together form a substantial minority.
- **Familial burden: 30–40%** of patients have a strong family history; versus a much weaker familial signal in late-onset Alzheimer's.
- **Survival: roughly 7–13 years from onset on average**; with a wide fork: the FTD–MND subgroup may survive only 2–5 years, while some language-variant patients live well over a decade.

### India

- No systematic national prevalence; young-onset dementia is doubly invisible in India; families attribute midlife behaviour change to stress, finances, "bad company", or the spouse, and psychiatric referral comes first (which is why psychiatrists are the real front line of Indian FTD detection).
- Indian memory-clinic series from metros report FTD forming a meaningful share of young-onset dementia, usually diagnosed after **2–4 years** of symptom duration, frequently after failed antidepressant trials and often after marital or employment breakdown.
- The genetic counselling infrastructure (clinical geneticists, pre-test counselling, insurance protections) is thin and private-sector dependent; the practical Indian approach is careful family-history mapping and tertiary referral when a dominant inheritance pattern stands out.
- The FTD–MND overlap is under-appreciated: neurology clinics manage the ALS; families are often never told the behavioural change is part of the same disease.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**Biological, the proteins:**

- **Tau** (the microtubule protein already famous from Alzheimer's tangles) misfolds in one group of FTDs, including the classic historical "Pick's disease" pattern.
- **TDP-43** (an RNA-handling protein) misfolds in the other large group; the same protein that accumulates in ALS, which is why the two diseases overlap.
- A rarer third protein (FUS) accounts for a small fraction.
- The pathology, not the clinical picture, decides the familial gene: tau-inclusion families carry *MAPT* mutations; TDP-43 families carry *GRN* or *C9orf72*.

**Genetic: know the trio:**

- **MAPT** (chromosome 17, tau); often prominent early personality change, sometimes parkinsonism.
- **GRN** (progranulin), often asymmetric, parietal-touching presentations; language and behavioural mixes.
- **C9orf72**: the massive hexanucleotide-repeat expansion that causes both FTD and ALS in the same family tree; may also present as late-onset psychosis-like pictures.
- Sporadic cases (the majority overall) have no identified mutation.

**Risk factors / red flags for familial disease:**

- Onset under 60, especially under 50.
- A family tree containing any mix of: young dementia, ALS/motor neuron disease, "psychiatric breakdowns" misdiagnosed decades ago, and early parkinsonism.

**Psychological/social amplifiers:** none cause FTD, but midlife stressors (job loss, debt, menopause, empty-nesting) are what families *blame*, and the blaming itself delays diagnosis by years. One clinical task is always to remove the blame and re-attach the change to the brain.

**Indian context:** joint-family conflict, property disputes and alcohol assumptions ("he must be drinking") are the commonest initial family explanations offered in Indian clinics for early bvFTD; every one of them delays the MRI by another year.

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: the conductor retires.** The frontal lobes are the brain's conductor: they inhibit impulses, sequence plans, monitor whether behaviour fits the room, and read other people's feelings. FTD begins by silencing the conductor, often asymmetrically, right side first. With the conductor gone, the orchestra plays whatever it likes: the jokes that were always privately thought now get said aloud; the second helping becomes the fifth; the schedule becomes a rigid ritual. **Nothing has been added to the person; something restraining has been subtracted**; that sentence, told to families, does more therapeutic work than any prescription.

**Story 2: the dictionary melts.** In the semantic variant, anterior temporal lobes hold the concept-dictionary, the meanings that link the word "camel" to humps, desert and patience. As those hubs dissolve, patients substitute increasingly general words ("animal... a walking thing"), lose the ability to name even common objects, yet keep fluent grammar and normal rhythm, the speech sounds effortless and empty. Remarkably, the same melting affects **knowledge about people and famous figures**, which is why some patients stop recognising the emotional weight of old friends long before they stop recognising faces.

**Story 3: the shared protein with motor disease.** In some patients, TDP-43 also climbs into the motor neurons of the spinal cord. Within the same year or a few years, the same patient who lost his manners begins to drop cups, trip on kerbs and waste the hand muscles, motor neuron disease. This overlap is not a coincidence of two illnesses; it is one protein process seen from two windows. Its recognition changes the conversation: prognosis planning, breathing and swallowing surveillance, and honest pacing with the family.

## 7. Symptoms

### Behavioural variant (bvFTD): the commonest face

- **Disinhibition:** tactless remarks, overfamiliarity with strangers, public impropriety, careless spending, new gambling or gift-buying, touching everything in a shop. *Not* mania, no sleeplessness, no flight of ideas, no contagious euphoria; the "high" is flat.
- **Apathy:** initiates nothing, abandons hobbies years-long-loved, sits for hours, the mirror image of disinhibition, and **the variant most mislabelled as depression** (but apathy is indifferent, while depression is painful; and antidepressants do nothing for FTD apathy).
- **Loss of empathy and social warmth:** stops asking after family, cold response to a spouse's illness, absent grief at a parent's death; described by families as "he is present in body only". This is the deepest wound, and the clearest discriminator from depression (the depressed patient still cares, and says so).
- **Compulsive, stereotyped behaviours:** walks the same route daily, counts/rearranges objects, repeats one joke or hymn, compulsive clock-watching, newly rigid food rituals; mimics OCD in content but is **ego-syntonic, unsought, unresisted and fixed** in form.
- **Hyperorality and dietary change:** sweet-and-carbohydrate craving, grabbing food from others' plates, eating non-food items in advanced disease, **weight gain** (vs weight loss in depression; vs late anorexia of Alzheimer's), one of the most reliable early signs in Indian family reports: "he finished the entire box of mithai meant for guests".
- **Executive decline with preserved memory:** can still recall yesterday's events and store new memories, while unable to plan a puja or follow a bank form. **Passes simple bedside memory tests: fails life.**
- **Neglect of hygiene and dressing**, absent embarrassment at soiling; utilisation behaviour (uses every object placed before him, glasses, pens, stethoscope) and environmental dependency (imitating the examiner's actions) in classical neurology teaching, frontal "release" signs.
- Insight is absent-to-patchy from early on; confabulated rationalisations fill the gaps ("I am not bathing because I am saving water").

### Semantic variant language (svPPA: "semantic dementia")

- Fluent, grammatical speech that empties of content; severe naming difficulty; word-comprehension loss (asked to define "camel": "a thing... for... going").
- Loss extends beyond words: faces of famous people, tools, animals lose their meaning.
- **Surface dyslexia**, reading irregular words by sound ("colonel" read as "kolonel"), a beloved exam pearl.
- Behavioural quirks of the temporal-right-dominant type: emotional coldness, rigid sweet food preferences, bizarre narrow collections (bottle caps, one celebrity's paraphernalia).

### Nonfluent/agrammatic variant (nfvPPA)

- Effortful, "telegraphic" speech; grammar collapses while word-meaning survives; grope-for-the-word quality (apraxia of speech).
- Often with asymmetric parkinsonism or, over years, progression to a broader four-limb syndrome (overlap with CBS/PSP phenotypes; see exam lens).

### Course and the MND shadow

- bvFTD: relentless progression of the behaviours above into muteness and dependency; early years are the period of maximal family damage.
- Language variants: slower, years of workable communication with strategy; longer survival.
- **FTD–MND:** at any point, ask at every visit, falling objects, weak grip, slurring, breathlessness, fasciculations (visible muscle twitching); their arrival reframes prognosis.

## 8. Diagnosis

### The logic of the criteria (paraphrased)

The current consensus criteria for behavioural-variant FTD (Rascovsky et al., 2011; mirrored inside ICD-11 and DSM-5's frontotemporal neurocognitive disorder language) reason like this: a **deterioration in behaviour and/or cognition** by history and testing, plus three or more of six characteristic clusters, disinhibition; apathy; loss of empathy; perseverative/compulsive behaviour; hyperorality; utilisation/executive pattern: with functional decline and preserved-other-explanations. *Possible* bvFTD is clinical; *probable* requires an imaging or genetic marker (frontal/anterior-temporal atrophy, hypometabolism, or a known pathogenic mutation); the old "Pick's disease" name belongs to post-mortem tau histology, not to bedside use.

### Workup

- **Collateral history is 80% of the diagnosis, and must be taken privately.** Ask the spouse/children for the *inventory of change*: manners, money, food, empathy, rituals, hygiene. Then compare with the patient's own account, which will often be "everything is fine, she exaggerates".
- **Bedside cognitive testing with frontal weighting:** simple memory screens miss it by design. Use tasks of inhibition/switching, abstraction (similarities), proverb-type interpretation, go/no-go-type patterns, Luria-type sequences; verbal fluency under timed conditions (category > letter dissociation in semantic variant). The paradox to remember: **a normal MMSE-style score never excludes FTD.**
- **Neuropsychological assessment** where available: the frontal-executive and language profile against preserved memory storage.
- **MRI brain:** frontal and/or anterior temporal atrophy, often strikingly asymmetric; the "knife-blade" anterior temporal atrophy of semantic variant. Early disease can look near-normal, then **FDG-PET** (frontal/anterior-temporal hypometabolism out of proportion) earns its place, especially in private Indian practice where MRI-normal is read as brain-normal by families and insurers alike.
- **Rule-out battery (the mimic killers):** thyroid and B12 as in any dementia; **HIV and syphilis serology** in younger behaviour-change (cheap, treatable, classic misses); MRI for masses and hydrocephalus; consider **neurosyphilis and autoimmune encephalitis in any rapid or atypical course**; sleep, alcohol and substance history; and a medication review (steroids, benzodiazepines and dopamine drugs can all caricature disinhibition).
- **EMG/clinical surveillance for MND** when wasting, fasciculations or slurring join the picture, coordinate with neurology early.
- **Genetic testing pathway:** offer counselling-driven testing when onset is early and the family tree is loaded (dementia, MND, early psychosis). In the Indian setting, map the tree on paper in clinic first; reserve mutation testing for tertiary genetic services, and never test an unaffected relative without formal pre-test counselling.

### The differential: psychiatry's department

| It looks like | But smells like FTD because |
|---------------|------------------------------|
| Depression | No sadness, no guilt, no sleep/appetite *loss*; *weight gain*; empathy absent rather than painful; antidepressants fail |
| Mania / bipolar | No psychomotor acceleration, no decreased sleep, no grandiose plans, just released behaviour at a flat, unrewarded pitch |
| OCD | Rituals unresisted, unsought, unchanged by challenge; no anxiety relief-seeking; adult onset |
| Marital infidelity / "character" | Progression, disinhibition *across* all social settings (not just home), other signs in the six-cluster inventory |
| Late-onset schizophrenia / delusional disorder | Content is absent/bizarre-minimal; behaviour is release-pattern; MMSE-normal but life-collapsed |
| Alcohol/substance personality change | Collateral confirms zero intake: always verify before blaming "he must be drinking" |
| Alzheimer's early-onset | Memory-first phenotype; atrophy posterior-parietal pattern; disorientation early |

## 9. Management

### (a) The foundation: explain the conductor, remove the blame

The first consultation task is to relabel: *the person has not turned bad; the front of the brain that policed behaviour is ill.* This single reframe prevents divorce proceedings, elder-abuse escalation and family excommunication. Written in the file, repeated at each visit, given to school-college-age children in words they can repeat.

### (b) Behavioural management (the real pharmacology of bvFTD)

- **Structure beats willpower:** fixed daily routine with photographs and written schedules; the FTD brain runs on rails.
- **Environment engineering:** money out of reach (joint accounts, spending limits, UPI locks; a very Indian necessity now that phones are wallets); lock the kitchen sweets; wardrobe rotation to prevent wearing the same clothes; hygiene scheduled into the routine.
- **Reduce audience and stimulation** for disinhibited behaviour; redirect, never confront or argue (the frontal lobes that could accept an argument are the ones that are ill).
- **Supervise food**: hyperorality with weight gain needs it as surely as anorexia does.
- Manage wandering with ID cards and locked doors early, a critical Indian safety step given traffic and open terraces.
- **Caregiver architecture:** bvFTD caregivers carry higher distress than Alzheimer's caregivers (behaviour, not dependency, is the burden); they need respite, siblings' rosters, and permission to be disliked by the patient without guilt.

### (c) Medicines: modest, targeted, honest

- **SSRIs** (sertraline, escitalopram) are the pragmatic first line for disinhibition, irritability and compulsive-type behaviours, modest evidence, reasonable tolerability; titrate patiently.
- **Trazodone**: a recognised second-line for irritability and agitation; watch orthostasis.
- Low-dose **quetiapine** for dangerous agitation only, fixed review dates; and **never levodopa-like expectations**, there is no cognition drug for FTD: acetylcholinesterase inhibitors have **no evidence of benefit here** (cholinergic systems are relatively spared), and some patients worsen on them, a common enough Indian prescribing reflex worth unlearning.
- Treat pain, constipation, sleep and hearing, the same trigger-first logic as all dementias (see the Alzheimer's note; one fact, one home).

*India note: the affordable essentials are sertraline (₹50–150/month generic, approx 2026), trazodone where available, and the free infrastructure, routine, locks, ID card, family roster. The MRI and FDG-PET costs (₹2,000–8,000 and ₹15,000–30,000 in metros, approx, varying) are the diagnostic expense; both are often avoidable or deferrable in textbook-clear behavioural cases with a family history, but decisive in courtship-of-mimics cases.*

### (d) Language variants: the speech therapist's hour

- Early referral to speech-language therapy: pacing boards, sentence starters, word books; family training in **yes/no scaffolding** and patience-centred turn-taking.
- Semantic-variant families build a **visual dictionary** (labelled photo albums of relatives, foods, objects) to route around the melted word-meanings.
- Bank the voice early if MND overlap is suspected (recording speech before it changes further is emotionally priceless later).

### (e) Legal, financial and vocational planning: the early-and-urgent work

While comprehension remains: employment exit strategy (medical board, disability documentation: India's disability certification pathway covers dementia), property and nomination planning, guardianship preparedness under the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 framework, and honest conversations with adult children. **This is more urgent in FTD than in any other dementia** because onset is midlife: school fees, loans and the earning decade are all still in play.

### (f) The MND overlap

- Regular screening questions at every visit (dropped objects, grip, slurring, breathlessness, fasciculations).
- When present: MND clinic co-management, swallow and breathing surveillance, seating/positioning equipment, early palliative-care conversation; caregiver counselling shifts toward prognosis pacing.

## 10. Indian practice

- **Who actually makes the diagnosis:** usually the psychiatrist (after failed antidepressant trials), occasionally the neurologist (after an EMG or a seizure), rarely the family physician. Psychiatrists seeing midlife "personality change" should hold FTD in the front of the differential; this single habit is the highest-yield change Indian psychiatry could make to young-onset dementia.
- **Family dynamics are the terrain:** the wife is usually blamed (in-law narratives of "she changed him"), the husband's spending triggers property disputes, disinhibition at family functions becomes community gossip before it becomes a consultation. Early family psychoeducation, with the conductor explanation, is the treatment's spine.
- **Certification and benefits:** disability certification for dementia (including young-onset) exists through the government pathway; NIMHANS and state medical boards process them; worth initiating early because processing takes months.
- **Caregiving reality:** middle-aged caregivers with jobs and school-going children; day-care for young-onset behaviour disturbance barely exists; paid male attendants are hard to find for hygienic care; respite is the scarcest resource; the treating team should prescribe it in words.
- **Genetic counselling:** almost entirely metro-private or tertiary academic; the practical Indian package is: draw the three-generation tree in the file, mark early dementia / MND / "psychiatric" deaths, counsel the family about the pattern, and refer only the loaded trees for mutation testing, with pre-test counselling, never direct-to-test.
- **What to tell the employer, in one line:** "This is a neurodegenerative illness like any other brain disease, not misconduct"; a letter from the treating doctor prevents termination-for-cause and unlocks medical benefits.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: the manager the family nearly divorced.** A 54-year-old bank branch manager developed, over three years: public teasing of a junior colleague, ₹6 lakh of online purchases on a whim, eating from colleagues' tiffins, two traffic challans for urinating by the roadside, and total silence about his mother's hospitalisation. Two psychiatrists had treated depression (two failed SSRI courses); the marriage was headed to mediation. On examination: memory intact (he recalled the entire interview outline later), naming fluent; frontal tasks collapsed: he sorted cards by one rule and could not switch, interpreted the proverb "people in glass houses..." literally, and imitated the examiner's hand-movements without being asked (utilisation/imitation pattern). MRI: right-frontal-predominant atrophy. The family conference relabelled the three years; spending control, routine and UPI limits were installed; sertraline softened the disinhibition; his wife joined a caregiver group. He remained at home for six more years, and the divorce mediation was closed.

*Teaching points: midlife "character change" across all social settings + intact memory = frontal until proven otherwise; two failed antidepressant trials in "depression without sadness" is a diagnostic alarm, not a treatment escalation; utilisation behaviour and proverb-literalism are 2-minute frontal exams; the consultation's real deliverable was the relabelling.*

**Case 2: the melting dictionary.** A 61-year-old retired schoolteacher's daughter noticed she was calling objects "that thing": the pressure cooker, the neem tree, her own spectacles. Speech stayed fluent and grammatical; she could still repeat long sentences verbatim. Two years on, she could not say what a "camel" or a "temple bell" was, though she used them appropriately when handed pictures; she had begun collecting biscuit wrappers in a locked cupboard; sweet craving drove 8 kg of weight gain. Naming, comprehension-of-low-frequency-words, and famous-face tasks failed; MRI showed knife-blade anterior temporal atrophy, left worse than right. Semantic-variant FTD was diagnosed; the family built a labelled photo dictionary; the speech therapist trained yes/no scaffolding; she read aloud fluently, but stumbled on irregular words read by sound. Four years later she still pottered in her kitchen, fully ambulant, conversation reduced but warm.

*Teaching points: fluent empty speech + lost word meanings + intact repetition = the semantic-variant triad; surface dyslexia (colonel read as "kolonel") is the pearl; collections and sweet craving are behavioural companions of the temporal-dominant type; language variants run slower courses than the behavioural variant; pacing the family honestly matters.*

## 13. FAQ

**"He has become a bad person, is that a disease?"**
Yes, that is the illness. The front of the brain that polices manners, spending and restraint is shrinking; the behaviours are released, not chosen. It is as physical as a paralysed leg; the paralysed part here is self-control.

**"Is this because of his job stress / her menopause / the family problems?"**
Stress does not cause this. Stress may have been the family's explanation because the real cause was invisible on scans that nobody ordered. Removing the blame is part of the treatment.

**"But his memory is perfect! How is it dementia?"**
Dementia means loss of thinking ability that changes daily life, and planning, judgement, self-restraint and language are thinking abilities. This disease spares memory first and attacks them first.

**"Will the memory medicines help?"**
Honestly, no: the chemical they target is not the one that is deficient here. What helps is structure, safety, family understanding, and targeted medicines for specific behaviours.

**"Is it hereditary? Our children are asking."**
In some families yes, in most no. The signal to act on: relatives with similar early dementia, motor neuron disease, or young-onset "psychiatric" illness. If the family tree looks loaded, we map it on paper and, where appropriate, arrange formal genetic counselling and testing, never a direct home test.

**"The eating worries us: he grabs food everywhere."**
That symptom (food craving and grabbing, usually sweets) is typical of this disease. Kitchen arrangements, scheduled portions and pre-portioned sweets work better than confrontation; expect weight gain, not loss.

**"Can she work? The office is initiating disciplinary action."**
The behaviours are symptoms of brain illness. A doctor's letter stating the diagnosis converts a disciplinary case into a medical one, medical exit, leave benefits, and dignity. Do this early, while the paperwork is still in the office's hands.

**"What is the life expectancy?"**
The honest range is broad: many years with gradually increasing dependency, often seven to thirteen years; longer in the language-dominant forms; much shorter if muscle disease (ALS) joins, which we monitor for at every visit.

**"We have small children and he is behaving inappropriately at home."**
Protect first, explain second: supervision plans for shared spaces, simple honest words for the children ("the front of his brain is ill, like a leg that cannot walk"), and scheduled one-to-one time so affection survives the behaviour.

**"Is there any surgery or new medicine?"**
Not yet. Trials exist, gene-targeted ones for the familial forms included, but nothing proven for clinic use today. Our job is to protect the family's functioning and the patient's dignity while the science matures.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **The six bvFTD clusters to recite:** disinhibition / apathy / empathy loss / perseveration-compulsion / hyperorality / executive decline, own them as a checklist, not a paragraph.
- **Highest-yield single fact:** FTD = personality/behaviour/language first, memory preserved, onset 45–65, the commonest young-onset dementia, and a normal memory screen never excludes it.
- **Classic traps:** (1) treating bvFTD apathy as depression through two antidepressant trials; (2) diagnosing mania without decreased-sleep/psychomotor-quickening; (3) believing a normal MRI with a collapsing life excludes disease (order FDG-PET or repeat MRI in a year); (4) prescribing cholinesterase inhibitors by reflex; (5) missing treatable mimics, neurosyphilis, HIV, autoimmune encephalitis, chronic subdural, hypomania from steroids/dopamine drugs.
- **Viva favourites:** pick's disease nomenclature (now a pathological, not clinical, label); the FTD–MND–ALS triangle; C9orf72; semantic vs nonfluent vs logopenic PPA (logopenic points to Alzheimer's, the classic "third variant" trick question); surface dyslexia; utilisation and imitation behaviour; Luria sequences; the asymmetric atrophy sign; why caregiver burden exceeds Alzheimer's at equal dependency.
- **Neurology crossover pearls:** PSP (vertical gaze palsy, axial rigidity) and corticobasal syndrome (alien limb, apraxia) are 4-repeat-tau cousins of FTD; "FTD spectrum" is a favourite short note.
- **Indian exam corner:** young-onset dementia differential (FTD vs early Alzheimer's vs neurosyphilis vs HIV vs hypothyroid psychosis vs alcohol); disability certification in dementia; MHA 2017 supported decision-making; the "midlife personality change" case vignette as a long-question favourite.
- **Rehearse one line:** "In midlife personality change, the memory test is irrelevant and the spouse's history is the scan."

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. A 56-year-old presents with 3 years of disinhibition, sweet-food craving with weight gain, apathy and preserved memory recall. The most likely diagnosis is:**
A. Major depression
B. Alzheimer's disease
C. Behavioural-variant frontotemporal dementia
D. Bipolar mania

**Answer: C.** The six-cluster pattern with weight *gain* and intact memory is the bvFTD signature; depression and mania both fail key gates (sadness; sleeplessness and quickening).

**2. Fluent speech with emptied word-meanings, intact repetition, and reading "colonel" as "kolonel" indicates:**
A. Nonfluent/agrammatic variant
B. Logopenic variant

**3. The gene causing both familial FTD and ALS in the same kindred is:**
A. MAPT
B. C9orf72
C. GRN
D. APP

**Answer: B.** The C9orf72 repeat expansion produces FTD, ALS, or both within one family, the core of the FTD–MND overlap.

**4. In confirmed FTD, which prescription reflex should be avoided?**
A. Sertraline for compulsive behaviours
B. Speech-language therapy referral
C. Donepezil for cognition
D. Trazodone for irritability

**Answer: C.** Cholinesterase inhibitors lack benefit in FTD (cholinergic systems are relatively spared) and can worsen behaviour; the SSRI, trazodone and therapy options all have pragmatic roles.

**5. At every FTD visit, the four screening questions for motor neuron disease overlap are about:**
A. Sleep, appetite, mood, and pain
B. Dropped objects, grip weakness, slurred speech, and breathlessness
C. Memory, orientation, spelling, and drawing
D. Blood pressure, sugar, lipids, and weight

**Answer: B.** Hand function, dysarthria and respiratory symptoms screen the TDP-43 spread into motor neurons; the FTD–MND subgroup carries the shortest survival.

**6. The family of a 50-year-old with bvFTD asks what actually helps. The best-supported core of management is:**
A. A cholinesterase inhibitor plus donepezil
B. Structure, safety engineering, caregiver support, and targeted SSRIs
C. High-dose antipsychotics from the start
D. No intervention changes anything

**Answer: B.** With no disease-modifying therapy, routine, environmental control, family education and modest pharmacology for specific behaviours are the evidence-shaped package.
