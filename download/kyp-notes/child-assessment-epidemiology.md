# Child Psychiatric Assessment & Epidemiology — Counting, Understanding and Interviewing Young Minds

## 1. In one line + overview

**About one child in ten has a psychiatric disorder, half of all lifetime mental illness begins by age 12, and finding it depends on an assessment that interviews the child, the parents, the school, and the family context as one system.**

Two Oxford chapters make up this note. The epidemiology chapter (Costello & Angold) delivers the numbers that justify child psychiatry's existence: national survey prevalence, the age-of-onset cascade, and, its unique gift, a honest anatomy of *why prevalence figures differ wildly between studies* (time frames, informants, impairment criteria), which protects you from being fooled by the next questionnaire-based survey. The assessment chapter (Bostic & Martin) teaches the craft: adults bring children for their own reasons; children rarely volunteer to change; the interview must be developmentally tuned and multi-informant; and the mental state examination, history structure and laboratory workup of child psychiatry differ from adult medicine in systematic, learnable ways. Together they answer two questions every clinician needs: *how common is this?* and *how do I find it?*

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Quote the UK national survey figures (overall ~9.5%, by disorder, by sex, incidence and persistence) and the adult-onset cascade (half by 12, three-quarters by 24).
- Explain the four study-design factors that make prevalence estimates vary: time frame, informants, age-sex structure, and impairment criteria, with the anxiety 39.5%-to-3.2% example.
- Describe the distinctive features of child psychiatric assessment: who initiates referral, children's motivation, multiple informants, adult-defined criteria.
- Structure the clinical interview: reason for referral, problem history, comorbidity, substance use, past treatment, developmental history (seven streams), family and medical history, strengths, media diet.
- Conduct a developmentally adjusted child mental state examination.
- List the laboratory tests worth considering by presentation, and quote the honest yield figures (~1% change the working diagnosis).
- Define developmental epidemiology and its growth directions (longitudinal, genetic, life-course, intergenerational, prevention science).

## 4. The core ideas

### 4.1 The epidemiology: how many, and why the numbers move

**The burden argument.** The National Comorbidity Survey Replication (US adults) found that of the 46.4% reporting a lifetime disorder, **half reported onset by age 12 and three-quarters by age 24**, and forgetting of early episodes makes true childhood onset probably commoner still. Child psychiatry is not a small subspecialty at the margin of adult psychiatry; it is where most psychiatric illness begins.

**The UK national survey** (Office for National Statistics; 10,438 children aged 5–15 across England, Scotland and Wales; parent and child interviewed with the DAWBA, a lay interview reviewed by clinicians for best-estimate diagnosis): **9.5% had a psychiatric disorder**, 11.2% of 11–15-year-olds versus 8.2% of 5–10-year-olds; boys 11.4% versus girls 7.6%. The breakdown: **conduct disorders 5.3%** (the largest group), **anxiety 3.8%**, hyperkinetic disorders 1.4%, depression 0.9% (rare in both sexes and all ages). Over 3 years, 7% of previously unaffected children developed a disorder; persistence was far higher for behavioural disorders (43%) than emotional disorders (~one in four). A Brazilian study using the same interview but DSM-IV found 12.7% overall with the same internal ordering; other world studies often report around 20%; at the high end.

**Why the numbers move (the chapter's masterclass).** Four design choices, not four different realities:
1. **Time frame**: "now" vs "past 3/6/12 months" vs "ever" changes counts; recall reliability collapses after ~3 months (use 3-month windows for trustworthy estimates). In comparisons of child depression studies, the time frame explained more variance than taxonomy, instrument or cohort.
2. **Informants**: children and parents disagree massively, of children with a diagnosis by child interview, only 26% also had one by parent interview (and vice versa 22%); just 13.5% of cases were reported by both (though the agreement is statistically significant). Parents miss drug use; young children can't rate their own hyperactivity; teachers miss depression. The clinical rule (and most instruments' rule): count a symptom if *any* informant reports it.
3. **Age and sex**: prevalence of *any* disorder traces a U-shape, enuresis, ADHD and separation anxiety fade with age while substance misuse and depression rise, creating a trough around 11–14; boys dominate developmental disorders, enuresis, ADHD and later drug abuse; girls overtake in adolescent depression.
4. **Impairment**: applying dysfunction criteria cuts the count dramatically, anxiety diagnosis rates fell from 39.5% (criteria only) to 3.2% (criteria plus both impairment measures at their most stringent) in the MECA data. Symptoms without impairment are not disorders.

**Where the field is going, developmental epidemiology** (Kellam's term): from counting cases to tracing how risk exposure and vulnerability change across the life course. Five growth directions: longitudinal studies (continuity from temperament to adult disorder); **genetic epidemiology** (two revolutions: psychiatric interviews in genetically informative designs, and molecular genetics in epidemiological samples enabling gene-environment questions at specific developmental stages); **life-course epidemiology** (the "embodiment" of social conditions into biology; health inequalities work that reshapes policy); intergenerational epidemiology; and prevention science.

### 4.2 The assessment: interviewing a system, not a child

**Why child assessment is structurally different.** (1) **Adults initiate** the evaluation, with their own motivations, sometimes seeking to alter the child to fit the adult's expectations or teaching style. The referral question ("who wants what changed, and why?") is itself clinical data. (2) **Children may not want to change**; they attribute problems to others; ask what *the child* wishes were different. (3) **Young children distrust unfamiliar adults; adolescents see you as another adult with judgements**; alliances must be built with child, parents, and outside parties (school, paediatrician) simultaneously; a breach in any one impedes treatment. (4) **DSM criteria were written on adults**; developmental differences in symptom expression must be translated (irritability instead of sadness, somatic complaints instead of verbalised anxiety). (5) **Multiple informants** are not optional (see the 13.5% figure above).

**The interview content** (the full paediatric psychiatric history):
- **Reason for referral**, who initiated it, their motivations, the changes each party seeks; expectations collide (school wants parental discipline changed; parents want school services) and must be reconciled.
- **History of the problem**: evolution, context, frequency/intensity trajectory; the *function* of problem behaviours including secondary gains (tantrums that abolish chores); single-domain vs pervasive impairment.
- **Past problems, comorbidity** (paediatric bipolar commonly preceded by ADHD), **substance use** (self-medication is common; ask what substances relieve), **previous treatments** (what was tolerated, what worked).
- **Developmental history, seven streams**: (a) sleep/eating/toileting regulation (sudden loss of skills flags emotional events; hunger and obesity both raise psychopathology risk); (b) psychomotor (gross vs fine motor may diverge; sports performance as a window); (c) cognitive (speech, reading, writing, maths progression, global vs specific difficulties); (d) interpersonal (stability of relationships, number of friends, shared activities); (e) emotional development and temperament (mood recognition, self-soothing, past suicidality, fears); (f) moral development (conscience too lax or too harsh; ability to acknowledge mistakes); (g) **trauma**: actual events and events *perceived* as traumatic, disclosures and adult reactions. Plus harmful behaviour toward self or others (head-banging → sensory disturbance; death talk → suicidality; cruelty → monitoring needs).
- **Family history** (few disorders are purely genetic, address parental fear about siblings; divorce and separation effects surfacing years later; adoption handled tactfully), **medical history** (pregnancy, birth, hospitalisations; emergency visits illuminate fears and parental protectiveness; allergies and medication responses *including naturopathic and homeopathic agents*, an Indian-consultation essential).
- **Strengths and weaknesses**: interests and talents as connection points for therapy; **the media diet** (what media, how much, what consequences; parental limit-setting).

**The child mental state examination**, the adult categories, recalibrated: appearance (self-care as an index of parental attentiveness too), manner of relating to clinician and parents (separation ease, guardedness, eagerness to please), activity level, speech (fluency, prosody), current affect and persisting mood (with age-appropriate expectations, giggling about a sibling's illness is data), coping and affect regulation, orientation (time sense develops with age), attention and cognition, memory, intelligence/fund of knowledge calibrated to age, judgement (asked after rapport, the envelope-by-the-mailbox questions), insight, thought process and content (magical thinking and night fears are often age-appropriate; auditory hallucinations suggest psychosis > PTSD > organic; obsessions are ego-dystonic, delusions ego-syntonic).

**Interview technique by age.** See parent and child together first; transitional objects ease separation; the child's beliefs about what adults want from the interview reveal perspective-taking. **Adolescents fear the parent will "skew" the interview first**, clarify objectives briefly together, then meet the adolescent alone at length; reduce resistance by leading with interests, strengths, music. **Parent interview technique** (its own craft): open-ended narrative before narrow fills; find cross-setting patterns (de-parentalises the problem); acknowledge good intentions gone awry (a harsh response usually masks fear about the child's future). **Confidentiality** must be made explicit; what will be shared with whom, agreed in advance. **Conclude collaboratively**: the child's curiosity about what you will say is an engagement opportunity.

**Investigations.** Few definitive tests exist in child psychiatry: laboratory results **change the working diagnosis in ~1% of cases; yield is under 5% without supportive physical findings**. Order by presentation: chromosomal testing (intellectual disability/PDD), Wood's lamp (neurocutaneous), thyroid (mood), monospot, Lyme titre, CBC and chemistry, **lead level** (a classic, eminently preventable cause), ASO/anti-DNase B titres (OCD/tics with infectious onset), urine drug screen, CSF and neuroimaging and EEG where the picture demands. Neuropsychological testing clarifies subtle processing difficulties: interpret cautiously with the tester's input. PET/SPECT/fMRI remain research tools in child psychiatry.

## 5. Why it matters clinically

- **The 10% figure funds services.** Every Indian district school of 1,000 children contains roughly 100 children with diagnosable disorder, the child guidance infrastructure, school counselling and DMHP child components are sized against numbers like these.
- **Impairment is the diagnosis's spine.** Symptoms without dysfunction are not disorder; the anxiety 39.5%→3.2% collapse is the clearest lesson in psychiatric gatekeeping, protecting clinics from being flooded by questionnaire positives.
- **Multi-informant assessment is forced by mathematics**, not preference: relying on a single informant (child or parent) misses most cases; the any-informant rule is how both research instruments and clinical teams work.
- **The referral question is diagnostic.** "Who wants what changed?" unmasks the adjustment disorder in the parent, the school's discipline problem, or the genuine child disorder faster than any rating scale.
- **Screening questions cost 30 seconds**: difficulty getting/staying asleep, daytime sleepiness, abnormal night-time behaviours, then a full sleep history (the child-sleep note details why).

## 6. India lens

- **No national DAWBA-style survey exists for India**; the existing Indian studies are mostly school-based, instrument-varying, and demonstrate exactly the methodological traps this chapter teaches (time-frame and informant differences). The honest position: apply the ~10% international anchor with local humility, and teach the four design factors so Indian surveys are read critically.
- **The gatekeeper structure is Indian too, with local actors**: parents, teachers, paediatricians, and, distinctively, grandparents, tuition teachers, family physicians and sometimes faith healers decide whether and where a child reaches care. Mapping *who* initiated the referral (and what they expect; often "make him study") is the first Indian assessment task.
- **Informant richness is native to Indian practice**: the joint family supplies multiple adult observers; use them deliberately, in separate turns if needed, to build the cross-setting picture Western assessors struggle to assemble.
- **Language**: assess in the child's mother tongue wherever possible; language switching between home and school can masquerade as (or mask) developmental language disorder.
- **The laboratory restraint lesson** transfers directly: in an Indian setting where families expect "tests", the honest ~1% yield figure supports a targeted, history-driven approach, but do not skip lead, thyroid and iron studies in suggestive presentations, since these are common and treatable in Indian children.
- **Developmental epidemiology's future in India**: NFHS-based and school-based cohorts, Anganwadi surveillance, and the RPwD Act's educational entitlements all depend on the counting discipline this chapter teaches.

**FAQ**
*"Is my child's problem common?"*: Roughly one child in ten has a psychiatric disorder at any time; conduct-type and anxiety problems lead; depression is genuinely rare before adolescence. You are not alone, and this is a mapped territory.
*"Why does the doctor want to meet my child's teacher?"*: Because parents and children each see a different half of the picture: they agree on only a minority of cases. The teacher adds the school half.
*"The questionnaire said my child has a disorder, is that a diagnosis?"*: No. Questionnaire cut-offs define "caseness" arbitrarily; diagnosis requires symptoms *plus* impairment *plus* clinical judgement. Symptom counts alone can label 40% of children anxious; proper criteria bring that to ~3%.
*"Why ask about my pregnancy and my child's first years? That's ancient history."*: Because development is the child's biography: regulation, motor, cognitive, social, emotional and moral streams each leave a trace, and their pattern separates developmental from reactive problems.
*"Do we need blood tests and scans?"*: Rarely: tests change the diagnosis in about 1% of children. They are ordered to answer specific questions raised by the history and examination, not as a routine sweep.
*"Is asking about trauma necessary? Won't it upset things?"*: Gently, yes: both documented events and events the child *experienced* as traumatic shape behaviour and relationships. The disclosure story (who was told, how adults reacted) is part of the clinical picture.

**Self-test MCQs**

**Q1.** In the UK national survey of 5–15-year-olds, which ordering of disorder prevalence is correct?
- (a) Depression > anxiety > conduct > hyperkinetic
- (b) Conduct > anxiety > hyperkinetic > depression
- (c) Hyperkinetic > conduct > depression > anxiety
- (d) Anxiety > depression > conduct > hyperkinetic

**Answer: (b).** Conduct disorders (5.3%) lead, anxiety (3.8%) follows, hyperkinetic (1.4%) and depression (0.9%) are comparatively rare in childhood.

**Q2.** Applying both impairment criteria at their most stringent level to MECA anxiety data reduced the prevalence from 39.5% to:
- (a) 20.5%
- (b) 13.0%
- (c) 3.2%
- (d) 9.6%

**Answer: (c).** Dysfunction criteria, not symptom counts, define disorder, the single most important lesson in interpreting screening surveys.

**Q3.** Of children receiving a diagnosis by child interview, approximately what proportion also receives one by parent interview?
- (a) Nearly all (~95%)
- (b) About half
- (c) About a quarter (26%)
- (d) None: the interviews are orthogonal

**Answer: (c).** Parent-child agreement is statistically significant but clinically modest; both informants are essential and the any-informant rule prevails.

**Q4.** According to NCS-R data cited in the chapter, by what age has half of lifetime psychiatric disorder begun?
- (a) Age 6
- (b) Age 12
- (c) Age 18
- (d) Age 24

**Answer: (b).** Half by 12, three-quarters by 24, and forgetting of early episodes makes childhood onset probably commoner still.

**Q5.** Which statement about laboratory investigation in child psychiatric assessment is accurate?
- (a) Comprehensive routine panels are standard practice
- (b) Results change the working diagnosis in about 1% of cases; testing should be history-driven
- (c) PET and SPECT are first-line diagnostic tools
- (d) Yield exceeds 50% even without physical findings

**Answer: (b).** Few definitive tests exist; the yield without supportive physical findings is under 5%, order tests to answer questions the clinical assessment raises.

**Q6.** An adolescent worries that "my parents will get to you first and tell you their version." The assessment response recommended by the chapter is:
- (a) Interview only the parents to save time
- (b) Clarify objectives briefly with parent and adolescent together, then meet the adolescent alone at length
- (c) Refuse to see the parents at all
- (d) Let whoever arrives first give the history

**Answer: (b).** The structure directly addresses the adolescent's fear of a skewed interview while preserving the multi-informant base.
