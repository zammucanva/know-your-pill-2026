# Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)

## 1. In one line + overview

**PTSD is what happens when a terrifying memory refuses to become a memory; it stays alive, erupting in nightmares, flashbacks and body-level alarm, while the person rearranges their entire life to avoid the trigger.**

The event is over, the accident, the assault, the shelling, the fire. But inside the person it is not over: the brain stored the terror "hot", un-filed, on permanent loop. Sounds and smells drag them back in a heartbeat; sleep becomes a battlefield of nightmares; the world is scanned for danger that is no longer there; feelings shut down; and the person begins avoiding everything connected to the event, the road, the people, the conversations, sometimes the whole personality they used to have. Two names matter beyond the classic picture: **complex PTSD** (ICD-11) for those who endured prolonged, repeated, inescapable trauma (captivity, chronic childhood abuse, trafficking) where the injury goes beyond fear into the very organisation of self and relationships; and **moral injury**, the wound of having done, witnessed or failed to prevent something that violated conscience, common in soldiers, disaster responders and physicians. PTSD is common (a meaningful share of trauma survivors), heavily comorbid with depression and alcohol use, and, the headline for patients and examiners alike, **treatable**: trauma-focused psychotherapy is among the most effective interventions in all of psychiatry.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Apply the DSM-5 architecture: trauma + four clusters + 1-month duration + impairment
- Describe each cluster in patient-recognisable language
- Diagnose complex PTSD (ICD-11) and distinguish it from borderline personality presentation
- Explain the neurobiology story: hot memory, broken filing, alarm miscalibration
- Deliver trauma-focused CBT, prolonged exposure and EMDR as concepts a patient can consent to
- Use SSRI/venlafaxine evidence, prazosin for nightmares, and the benzodiazepine warning
- Screen the Indian trauma contexts: road injury, sexual violence, disasters, communal violence
- Address comorbidity: depression, alcohol, TBI overlap, suicidal risk
- Recognise PTSD in children and in first responders, including yourself

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Lifetime trauma exposure is extremely common (half to two-thirds of people in surveys), but PTSD is the exception, not the rule: conditional risk after trauma is roughly 5–10% overall, with strong gradients; interpersonal violence (sexual assault, combat, torture) produces rates several-fold higher than accidents or natural disasters. Lifetime prevalence ~3–8% depending on country and method; women's rates roughly double men's (both higher exposure to sexual violence and higher conditional risk). Mean delay to care is years; comorbidity is the norm, depression, substance use, anxiety disorders, and elevated suicide risk. Without treatment, a substantial share become chronic; spontaneous recovery, when it happens, usually occurs in the first year.

**India.** NMHS 2015–16 did not measure PTSD directly; exposure surveys and regional studies suggest high trauma load (road injuries, disasters, violence) with low recognition. Indian-specific patterns worth knowing: post-riot and communal-violence survivors presenting years later with somatic complaints rather than psychological words; disaster cohorts (super-cyclone, floods, tsunami-affected Tamil Nadu 2004, where the SCTIMST/Sneha mental-health responses became classic Indian literature); railway and bus accident survivors processed largely through compensation channels; and sexual-violence survivors whose PTSD presentation is often masked by family "protection" strategies that suppress disclosure. Combat PTSD in Indian forces remains under-measured and under-recognised, with stigma dominant in service culture; moral injury concepts apply strongly to counter-insurgency veterans.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**The trauma (required):** actual/threatened death, serious injury, sexual violation, direct, witnessed, close-person informed, or repeated extreme exposure (emergency crews, mortuary workers, police).

**Why one person and not another (post-trauma risk):**
- Biological: prior anxiety/depression, family psychiatric loading, head injury at the time (double role: injury + impaired extinction), pre-trauma small hippocampal volume (a vulnerability marker in twin studies; the horse partially precedes the cart)
- Psychological: peritraumatic panic and dissociation, catastrophic appraisals ("I should have stopped it; therefore I am worthless"), prior trauma accumulation (especially childhood), avoidant coping style
- Social: ongoing threat (abuser still present, communal tension), no support, blame responses from family, poverty and displacement preventing stabilisation
- Event-level: intent (human-caused > natural), duration, betrayal by trusted people, moral injury content

**Indian context:** the "second trauma" of the system (police stations, court adjournments, compensation fights, victim-blaming) maintains PTSD as reliably as the original event; document the medico-legal layer as part of the trauma history.

## 6. What happens in the brain

**Story 1: the filing failure.** Deep sleep and calm waking states normally let the hippocampus convert raw experience into narrative memory, filed, dated, past-tense. Trauma of sufficient intensity (with the amygdala screaming and stress hormones saturating) jams this filing: the memory stays in raw, sensory form (images, smells, body states) without a "this is over" stamp. Trivial sensory matches (a horn, kerosene, a sleeve of a shirt) re-activate it as NOW. Trauma-focused therapy is, in essence, re-running the memory under safe conditions until the brain finally files it: past tense, less charge.

**Story 2: the smoke alarm recalibrated.** The amygdala learns fear fast, and unlearns it slowly; the medial prefrontal cortex (the officer that says "false alarm, stand down") is under-recruited in PTSD. Result: the alarm rings for doorbells and backfires; the body stays braced (startle, insomnia, scanning); exhaustion and numbness follow. Medications dampen the alarm; therapy re-trains the officer.

**Story 3: the shattered map of the world.** PTSD is not only fear. Deep assumptions ("the world is mostly safe", "I am capable", "people are mostly decent") collapse in one afternoon. The negative-cognition cluster (guilt, alienation, foreshortened future) reflects the mind rebuilding a worldview that makes sense of what happened, often badly. Complex PTSD adds a deeper layer: when the violation happened in childhood, at the hands of caretakers, inescapably; the "self" that was supposed to be built on trust gets built on sand: chronic shame, storms of emotion, relationships that alternate between merging and flight.

## 7. Symptoms

### The four DSM-5 clusters (patient language)

**1. Intrusion (at least one):**
- Unwanted memories that barge in: at work, in the middle of a meal
- Nightmares (the event itself or variants where the rescue fails)
- Flashbacks: full or partial reliving, smells, sounds, body sensations; a veteran flat on the road at the sound of a tyre burst; a survivor who smells the smoke again
- Intense distress at reminders (the intersection, the hospital corridor, the colour of the vehicle)

**2. Avoidance (at least one):**
- Steering around the place, the street, the news, the festival where it happened
- Refusing to speak of it: changing the topic, leaving the room

**3. Negative alterations in cognition and mood (at least two):**
- Amnesia for chunks of the event
- Persistent self-blame or other-blame that will not update with facts ("if I hadn't asked him to drive that night")
- Detachment: the family notices "he is present but absent"
- Flat affect; no appetite for life's previous joys
- Foreshortened future: "what is the point of saving for the children's education: I won't see it"
- Alienation: "nobody who hasn't been through it can understand me"

**4. Hyperarousal/reactivity (at least two):**
- Sleep initiation and maintenance failure
- Irritability and anger outbursts (often the presenting complaint in Indian clinics; "he beats the children")
- Hypervigilance: checking locks, sitting facing the door, scanning the road
- Startle: exaggerated jump at dropped vessels
- Concentration failure

**Duration > 1 month + real impairment = the diagnosis.** With dissociative specifier: depersonalisation ("I watched myself from the ceiling") or derealisation ("the world went unreal, like watching an old film"). With delayed expression: apparent quiet for months, then eruption.

### Complex PTSD (ICD-11): the three core clusters plus self-organisation injury
- Re-experiencing, avoidance, and sense-of-current-threat (the "classic three")
- PLUS **affective dysregulation** (either explosive or shutdown states), **negative self-concept** ("I am ruined, filthy, worthless"), **disturbed relationships** (unable to trust, or clinging then cutting off)
- Grounded in prolonged, repeated trauma where escape was impossible

### In children
- Play re-enactment, regression (bedwetting returns), new separation panic, somatic complaints, aggression; teenagers may show conduct-level changes and risk-seeking. Ask through drawing, story stems, and the caregiver's timeline, not interrogation.

### First responders / healthcare workers (including you)
- Cumulative exposure counts; watch for the changes nobody reports: cynicism with patients, dropping hobbies, hypervigilance at home, drinking between shifts, moral injury after the case that went wrong despite everything.

## 8. Diagnosis

**The architecture (in own words):** a qualifying event; symptoms across all four clusters (1 intrusion, 1 avoidance, 2 mood/cognition, 2 arousal); at least one month; impairment; not better explained by substances, medication or another illness. ICD-11 offers a slightly leaner PTSD (the classic three clusters) plus a separate complex PTSD construct.

**The interview craft:**
- Take the trauma history LAST, not first, establish safety and rapport; the shortest trauma question in the clinic is the screening one ("in your life, have you ever experienced anything frightening or overwhelming that you still think about?")
- Do not demand the story to justify treatment; patients can be treated without a full narrative retelling to you
- Always screen: depression, alcohol/substances, suicidal ideation, head injury at the time, current ongoing threat (domestic violence changes the plan: safety first)
- Instruments by name: PCL-5 (PTSD Checklist) for screening/monitoring; CAPS-5 as the structured gold standard; ITQ (International Trauma Questionnaire) for ICD-11 complex PTSD; PHQ-9 + AUDIT-C alongside

**Workup:** thyroid and basic bloods if somatic fatigue dominates; head-injury history; sleep assessment (rule out OSA before blaming PTSD for all unrefreshing sleep); in veterans/police: hearing, pain, musculoskeletal issues, the physical load of vigilance.

### Differential table

| Looks like | Clue pointing elsewhere |
|---|---|
| Acute stress disorder | Symptoms < 1 month after the event |
| Adjustment disorder | Stressor not qualifying trauma-level; symptom pattern not the PTSD cluster architecture |
| Panic disorder | Attacks without trauma-locked triggers; fear of the panic itself |
| OCD | Intrusions are ego-dystonic themes (contamination, doubt), not memory replays; neutralising rituals, not avoidance of reminders |
| Borderline personality | Relational instability since adolescence, sensitivity to abandonment; complex PTSD has trauma-lock and shame core, considerable overlap, formulate carefully |
| Psychotic disorder | True hallucinations/delusions beyond flashback phenomena; flashbacks are recognised as memory by the person when calm |
| Depression with rumination | Guilt content tied to mood, no reminder-evoked intrusion cluster |
| TBI sequelae | Irritability and concentration deficits since injury without intrusion/avoidance clusters; the overlap case (TBI + PTSD) is common and both need treating |
| Substance withdrawal insomnia | Sleep tied to use pattern; autonomic picture |

## 9. Management

**The rule of order: safety → stabilisation → trauma processing → rebuilding.** Beginning trauma processing while the abuser still shares the house, or while the person drinks a bottle a night, fails predictably.

### Step 1: Safety and stabilisation
- Ongoing domestic/communal threat addressed first (safety planning, legal protection, relocation where possible; see Suicide & Self-Harm note for safety-plan structure)
- Sleep restoration (sleep hygiene, brief hypnotic bridge if needed), substance reduction plan, psychoeducation ("your symptoms make neurobiological sense") and the single most validating sentence: "you are having a normal reaction to an abnormal event that has outstayed its welcome"

### Step 2: Trauma-focused psychotherapy (FIRST-LINE, highest effect sizes)
- **Prolonged exposure (PE)**: graded real-world confrontation with avoided places + structured retelling of the memory in session, repeated until it loses charge. The principle: avoidance maintains the disorder; controlled re-encounter dissolves it. Typically 8–15 sessions.
- **Cognitive processing therapy (CPT)**: identifying and re-testing the "stuck points", the beliefs the trauma installed ("I am to blame", "nobody can be trusted"), through structured writing and Socratic work.
- **Trauma-focused CBT** generally (including for children and adolescents, with caregiver involvement)
- **EMDR (eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing)**: recalling the memory while tracking bilateral stimulation; multiple guideline endorsements as first-line despite ongoing theory debates; the memory-processing component does the work.
- **Brief eclectic psychotherapy and narrative exposure therapy (NET)**: the latter designed for multiple/continuous trauma and refugees, short-course, testimony-based, ideal structure for disaster and conflict settings in Indian conditions.

### Step 3: Pharmacotherapy (second-line or combined, or when psychotherapy unavailable/declined)
- **SSRIs**: sertraline and paroxetine carry the best evidence (some regulatory approvals); fluoxetine mixed; escitalopram/citalopram used in practice. Full trial = 8–12 weeks at adequate dose.
- **SNRI**: venlafaxine, positive trials, reasonable second choice.
- **Prazosin** for trauma nightmares: widely used (α-1 blockade at bedtime); evidence mixed by population: helpful in many civilians and veterans, with two large negative trials in chronic veteran cohorts. Honest counsel: "worth a proper trial, monitored."
- **Avoid benzodiazepines**: they numb arousal but impair the extinction learning psychotherapy depends on, and carry dependence risk in a population self-medicating already.
- Others: quetiapine and other antipsychotics are NOT routine (no solid base unless comorbid psychosis); topiramate/anti-adrenergics adjuvant data are modest.
- **Stellate ganglion block, MDMA-assisted psychotherapy, cannabis**: research headlines, not clinic standards as of this writing, say so plainly if asked.

### Step 4: Rebuilding
- Family psychoeducation (the symptom behaviours are the illness, not the character, transforms households)
- Graded return to avoided roles (driving, work, temple, market)
- Comorbidity treated in parallel (depression, alcohol; see those notes)
- Children: TF-CBT with caregiver module; school re-integration plan
- Moral injury: chaplaincy-equivalent, meaning-centred work, testimony/legacy projects; the layer medication does not reach

*India note: TF-CBT/EMDR competence concentrates in cities and tele-consultation; NET is deliberately designed for low-resource multi-trauma settings and has been used in Indian conflict/disaster cohorts. Drug costs (approx 2026): sertraline 100 mg ≈ ₹80–160/month, paroxetine ≈ ₹100–200/month, venlafaxine XR 75 mg ≈ ₹200–400/month, prazosin ≈ ₹50–120/month, EMDR/TFCBT private sessions ≈ ₹600–1,500/session; medical-college and DMHP psychologists treat at nominal or no charge. Yoga-based adjuncts (breathing, yoga nidra) have supportive small-trial evidence in Indian cohorts; use as adjuncts, not substitutes. Group work through survivor organisations (Sneha-model, 181-linked women's services) carries real value where individual therapy is scarce.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **The somatic front door.** Indian PTSD patients usually arrive complaining of body: "gas", headache, burning, trembling, "heat in the head" (see culture-bound somatic frames). The screening question belongs in every clinic with unexplained somatic symptoms following an accident/assault: "since that event, how is your sleep? Do dreams take you back there?"
- **Riot and communal-violence survivors.** Presentations years later with avoidance of "the other community", chronic distrust, and family-level transmission of vigilance to children. Treatment may need community framing, not individual framing alone; the clinician's neutrality is itself therapeutic.
- **Sexual violence.** One-stop centres, POCSO courts, and survivor-counselling programmes exist unevenly; the physician's tasks: confidentiality, trauma-informed exam language, and PTSD treatment referral that does not depend on the case's legal fate (treatment is not contingent on testimony).
- **Compensation and courts.** Repeated testimony re-activation is real: counsel lawyers about minimising repetitions, prepare survivors for the courtroom with a support person, and document PTSD for compensation/insurance claims factually, the certificate often funds the treatment.
- **Veterans and police.** Service-culture stigma ("weakness of the mind") delays presentation to the point of retirement; moral injury and alcohol dominate the picture. Direct language ("operational stress injury" reframing) lowers resistance; ex-servicemen clinics are an engagement point.
- **Disaster cohort follow-up.** After the first-month response (see Acute Stress note), the year-one task is identifying the non-resolvers: the fisherman who will not go to sea; the teacher who avoids the rebuilt school; the mother whose "weakness" is actually hyperarousal. DMHP lists from the acute phase make this follow-up possible, keep them.
- **Family as co-clinician.** Teach two jobs to one reliable family member: protect sleep (the household adjusts) and report the three earliest return-signs (nightmares back, avoidance creeping, temper shortening). Recovery at home is maintained at home.

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: the bus accident widow.** A 42-year-old widow from rural Maharashtra survived a bus plunge in which her daughter died beside her. Eleven months later she presented to the district hospital for "severe gas and weakness". She had stopped cooking (the stove's hiss resembled the crash sound), would not travel beyond the village, kept her remaining son home from the school 8 km away, and had re-told the event to nobody. Sleep: 3 hours. Screening at the somatic clinic flagged her; the PCL-5 was strongly positive. Management: psychoeducation in Marathi ("what happened in your nervous system"), sertraline 50→100 mg for the mood/nightmare load, sleep bridge, and NET-formatted narrative work through the district psychologist over nine sessions, with the sister present for two. By month four she was cooking, had resumed bus travel to the taluka with a companion, and the son returned to school. Teaching points: (1) somatic door, PTSD engine; (2) functioning goals (cooking, school bus) beat symptom scores for family-visible progress; (3) NET is structured for exactly this resource setting.

**Case 2: the constable who could not unsee it.** A 34-year-old police constable in Lucknow, eight years of riot duty and body-handling, was brought by his wife after he struck his son during a Diwali cracker barrage. Symptoms: nightly broken sleep, door-facing sitting, hypervigilance on duty (initially praised as "alertness"), heavy weekend drinking, emotional flatness at home, intrusive images of a specific child's body from a 2019 posting, and the belief "I should have pulled one more person out: I don't deserve leave". Diagnosis: PTSD with moral injury features, alcohol misuse, depression comorbidity. Management: staged, alcohol plan first (see Alcohol note), sertraline, prazosin trial for nightmares (partial response), then CPT addressing the stuck-points ("deserve", "should have"), with a supervisor-negotiated graded return and one session involving the wife on symptom education. At six months: no further violence, sleep 6 hours, drinking limited, still avoidant of fireworks season; openly discussed as the remaining work. Teaching points: (1) first-responder PTSD often presents as family violence + alcohol, not as fear; (2) guilt stuck-points respond to cognitive work, not reassurance; (3) involving the employer (supervisor) in graded duties is as therapeutic as any prescription.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way patients and families ask it

**"The accident was a year ago. Why am I worse now, not better?"** The memory never got filed: it stayed hot. Over months, avoidance (which protects you minute-to-minute) quietly cements the disorder, and exhaustion builds. The timing of your getting worse is common in PTSD, and it is treatable at any stage, however long it has been; decades-old cases still respond.

**"Will I have to describe everything that happened?"** Only as much as the treatment needs, and at a pace you control. The therapies that work best do involve approaching the memory rather than running from it, but it is graded, agreed, rehearsed and done with support, never ambush. Many patients say the retelling, done this way, was the moment the memory finally became past tense.

**"Why can't I just take tablets and be done?"** Tablets are genuinely useful, for sleep, mood, nightmares and the sharpest edges. But the core problem is a memory that needs re-filing, and only memory-work (the psychotherapies) does that. The best outcomes use both when needed; medicine alone usually manages rather than cures.

**"He never talks about it. Isn't silence handling it?"** Silence is avoidance, the disorder's maintenance kit. He is not coping; he is defending. When one person in the house is avoiding and the family walks on eggshells around the silence, the whole household organises itself around the PTSD. Treatment gives the family a way out of that arrangement.

**"Is she going mad?"** No. Flashbacks feel like madness from inside, but they are memories arriving with the "now" label stuck on. The rest (jumpiness, numbness, anger) is the alarm system staying switched on. This is a recognisable, nameable, treatable condition, not a broken mind.

**"The doctor gave an antidepressant, am I depressed then?"** Antidepressants are also PTSD medicines: SSRIs quieten the alarm and take the charge off the memories. You can have PTSD without being "depressed" and still benefit from these tablets for a year or more.

**"Why do I explode at my children when I never used to?"** Irritability and anger are arousal symptoms; the nervous system's brakes are worn thin by 24×7 vigilance. It is a symptom, not your character, and both the therapy and the medicine target it. The children need to hear this explanation too, in words their age can hold.

**"Can I drive again / go back to that road?"** Yes, as a plan, not a plunge. Graded return with company, short distances, off-peak hours, until the road becomes a road again. Avoidance keeps the road a war zone; exposure, done stepwise, returns it to tarmac.

**"We did the pooja, we did the temple. It helped for a week."** Faith addresses meaning and community: real ingredients of recovery. What it cannot do alone is re-file the memory that keeps firing. Keep the faith supports, and add the treatment; they are not competitors.

**"My husband is a police officer and says seeking help will end his career."** That fear is real in many services, and confidentiality norms in Indian forces have improved unevenly. Options that lower the stakes: teleconsultation, civilian private care, ex-servicemen clinics, and framing the contact as sleep and anger management first. The untreated version (alcohol, domestic strain, a crisis on duty) is far more career-ending than treatment is.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**Cluster mnemonic, "INCA":** Intrusion (≥1), Negative cognitions/mood (≥2), Avoidance (≥2)... careful: correct minimums are 1-2-1-2 (intrusion 1, avoidance 1, mood/cognition 2, arousal 2). Use "1-1-2-2" as the count sequence: one intrusion, one avoidance, two mood, two arousal, ≥ 1 month, plus impairment.

**High-yield facts:**
- DSM-5 moved PTSD OUT of anxiety disorders into "Trauma- and Stressor-Related Disorders", a favourite theory question
- Sertraline and paroxetine = best-evidence SSRIs; venlafaxine the SNRI option
- Benzodiazepines contraindicated-in-practice: impair extinction
- Prazosin: nightmares; mixed evidence (positive civilians/older veteran trials; two large negative chronic-veteran trials)
- EMDR and TF-CBT share first-line status in WHO/NICE/ISTSS-tier guidance
- ICD-11 splits classic PTSD (three clusters) from complex PTSD (three + self-organisation disturbances)
- Hippocampal volume: partly pre-trauma vulnerability (twin studies), classic "chicken or egg" question
- Comorbid TBI: both diagnoses stand; treat both

**Classic traps:**
1. Diagnosing PTSD < 1 month (that is ASD territory)
2. Requiring the full narrative from the patient as the price of treatment
3. Missing ongoing trauma (domestic violence) and starting exposure work into an unsafe household
4. Missing alcohol as both comorbidity and self-medication
5. Confusing OCD intrusions (ego-dystonic, themed) with PTSD flashbacks (memory content)
6. Treating only the depression and calling the avoidance "personality"
7. Forgetting the dissociative specifier when the patient describes cinema-unreality, and then over-diagnosing psychosis

**Indian exam corner:** "PTSD, diagnostic criteria and management", "complex PTSD vs borderline personality" (modern viva favourite), "mental-health response to disasters, Indian experience", "EMDR, what and evidence", " differentiate adjustment disorder, ASD and PTSD" (the trio question). The 2004 tsunami mental-health response is the most-cited Indian disaster-psychiatry vignette; NMHS 2015–16 gap data give the context marks.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. Minimum symptom counts across the four PTSD clusters in DSM-5 are:**
A. 2-1-2-3
B. 1-1-2-2 (intrusion, avoidance, mood/cognition, arousal)
C. 1-2-2-2
D. 2-2-2-2
**Answer: B.** One intrusion, one avoidance, two negative-mood/cognition, two arousal, for at least one month.

**2. First-line treatment for PTSD across major guidelines is:**
A. Benzodiazepines for arousal
B. Trauma-focused psychotherapy (PE/CPT/EMDR/NET)
C. Antipsychotic polypharmacy
D. Supportive counselling without trauma focus
**Answer: B.** Trauma-focused therapies carry the largest effects; benzodiazepines are specifically discouraged.

**3. Complex PTSD (ICD-11) adds which domain to the classic three clusters:**
A. Psychotic symptoms
B. Disturbances in self-organisation (affect regulation, negative self-concept, relationships)
C. Cognitive decline
D. Mania
**Answer: B.** The "three-plus-three" architecture of complex PTSD.

**4. A PTSD patient asks for "something to calm me down immediately, like a Valium". The evidence-based reason to decline long-term benzodiazepines:**
A. They cause weight gain
B. They impair the extinction learning on which recovery depends
C. They make flashbacks more vivid
D. They are unavailable in India
**Answer: B.** Benzodiazepines suppress arousal while blocking the learning therapy requires, plus dependence risk.

**5. Prazosin in PTSD is used for:**
A. Concentration
B. Trauma-related nightmares
C. Dissociation
D. Anger only
**Answer: B.** Bedtime α-1 blockade for nightmares: with mixed trial evidence by population, monitored trial is fair practice.

**6. A survivor of a 2019 factory fire presents in 2026 with somatic complaints, avoids fire rituals at festivals, and has 4-hour sleep. Best initial step:**
A. Explain that the fire was long ago and reassure
B. Screen with trauma-focused questions, assess PTSD formally, address sleep, offer trauma-focused care
C. Start long-term benzodiazepines for sleep
D. Refer for an MRI
**Answer: B.** The somatic front door with trauma screening; treatment follows the established order.

**7. The classic twin-study finding on hippocampal volume in PTSD:**
A. Volume shrinks only after PTSD develops
B. Smaller volume is partly a PRE-trauma vulnerability (seen in non-traumatised co-twins)
C. Hippocampus enlarges with PTSD
D. No relationship exists
**Answer: B.** Gilbertson et al.: the vulnerability-marker reading that exams love.
