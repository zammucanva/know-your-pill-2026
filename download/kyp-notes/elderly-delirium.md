# Delirium in the Elderly — The Quiet Emergency That Looks Like Dementia

## 1. In one line + overview

**In old age, delirium is usually *quiet*, a hypoactive, under-recognised emergency in which mild upsets derail a vulnerable brain, missed daily and mislabelled as dementia or depression.**

The general architecture of delirium is covered in the A1 note (`delirium.md`). This note is the Oxford old-age chapter's specific teaching: delirium results from an **interaction between individual vulnerability** (brain disease, sensory impairment, frailty) **and external insults** (illness, drugs, environment), and because both sides of that equation accumulate with age, delirium becomes primarily a geriatric disease with a transformed clinical face. In younger patients a major insult is needed and the disturbance is dramatic; in the vulnerable elderly, a urinary infection, a catheter, or a room change can suffice, and the picture is often a *reduced* conscious level, poor attention, incoherent speech and underactivity rather than agitation. The consequences: missed or misdiagnosed as dementia or depression; prolonged courses (one-third); increased mortality, longer stays, functional decline and nursing-home discharge; persistent cognitive decline afterwards; and, the chapter's hopeful counterweight, **multi-component prevention bundles demonstrably and cost-effectively reduce incidence**.

## 4. Epidemiology

Community prevalence rises with age to **14% in those aged 85 and over**. In medical and surgical inpatients: prevalence 10–30%, incidence 4–53% (methodological and population variation); similar rates in acute psychogeriatric admissions; consistently higher rates in **hip fracture** patients; nursing-home residents appear comparably affected. One-third of patients have prolonged or recurrent episodes. **India**: delirium's geriatric burden is rising with the demographic transition, but the diagnosis is rarest where the patients are commonest, busy Indian medical wards, where fluctuating drowsiness in an old person is routinely attributed to "weakness", "age", or pre-existing dementia; the Indian-specific task is the screening habit (see section 8) and the family's account of baseline.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**The model**: vulnerability × insult. Predisposing (patient-related): visual impairment, severity of illness, cognitive impairment (dementia above all), and a **blood urea nitrogen/creatinine ratio of 18 or more**. Precipitating (hospital- and treatment-related): use of **restraints**, **malnutrition**, **more than three medications**, **bladder catheterisation**, and the number of iatrogenic events; these risk factors are *multiplicative*.

**Common physical causes** (the chapter's table, the aetiological checklist): drugs (psychotropics, hypnotics, anticonvulsants, anticholinergics, dopamine agonists, analgesics, anaesthetics, alcohol withdrawal); infection (urinary tract, pneumonia, septicaemia, pressure sores, endocarditis, wound infection); metabolic/endocrine (electrolytes, diabetes, thyroid, renal and hepatic failure, hypothermia, malnutrition); cardiovascular (cardiac failure, myocardial infarction, vascular disease, anaemia/polycythaemia); respiratory (pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, effusion); intracranial (trauma, subdural haematoma, stroke, tumour, epilepsy); gastrointestinal (perforation, pancreatitis, cholecystitis, haemorrhage, constipation: the last an underrated Indian-ward classic).

**Drugs as the geriatric signature**: age changes distribution, metabolism and excretion unpredictably, toxicity at "therapeutic" doses. Tricyclics, thioridazine and benzhexol are the worst anticholinergics, but many routine elderly prescriptions carry anticholinergic activity (digoxin, prednisolone, cimetidine, ampicillin, warfarin), individually small, cumulatively significant on polypharmacy. **Alzheimer's patients are especially vulnerable** to anticholinergic delirium (already impaired cholinergic function). In a minority of vulnerable patients, purely environmental and psychological insults (sensory deprivation; stress responses via the HPA axis) suffice. Rule of thumb: **hyperactive delirium suggests infection or toxicity/withdrawal; hypoactive suggests metabolic causes**.

## 6. What happens in the brain: two short stories

**The seesaw tilts on its own story.** The general delirium model (A1 note) describes a brain whose attention systems fail acutely. The old-age chapter adds the arithmetic: vulnerability on one side, insult on the other. A young brain needs a massive insult to tip; the aged brain (cholinergic deficit, cerebrovascular burden, sensory deprivation) may tip with a whisper. This is why the same urinary infection that inconveniences a 40-year-old fells an 85-year-old, and why removing the catheter can be as therapeutic as any prescription.

**The quiet-dominance story.** The florid stereotype (agitation, hallucinations, pulling out lines) comes from centuries of observation of *younger* patients. In the elderly, the same electrophysiology presents as withdrawal: reduced conscious level, poor attention, poor contact, incoherent speech, reduced psychomotor activity, unawareness of surroundings, disorientation, poor memory. Overactivity, when it appears, is confined to purposeless behaviour such as pulling at bedclothes; violence is uncommon (elderly patients injure themselves more than others). The paradox of severity: hypoactive patients are *sicker* than hyperactive ones; partly because their delirium goes unrecognised and its causes untreated, partly because hypoactive causes (metabolic) are themselves graver.

## 7. Symptoms: and the classification honesty

Most elderly delirium is **quiet, hypoactive**: reduced conscious level, poor attention and contact, incoherent speech, psychomotor slowing, lack of awareness, poor orientation and memory. Hyperactive delirium exists but is attenuated, purposeless overactivity rather than violence. ICD-10 and DSM-IV agree on the four essentials: **disturbance of consciousness, disturbance of cognition, rapid onset/fluctuating course, and evidence of an external cause**, but none of these features is specific to delirium *versus dementia*, and current criteria predict outcome poorly. **Reversibility** is the most discriminating feature; problematic as a criterion because outcome is unknown at the outset. The classifications also miss **subsyndromal delirium**: partial and transitory disturbances common in the elderly, lying on a continuum with the full syndrome, sharing the same risk factors and the same increased mortality. Clinically: treat partial pictures with full seriousness.

## 8. Diagnosis

**Two stages: diagnose the delirium; then find the cause(s), usually plural.** Consider delirium whenever cognitive decline is *rapid* from whatever baseline, or any recognised sign is present. An informant history (relatives or ward staff) is essential for onset and course; in Indian practice the accompanying family member is the single most valuable diagnostic instrument. Routine screening: MMSE is not diagnostic but flags sudden decline; better, the Delirium Rating Scale, the Confusion Assessment Method, and the Delirium Symptom Interview. Screen proactively on the risk factors (visual impairment, illness severity, cognitive impairment, BUN/creatinine ≥18; restraints, malnutrition, >3 drugs, catheterisation, iatrogenic events).

**The differentials:**
- **Dementia**: the main differential *and* the main comorbidity. Recent onset and rapid decline from baseline = delirium until proved otherwise. Delirium can be prolonged in the elderly; failure to recover quickly after treating the cause does *not* by itself mean dementia. Premorbid history resolves the question.
- **Depression**: in severe depression, cognitive impairment is mild relative to the affective disturbance; in delirium, the reverse. Diurnal variation: depression worse in the **mornings**, delirium worse in the **evenings**. But elderly depressed patients are delirium-prone (self-neglect; anticholinergic tricyclics); bereavement can precipitate both.
- **Mania**: rarer than delirium in old age and often mistaken for it; late-life first presentations usually ride on organic brain disease; elderly manic patients are often exhausted and dehydrated; **"manic delirium" is a common presentation**.
- **Others**: anxiety (unless severe, unlikely to mimic); paranoid states/schizophrenia rarely confuse, though these patients are delirium-prone (self-neglect, neuroleptics and anticholinergics). Rare mimics: amnesic syndromes, epilepsia partialis continua, twilight states, **Charles Bonnet syndrome**, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, catatonia. **EEG helps where the picture is inconclusive.**
- **The working rule: when in doubt, investigate and manage as delirium until the situation is clear.**

## 9. Management: the four steps

1. **Address the underlying causes**; identify and treat *all* contributory factors (multifactorial by nature). Stop the anticholinergic, treat the infection, correct the metabolic derangement, clear the constipation, remove the catheter.
2. **Behavioural control.** *Non-pharmacological first*: reduce the confusing, frightening, disorienting hospital environment, good lighting, low noise, a visible clock, personal possessions, familiar individuals (relatives; in India, an attendant usually present by right); introduce and explain any invasive intervention simply, slowly, clearly, repeatedly; holding the patient's hand while talking focuses attention and reassures. *Pharmacological*: as in younger patients but **start lower, haloperidol 0.5–2 mg orally (or IM if necessary), repeated until controlled**; prescriptions for short periods only (up to 24 hours) to force review; once delirium resolves, taper over 3–5 days; if neuroleptics are not tolerated, use a benzodiazepine (diazepam, lorazepam, alprazolam).
3. **Prevent/treat complications**: falls (hyperactive) and pressure sores (hypoactive); anticipate urinary incontinence, sleep disturbance, malnutrition, immobilisation.
4. **Rehabilitation and family support**: return the patient to premorbid functioning; assess ADLs regularly and encourage independence; involve the family (they carry the aftercare); tell them delirium is often **recurrent** and teach the early signs; delirium is a useful marker of vulnerability and of the need for more intensive community aftercare.

**Prevention, the evidence-based optimism**: multi-component interventions targeting poor clinical practice reduce incidence cost-effectively: *prescribing discipline* (avoid deliriogenic drugs especially in Alzheimer's patients; regular drug-chart review; minimum drug count; non-pharmacological sleep promotion instead of hypnotics); *ward environment and routines* (minimise disorientation, sensory impairment, sleep deprivation; mobility; adequate food and fluid; staff training in recognising and managing delirium); *surgical routines* (good pre-, peri- and postoperative care, infection control, blood pressure, oxygenation). The cholinergic hypothesis of delirium makes cholinesterase inhibitors a plausible future preventive option, a research direction the chapter flags.

## 10. Indian practice

- **The quiet-ward problem**: crowded Indian medical wards with noise, night lights, unfamiliar attendants, and absent clocks manufacture exactly the sensory-environmental insults the chapter indicts; the realistic Indian prevention bundle is: fix spectacles and hearing aids, ensure the familiar attendant, day-night light rhythm, early mobilisation, treat constipation, and audit the drug chart.
- **The polypharmacy audit is free and high-yield**: Indian elderly prescriptions accumulate anticholinergics (tricyclics, bladder antispasmodics, first-generation antihistamines in "cold" combinations, benzhexol), the cumulative-burden lesson applies verbatim.
- **BUN/creatinine ≥18**: dehydration and pre-renal azotaemia from gastroenteritis and hot-season under-drinking are everyday Indian precipitants; check it before assuming dementia progression.
- **The family as care team**: Indian relatives already attend in-hospital; formalise their role (orientation, feeding, mobility, early-warning recognition on discharge) rather than tolerating them as bystanders.
- **The mislabel cascade**: "senile", "weak", "dementia ho gaya"; each label ends the diagnostic search. The countermeasure is the single screening question to the family: *"Is this how he usually is? When did the change start?"* A rapid change from baseline in an elderly patient is delirium until proven otherwise.
- **Costs (approx 2026)**: haloperidol is among the cheapest drugs in India; the prevention bundle costs discipline, not money; the expensive outcomes are the ones it prevents (ICU stays, prolonged admissions, nursing-home discharge).

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: The night the grandfather went quiet.** An 84-year-old man with mild Alzheimer's disease becomes drowsy and unresponsive over two days at home; the family brings him with the message "his dementia has become very advanced". Examination: reduced conscious level, poor attention, disorientation beyond his baseline; he has not passed urine properly; dry mucous membranes; BUN/creatinine ratio 26; an indwelling catheter placed at a clinic two weeks ago. Informant history: he was managing conversations and self-care until last week. Formulation: hypoactive delirium (dehydration + urinary infection + catheter + his Alzheimer's vulnerability) presenting as "sudden dementia". Management: fluids, infection treatment, catheter out with a voiding programme, quiet well-lit side of the ward with his wife attending, haloperidol 0.5 mg at night for one dose only (he settles without it), and graded mobilisation. He returns near baseline in five days; the family is taught the early-warning signs and the delirium-recurrence risk. Teaching points: rapid decline from baseline is delirium until proved otherwise; the vulnerability-insult arithmetic in action; hypoactive patients are sicker, not safer; the discharge teaching *is* the follow-up plan.

**Case 2: The man who got busier after surgery.** A 78-year-old woman, three days post hip-fracture repair, begins pulling at her bedclothes at night, calling out, and trying to climb off the bed; by day she is drowsy and eats little. Notes show: on three new postoperative drugs, opiates at night, no spectacles on the ward (left at home), and constipation for three days. Management: the four steps, opiate rationalisation and laxatives, spectacles fetched by family, infection screen clear, night-light and clock, her daughter present evenings, and single low-dose haloperidol 0.5 mg only if nocturnal agitation endangers her. Teaching points: hip-fracture patients are the highest-risk delirium group; the precipitating factors here were *all* modifiable and none were infections; pharmacology is the last and lightest touch of the management.

## 13. FAQ

**"Grandfather has become suddenly very demented, is this the end?"**: Not yet: a rapid change from his usual self is delirium until proved otherwise; often reversible once its causes (infection, dehydration, drugs, constipation) are treated, even though recovery in the elderly can take weeks rather than days.
**"He is just lying quietly, at least he isn't agitated."**: Quiet delirium is the *more* dangerous kind: it is missed, its causes go untreated, and it carries worse outcomes than the noisy form. Quiet is not calm.
**"The confusion hasn't cleared after a week, so it's dementia?"**: Not necessarily: elderly delirium can be prolonged. Treat everything treatable, reassess at recovery-speed rather than by the calendar, and only then re-examine the baseline.
**"Could his medicines be doing this?"**: Very commonly, yes: anticholinergic effects accumulate across ordinary-seeming prescriptions, and Alzheimer's patients are the most sensitive. The drug-chart review is a treatment.
**"Was it the hospital that made him confused?"**: Partly, often: catheters, restraints, noise, lost spectacles, sleepless nights and immobility are proven precipitants, which is why prevention bundles target them, and why your family member's presence, his glasses and his walking are therapy.
**"Will it come back?"**: One-third of patients have prolonged or recurrent episodes, and an episode can herald lasting vulnerability; learn the early signs (new drowsiness, poor attention, night-time confusion) and seek care early next time.
**"Are there medicines to prevent it?"**: The proven prevention is the care bundle, not a tablet: disciplined prescribing, orienting environment, mobility, food and fluid. Cholinesterate-inhibitor prevention is a research direction, not routine practice.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **The model sentence**: delirium = vulnerability × insult; both accumulate with age, hence the geriatric dominance.
- **Numbers to quote**: community 14% at 85+; inpatient prevalence 10–30%, incidence 4–53%; hip-fracture excess; one-third prolonged/recurrent.
- **Hypoactive predominance**, and its paradox: the quieter, the sicker.
- **The differential trio**: dementia (rapid decline from baseline; premorbid history resolves), depression (affective > cognitive; worse mornings vs delirium's evenings), mania (often first presentation on organic disease; exhausted-dehydrated "manic delirium").
- **Risk-factor pairs**: patient (visual impairment, illness severity, cognitive impairment, BUN/Cr ≥18) × hospital (restraints, malnutrition, >3 drugs, catheterisation, iatrogenic events), multiplicative.
- **Drug traps**: tricyclics, thioridazine, benzhexol, plus the cumulative-anticholinergic list (digoxin, prednisolone, cimetidine, ampicillin, warfarin).
- **Management: four steps** + haloperidol 0.5–2 mg, ≤24-hour prescriptions, 3–5-day taper.
- **Prevention bundle: three areas** (prescribing, ward environment, surgical routines), cost-effective, evidence-based.
- **Indian exam corner**: rapid-decline-from-baseline question to family; BUN/creatinine in hot-season dehydration; constipation as precipitant; formalising the attendant's role.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**Q1.** The reason delirium is most frequently encountered in late life is:
- (a) Doctors look for it more in the old
- (b) Both individual vulnerability factors and external insults accumulate with age, and their interaction produces delirium
- (c) Young brains cannot become delirious
- (d) It is purely genetic in the elderly

**Answer: (b).** The vulnerability-insult interaction model: rates of brain disease, sensory impairment, physical illness and deliriogenic medication all rise with age.

**Q2.** In an elderly inpatient, drowsiness, poor attention, incoherent speech and psychomotor underactivity without agitation most likely represent:
- (a) The most common form of elderly delirium, the hypoactive variety
- (b) Normal ageing
- (c) Inevitably dementia
- (d) A benign sleep state

**Answer: (a).** Most elderly delirium is quiet and hypoactive, and paradoxically carries worse outcomes than the hyperactive form, partly because it is missed.

**Q3.** The most discriminating feature of delirium from dementia, though problematic as a diagnostic criterion because outcome is unknown at the outset, is:
- (a) Age of the patient
- (b) Reversibility of the cognitive impairment
- (c) Family history
- (d) Sleep pattern only

**Answer: (b).** Rapid decline from baseline suggests delirium; recovery confirms it, but you cannot wait for proof, so the working rule is "manage as delirium until clear".

**Q4.** A BUN/creatinine ratio of which value or above is cited as a delirium risk factor in elderly medical patients?
- (a) 5
- (b) 10
- (c) 18
- (d) 40

**Answer: (c).** Dehydration/pre-renal azotaemia markers join visual impairment, illness severity and cognitive impairment as patient-related predictors.

**Q5.** Recommended starting haloperidol dose for behavioural control of delirium in elderly patients is:
- (a) 10 mg daily
- (b) 0.5–2 mg (orally, repeated until controlled), with prescriptions for up to 24 hours to force review
- (c) 50 mg depot injection
- (d) Full adult loading doses

**Answer: (b).** Start low, prescribe short, taper over 3–5 days once the delirium resolves; with benzodiazepines as the alternative if neuroleptics are not tolerated.

**Q6.** Which intervention has randomised evidence of cost-effectively reducing delirium incidence in elderly inpatients?
- (a) Routine night sedation
- (b) Multi-component interventions targeting prescribing, ward environment and surgical routines
- (c) Prophylactic antipsychotics for all
- (d) Mandatory bed rest

**Answer: (b).** Drug-chart discipline, orientation, sensory correction, mobility, nutrition, hydration and good perioperative care, the prevention bundle that outperforms any single measure.
