# Social Anxiety Disorder & Specific Phobias

## 1. In one line + overview

**Social anxiety disorder = a persistent, pounding fear of being watched, judged and humiliated by other people, strong enough to make the person avoid the very situations that build a life; specific phobias = focused, lightning-strike fears of particular objects or situations (dogs, injections, heights, lifts) that the person knows are excessive but cannot switch off.**

These two conditions are cousins: the same alarm machinery, aimed at different targets. In social anxiety, the target is other human beings (meetings, classrooms, weddings, presentations, even eating in front of others) because the mind has decided that scrutiny equals danger and that a stumble equals ruin. In a specific phobia, the target is one circumscribed thing: the needle, the dog, the flight. The person is perfectly calm everywhere else and can be rational about the fear everywhere except in front of the feared object. Both are among psychiatry's most treatable conditions: graded, repeated exposure to the feared situation is a genuine cure in the majority of phobia cases and a strong treatment for social anxiety, yet both conditions are under-referred because the suffering is quiet (avoidance, not collapse) and the Indian consultation culture rarely brings them in by name.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:

- Distinguish the performance-only subtype from the generalised form, and their different treatments
- Name the five specific phobia categories and the blood-injection-injury phobia's unique physiology
- Explain the safety-behaviour engine that maintains both conditions
- Build a graded exposure ladder for a phobia and a behavioural-experiment programme for social anxiety
- Use SSRIs for generalised social anxiety and beta-blockers for performance-only anxiety
- Apply Indian-context targets: English-medium presentations, arranged-marriage "meetings", vivas, eating in canteens
- Recognise taijin kyofusho (the culture-bound variant) and autism's social difference as differentials

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Social anxiety disorder is one of the most common mental disorders: lifetime prevalence ~7–12% in Western surveys, onset concentrated in early-to-mid teens (median ~13), running chronically without treatment. It precedes and feeds secondary depression, alcohol misuse (the "party lubricant" route), and academic/occupational underachievement, promotion declined, seminar questions unasked. Specific phobias are the most common anxiety category of all: lifetime ~10–12%, beginning in childhood for animal/natural-environment types and spreading across adult life for situational ones; they cause less global impairment (by design; avoidable) but the dental, injection and flight versions have real medical and occupational consequences.

**India.** NMHS 2015–16 pooled anxiety disorders (found in roughly 3% of adults with large treatment gaps). No Indian-specific prevalence for social anxiety, but clinical and campus data suggest enormous uncounted burden: seminar-phobia among first-generation English-medium students, viva-voce terror in professional colleges, interview failure loops among skilled candidates, wedding-stage avoidance (a specific Indian exposure; every life event is a public performance), and the "he is just shy" family frame that pathologises nothing and treats nothing. Specific phobias in India arrive wearing practical costumes: the never-scaled staircase uncle, the cousin who "cannot see the needle" and skips blood tests, the dog phobia sustained by street-dog reality (in India, the feared object genuinely exists outside the door; graded exposure needs geography-aware design).

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**Biological.**
- Moderate heritability (~30–40%); temperamental behavioural inhibition in infancy (the shy, high-reactive toddler) is the best-established developmental precursor of social anxiety
- Amygdala hyper-activation to faces; but for specific phobias, the circuit is narrow and often laid down by a single conditioning event

**Psychological.**
- Conditioning (the dog bite that installs the dog phobia; the classroom humiliation that installs the reading-aloud phobia), but many phobias arrive without a remembered cause, suggesting preparedness: humans come pre-wired to rapidly learn fear of evolutionarily relevant things (snakes, heights, blood, strangers' scrutiny)
- The maintenance engine (this matters more than the origin): avoidance and safety behaviours prevent the disconfirmation that would dissolve the fear; self-focused attention ("how am I coming across?") crowds out the data that others are not actually judging
- Perfectionist standards for performance; harsh internal critic

**Social.**
- Teasing/bullying histories; over-critical parenting ("what will people say" parenting is a cultural risk factor factory)
- Indian context: linguistic humiliation in English-medium transitions, single-attempt exam culture, "log kya kahenge" (what will people say) as a household refrain, and the stage-density of Indian social life (every rite is a performance with an audience of a hundred)

**For specific phobias by category:** animal/natural-environment phobias skew to childhood onset and female prevalence; situational (lifts, flights, tunnels) to adult onset; blood-injection-injury runs strongly in families (a genuinely genetic flavour).

## 6. What happens in the mind/brain

**Story 1: the spotlight defect.** Healthy social brains run a dim, generous spotlight: others notice us a little, mostly kindly. The socially anxious brain installs a prison-spotlight pointed at oneself, everything I do is being recorded and judged, plus a funhouse mirror of how I appear (blushing enormous, trembling visible from the back row). Attention turns inward, the actual feedback (bored, kind, busy people) never gets processed, and the distorted self-image is never corrected. Treatment works by turning the spotlight outward and shrinking the mirror, through deliberate experiments the camera of reality contradicts the imagination.

**Story 2: the safety trap.** Every avoidance and safety behaviour (sitting in the last row, rehearsing sentences silently, holding the glass with both hands to hide the tremor, never attending the wedding) buys immediate relief, and pays for it by extending the fear's warranty: the brain records "that was dangerous, and the hiding saved me". Relief today is rent on the phobia; the fear never meets its own evidence. Graded exposure is the deliberate defaulting on that rent, room by room, until the landlord gives up.

**Story 3: the needle and the fainting switch.** A special story for blood-injection-injury phobia: where all other phobias run the panic script (racing heart, adrenaline), this one runs the opposite; a vagal DROP: heart slows, blood pressure falls, vision greys, and the person faints. It is hereditary, it is genuine, and it is uniquely treatable with applied tension (tensing the large muscles to keep blood pressure up), one of the most satisfying one-session cures in medicine and a favourite exam pearl.

## 7. Symptoms

### Social anxiety disorder
- Fear of situations where others can watch or judge: speaking, eating, drinking, writing, performing in front of others; meetings, classrooms, interviews, weddings, toilets with queues near them
- Physical storm when exposed: blushing, sweating, trembling, racing heart, "mind going blank", voice wavering, nausea, urgency to escape
- Fear is of the SYMPTOMS as much as the situation ("they will SEE me blush"; the blush is the feared catastrophe)
- Anticipatory anxiety days ahead (the sleepless night before the seminar); post-mortem rumination for days after
- Avoidance and safety behaviours: last-row seating, arriving early to avoid the entrance-walk, declining invitations, scripted phone calls, alcohol before functions
- Generalised subtype: nearly all social situations; performance-only subtype: public speaking/presentation alone (better prognosis, different treatment)
- Secondary consequences: underachievement, depression, alcohol misuse, deep loneliness ("everyone else got to practise being human")

### Specific phobias: the five categories
1. **Animal** (dogs, snakes, insects, lizards)
2. **Natural environment** (heights, water, storms, darkness)
3. **Blood-injection-injury** (needles, blood draws, dentists, watching surgery videos), with the fainting physiology
4. **Situational** (lifts, flights, tunnels, bridges, enclosed spaces; the boundary with claustrophobia/panic runs here)
5. **Other** (choking/vomiting phobias, costumed characters, loud sounds)

**The signature:** instant, intense, focused fear with full insight ("I know the lift is safe; my body does not"); near-total calm elsewhere; anticipatory dread when the encounter is unavoidable; avoidance engineering of impressive creativity (the twelve-storey climb, the cross-country train to avoid a flight, the vaccination declined).

## 8. Diagnosis

**The logic (DSM-5, paraphrased):**
- *Social anxiety disorder*: marked fear/anxiety about social situations with possible scrutiny; fear of showing anxiety symptoms and being negatively evaluated; the situations almost always provoke fear; avoidance or intense distress; out of proportion to actual threat; 6 months or more; impairment. Specify performance-only.
- *Specific phobia*: marked fear about a specific object/situation; immediate fear when encountered; avoidance or intense distress; out of proportion; 6 months; impairment. Code by the five categories. Adults acknowledge the excess: children may not.

**The assessment craft:**
- Build the avoidance inventory: list the declined situations (the ladder of a life shaped around the fear; this becomes the exposure ladder)
- Ask the blush-tremor-blank questions specifically: physical symptoms ARE the feared object in social anxiety
- Sample the inner monologue ("what do you predict they will think?"): predictions become experiment hypotheses
- Screen the boundary conditions: panic disorder (surges out of the blue, not situation-locked), agoraphobia (fear clusters in escape-difficult situations broadly), autism (social difficulty from non-reading, not from fear of judgment; the treatment differs completely), psychosis-spectrum paranoia (conviction of being watched/harmed, not embarrassment), body dysmorphic disorder (conviction of an appearance defect rather than fear of judgment generally)
- For children: selective mutism history, school-refusal pattern

**Instruments by name:** LSAS (Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale) for severity/tracking; SPIN as the screen; specific phobia work uses behavioural avoidance tests (BAT), the walk toward the dog, scored by steps.

### Differential table

| Looks like | The distinguishing feature |
|---|---|
| Ordinary shyness | No 6-month impairment engine; functioning holds |
| Panic disorder | Surges unpredictable and un-locked to social scrutiny; agoraphobic spread |
| GAD | Worry across all domains, not scrutiny-locked |
| Avoidant personality | Long-standing pervasive pattern; considerable overlap with generalised SAD: the treatment overlaps too |
| Autism spectrum | Social difference is cognitive (not reading cues), not fear-driven; no blush-catastrophe |
| Paranoia (psychotic) | Conviction of persecution/harm, not embarrassment; does not yield to exposure logic |
| Body dysmorphic | Fixed belief about a specific appearance defect, mirror-checking |
| Selective mutism (child) | The childhood silhouette of social anxiety; treat early |

## 9. Management

### Social anxiety disorder

**The core treatment, CBT with exposure (first-line, durable):**
1. **Psychoeducation**: the spotlight defect and the safety trap explained; the diagnosis re-framed ("your brain is over-defending a territory that was never at war")
2. **Attention re-training**: deliberately moving attention outward during conversations (count the other person's questions, notice their clothing), starves the funhouse mirror
3. **Behavioural experiments with video-feedback**: predictions written ("I will look insane"), the speech recorded, the video watched, the patient meets the real image, not the imagined one; the single most powerful session in social-anxiety CBT
4. **Graded exposure ladder**: from "ask a shopkeeper a question" through "eat in the canteen" to "present in the seminar", repeated until the situation bores the alarm
5. **Safety-behaviour stripping**: exposure done WITH the crutches first, then without (speech with trembling hands visible, without the scripted notes)
6. **Social skills training** where genuine skill gaps coexist (not all socially anxious people lack skills; most over-monitor the ones they have)

**Pharmacotherapy:**
- **SSRIs** (sertraline, escitalopram, paroxetine): first-line for the generalised subtype; 8–12 week trials; 12-month maintenance after response
- **Beta-blockers (propranolol 10–40 mg, 30–60 min pre-performance)**, the classic treatment for performance-ONLY subtype: blunts the tremor-racing-heart storm that is the catastrophe
- **MAOIs (phenelzine)**: historically effective, logistically obsolete; know for exams
- **Benzodiazepines**: short bridges only, same trap logic as GAD (and alcohol as a self-prescribed social lubricant is the comorbidity to name explicitly)

### Specific phobias

- **Graded in-vivo exposure is the cure**: therapist-guided, repeated approach in steps until fear extinguishes; average treatment for animal/situational phobias is a handful of sessions
- **One-session treatment (Öst's protocol)**: a single prolonged (2–3 hour) massed exposure session, spectacular results for animal, injection and dental phobias; named in exams as the flagship brief therapy
- **Applied tension for blood-injection-injury phobia**: tensing the torso/limb muscles through the encounter to prevent the vagal drop; combined with graded exposure to needles/blood: fainting usually retires in 1–2 sessions; pair with gradual re-introduction of needed care (blood tests, dental visits, vaccinations)
- **Cognitive work** where beliefs ride along ("if I panic in the lift I will be trapped and die"; test the escape truth, the duration truth, the crowd truth)
- **Medication has almost NO role in specific phobias**; exposure is the treatment; saying this is an exam point

*India note (approx 2026): the exposure ladder is FREE; the Indian street is a graded exposure gymnasium of unmatched density (shops, autos, chai stalls, bus queues). Private CBT ≈ ₹600–1,500/session; district/college counsellors and Tele-MANAS (14416) provide the base tier; propranolol ≈ ₹30–80/month for performance dosing; sertraline/escitalopram per the GAD note's ledger. The Indian clinical shortcut worth institutionalising: professional colleges running pre-seminar exposure modules (a 6-session practice group before the presentation season) prevent years of avoidance; this is cheap, scaleable, and nobody currently does it.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **The English-medium trap.** First-generation students from vernacular schooling meet their first seminar in English: the linguistic anxiety and the social anxiety fuse into seminar-phobia and, not infrequently, course abandonment. Interventions that work: practice-pairs in the mother tongue first, permission to open in the mother tongue ("one sentence of Telugu, then the English term"), recorded practice with video-feedback logic. Faculties that mock accent entrench the disorder; faculties that grade content over accent dissolve it.
- **The arranged-marriage meeting.** A one-hour performance before a panel of elders, with life consequences, the Indian social-anxiety pressure test. Presentations to the clinic occur after failed meetings ("she sat frozen, they read it as arrogance"). Treatment frame: exposure rehearsals with family role-play, propranolol judiciously for the performance-only profile, and family re-briefing ("frozen is not arrogant").
- **Log kya kahenge as household software.** The refrain "what will people say" trains children in audience-consciousness daily. The family module replaces it deliberately: the "audience experiment" (deliberately do a small odd thing in public, wear the mismatched kurta, and count who actually notices: almost nobody) is the vaccine, and it needs the family's buy-in.
- **Wedding-stage engineering.** Indian weddings force performances (the couple's walk, the speeches, the photographs). Families can grade the exposure: seated roles before speaking roles, small gatherings before large, rehearsal at home with video feedback; weddings become the treatment arena rather than the minefield.
- **Viva-voce terror.** The Indian professional exam's oral format is a social-anxiety amplifier. Medical and engineering colleges lose capable candidates to viva-avoidance and freeze. Practice vivas (recorded, repeated, desensitised) are the specific antidote; student-counselling cells should run them in exam season as routinely as mock written papers.
- **The needle-phobia medical cost.** Skipped blood tests, declined vaccinations, avoided dental care; the BII phobia's Indian footprint is medical, not psychological, on the surface. Every lab and dental clinic should know applied tension; teach it in the first 30 seconds of the encounter ("tense your legs and arms like this through the draw; you will not faint").
- **Dogs and geography.** Street dogs make Indian dog-phobia semi-rational; graded exposure designs must include the real environment (a known calm dog first, a street-dog corridor later, carrying a stick as a temporary transitional safety-behaviour to be faded) rather than clinic-room-only work.

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: the topper who could not speak.** A 20-year-old second-year engineering student in Coimbatore, state topper in her school, froze in her first English-medium seminar: voice trembled, notes shook, classmates snickered, and she left mid-sentence. Within a term she was skipping all presentation components ("headache" certificates), eating alone, and answering only written questions. Assessment: social anxiety disorder, generalised-ish but performance-triggered, with secondary avoidance and early depressive colouring. Management: 10 sessions of CBT, psychoeducation of the spotlight defect, video-feedback of a practice talk (she predicted "catastrophic shaking"; the video showed a normal nervous student), a graded ladder from shopkeeper questions through canteen-eating to a 5-minute seminar with a supportive friend in the front row, safety-behaviour stripping (notes surrendered in session 8), and a brief propranolol trial for the two highest rungs. At semester's end she presented in class, scoring average, and celebrated the average like a medal. Teaching points: (1) academic collapse is the presenting face; (2) video-feedback corrects the funhouse mirror faster than any argument; (3) the ladder ends at functioning, not at comfort; average performance after terror is a cure.

**Case 2: the engineer who walked twelve floors.** A 55-year-old government engineer in Bhopal had not entered a lift for 30 years, climbing stairs to his twelfth-floor office with dignity and breathlessness, declining field postings to towers and dams. A routine cardiac workup (for the exertion) surfaced the phobia. Management: three sessions of graded in-vivo exposure, standing before an open lift; stepping in with the doors held; riding one floor with the therapist; riding to the twelfth with conversation; then solo trips with a phone call running. Applied relaxation for the anticipatory dread. At week 6 he rode the lift daily and requested a field posting. Teaching points: (1) situational phobias hide behind "lifestyle habits" and endurance careers; the body's bill eventually presents; (2) the exposure ladder is short for situational phobias (sessions, not years); (3) no medication was needed or used; exposure is the specific cure.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way patients and families ask it

**"My son is just shy. Shy is not a disease, no?"** Shyness is a temperament; social anxiety disorder is what happens when shyness grows an engine, when it costs marks, friendships, careers, and sleep for months and years. The shy man still attends the wedding; the socially anxious man declines it and suffers twice.

**"Why do I blank out the moment I stand to speak?"** Adrenaline shifts the brain to its emergency setting, which is fast-acting and narrow; words are stored in the slow retrieval system, which gets switched off. The blank is physiology, not stupidity; and it is trainable: repeated exposure re-ranks the situation as ordinary, and the retrieval system stays online.

**"Everyone says 'just speak, practice makes perfect'. Is that the treatment?"** Almost: the advice is right but ungraded. "Just speak" as one giant step fails; the same step, split into a graded ladder (shopkeeper, canteen, two friends, seminar), works reliably. Treatment is your grandmother's advice, engineered.

**"Will a tablet cure my stage fear?"** For pure stage fear there is an honest tablet: a small dose of propranolol before the performance that steadies the tremor and pounding heart without sedation. For fear spread across situations, tablets (SSRIs) plus graded practice work better than either alone.

**"I know the lift is safe. I know it. Why does my body not know it?"** Because the fear is not stored where knowledge lives. The alarm circuit learned the fear in one lesson, and it does not read, it only updates through experience: repeated, graded, survived encounters. Knowing and feeling rejoin through practice, not through argument.

**"I faint at needles: my mother is the same. Is there anything for it?"** It runs in families, it involves a blood-pressure drop rather than panic, and it has a near-specific cure: tensing your big muscles through the draw (applied tension) plus graded practice, usually one or two sessions. Labs should be told beforehand; nearly everyone can get blood tests after it.

**"My daughter does not talk in school at all but talks normally at home."** That is selective mutism, the childhood silhouette of social anxiety. It deserves early treatment (a talking-ladder built at school with the teacher), because the waiting-game version hardens into the adult disorder.

**"Can I just take a drink before functions? It works."** It works the way a rented crutch works; it also teaches the brain that humans are only survivable under chemistry. The standard endpoint of that route is needing two drinks to speak to two people. We can get you to the same wedding without it, in steps.

**"Is this because I was kept at home too much / scolded too much?"** Partly contribution, not cause: an inborn cautious temperament meets an audience-conscious upbringing ("what will people say") and fuses into the disorder. Blame-mapping the family is inaccurate and unhelpful; the family re-training IS the treatment ally.

**"Will I have to do the feared thing in the sessions themselves?"** Yes: that is where the cure is. But graded, agreed in advance, with your consent at each rung, and never by ambush. Nothing is done to you in the room that was not first written on the ladder.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**Gates:** both diagnoses need the 6-month duration, out-of-proportion fear, and impairment clauses; social anxiety adds the negative-evaluation core; phobias add the five categories and immediate-onset timing.

**Mnemonics:**
- Specific phobia categories, "**A N B S O**: Animal, Natural environment, Blood-injection-injury, Situational, Other"
- Social anxiety maintenance, "**The 3 S's**: Spotlight (self-focused attention), Safety behaviours, Selective attention to threat"

**High-yield facts:**
- Behavioural inhibition in infancy = developmental precursor of SAD
- Blood-injection-injury phobia: vasovagal FAINT (bradycardia/hypotension, opposite of panic); applied tension as the counter
- Propranolol pre-performance = performance-only subtype; SSRIs = generalised subtype
- Öst's one-session treatment: massed prolonged exposure; flagship for animal/BII phobias
- No routine pharmacotherapy for specific phobias; exposure is the cure (an exam line)
- Video-feedback and attention-training as SAD-specific techniques
- Taijin kyofusho (fear of OFFENDING others with one's body/odour, the Japanese culture-bound cousin; ICD appendix): a one-mark favourite
- Selective mutism = the childhood silhouette

**Classic traps:**
1. Missing the performance-only/generalised split and putting a tremor-case on long SSRIs it never needed
2. Treating lift phobia with benzodiazepines chronically instead of three sessions of exposure
3. Missing alcohol as the self-treatment route in generalised SAD
4. Calling autistic social difference "social anxiety" and subjecting it to exposure it does not need
5. Diagnosing panic disorder when the surges are strictly situation-locked
6. Forgetting that BII phobia patients FAIN (not faint-panic), and injuring them with classic reassurance posture during blood draws

**Indian exam corner:** "social anxiety disorder (criteria and management", " differentiate social phobia from avoidant personality disorder", " blood-injection-injury phobia) applied tension", " specific phobia, types and treatment (one-session therapy)". Indian-context additions: viva-voce and English-medium seminar anxiety as school/college health issues, "log kya kahenge" as cultural maintenance factor, and the NMHS anxiety-bucket figures.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. The core fear in social anxiety disorder is:**
A. Open spaces
B. Negative evaluation by others
C. Physical illness
D. Being alone
**Answer: B.** Fear of scrutiny and negative judgment, and of visible anxiety symptoms themselves.

**2. A surgeon wants a medicine ONLY for her quarterly conference presentations, trembling being the main problem. Best choice:**
A. Long-term SSRI
B. Propranolol before each performance
C. Clozapine
D. Diazepam nightly
**Answer: B.** Performance-only subtype: pre-performance beta-blockade targets the visible tremor-storm; no chronic treatment needed.

**3. The unique physiology of blood-injection-injury phobia:**
A. Panic with tachycardia and hypertension
B. Vasovagal response: bradycardia and hypotension with fainting
C. Seizures
D. Hypoglycaemia
**Answer: B.** The diphasic vagal response: the reason for applied tension as its specific remedy.

**4. The maintenance engine of both social anxiety and phobias is:**
A. Genetic mutation
B. Avoidance and safety behaviours blocking corrective learning
C. Dopamine excess
D. Poor parenting only
**Answer: B.** Relief today is rent on the fear, the exposure rationale in one line.

**5. Öst's one-session treatment involves:**
A. Twelve weekly psychoeducation talks
B. A single prolonged massed exposure session
C. Hypnosis
D. Medication desensitisation
**Answer: B.** One 2–3 hour graded-to-top exposure: a flagship evidence-based brief therapy.

**6. A student never speaks in school but speaks fluently at home. Diagnosis:**
A. Autism spectrum disorder
B. Selective mutism
C. Intellectual disability
D. Depression
**Answer: B.** The childhood silhouette of social anxiety; treat early with a school-graded talking ladder.

**7. First-line treatment for GENERALISED social anxiety disorder:**
A. Benzodiazepines lifelong
B. CBT with exposure (+/- SSRI)
C. Antipsychotics
D. Avoidance counselling
**Answer: B.** Structured exposure-centred CBT, SSRIs for the broader subtype; benzos are bridges only.

**8. In the cognitive model, the social anxiety "funhouse mirror" refers to:**
A. The patient's actual distorted face
B. The distorted processed self-image, correctable by video feedback
C. The audience's judgment
D. The therapist's countertransference
**Answer: B.** Watching one's recorded performance collapses the imagined catastrophic image, the most powerful session in SAD treatment.
