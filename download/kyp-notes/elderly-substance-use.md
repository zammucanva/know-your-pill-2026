# Substance Use Disorders in Older People — The Silent Epidemic

## 1. In one line + overview

**Alcohol and medication problems do not disappear with age; they go quiet: lower doses cause the harm, presentations hide behind falls and confusion, everyone asks the questions less, and the treatment works as well as or better than in the young.**

The Oxford old-age chapter on substance use divides the territory three ways: **alcohol use disorders (AUDs)**, the best-studied; **medication use disorders (MUDs)**: the most geriatric-specific (older people take ~3 times the medications of the general population, and benzodiazepines lead the risk); and **illegal substances and nicotine**: smaller today, growing tomorrow. Its two governing insights: the **threshold problem** (pharmacokinetic ageing, comorbidity and interactions mean elderly people develop problems at intakes that would be unremarkable at 40, so population "safe limits" are set too high, and diagnostic criteria built on tolerance, withdrawal and social consequences arrive wearing disguises) and the **invisibility problem** (patient, clinician, family and society each contribute reasons not to ask, not to refer, not to treat, ageism dressing up drinking as "understandable"). Against both problems stands the chapter's best-kept secret: **older people do at least as well in treatment as younger people.**

## 4. Epidemiology

**AUDs.** Community: 2–4% of older people (men 4–6 times women); looser "excessive consumption" criteria raise this to 16% of men. Clinical settings climb the ladder: **emergency departments 14%, nursing homes 18%, psychiatric inpatients 23%**. True consumption probably declines with age (premature death of heavy drinkers, reduced reserve, cohort and network effects), but the ageing of populations makes absolute numbers grow: the "silent epidemic" warning. **MUDs.** Older people: ~13% of the US population using >30% of prescription and 35% of OTC drugs (~3× the general rate); an Irish community study found **17% of older people prescribed benzodiazepines** (women 2× men; 52% of users on long-acting agents; 18% on a second psychotropic). **India**: the epidemiology is thinner and the picture is distinct; Indian elderly populations carry rising alcohol exposure (documented in national surveys as a male-dominated pattern persisting into older age), enormous iatrogenic benzodiazepine exposure (sleeping tablets are among the most self-purchased and over-prescribed drugs in Indian pharmacies), and near-total absence of elderly-specific services; the silent epidemic concept applies with full force.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**AUD, biological/medical**: genetic contribution to both early- and late-onset AUDs, overlapping with risk for antisocial personality, drug problems, anxiety and mood disorders; illness as both cause and consequence of drinking. **Social**: male gender; bereavement; cohort effects; culture, ethnicity, religion; divorced/single status (two-way relationship with marital problems); retirement and social-network changes. **Psychological**: early-onset, antisocial personality, hyperactivity, impulsivity; late-onset: neuroticism, depression (drinking to self-medicate, or becoming depressed from drinking). **The general elderly risk table** (Atkinson): predisposing (family history, previous abuse, cohort consumption patterns, personality traits); exposure-increasing (gender patterns: men alcohol and illicit drugs, women sedative-hypnotics and anxiolytics; chronic illness with pain, insomnia or anxiety; long-term prescribing; caregiver overuse of PRN medication in institutions; life stress, loss, isolation; negative affects; family collusion; discretionary time and money); effect-amplifying (age-associated drug sensitivity; chronic illness; alcohol-drug and drug-drug interactions). **MUD-specific**: female gender, lower education, separated/divorced status, older age, personality disorder, depression and anxiety, chronic pain, insomnia, long-term prescribing.

## 6. What happens in the body: two short stories

**The same dose, a different blood level.** Age redistributes body water and fat: the higher fat-to-lean ratio dilutes alcohol's volume of distribution, so the same grams of ethanol produce a higher blood concentration in an 80-year-old than a 40-year-old; metabolic efficiency and physiological reserve fall in parallel. Add comorbid illness and interacting prescriptions, and the geriatric liver-brain axis behaves like a person drinking more than the bottle says. This is why the NIAAA caps older drinking at **one drink per day**, and why population limits (21/14 units weekly) are, in the chapter's words, inappropriately high for older people.

**The criteria in disguise.** Dependence criteria were written for working-age adults. In the elderly: craving and compulsion are less clear-cut; tolerance and withdrawal are masked by medical conditions; and the social consequences (financial, occupational, family, legal) largely evaporate (no job to lose, no licence, family reluctant to label grandpa). The disorder doesn't weaken with age; its *measurable signature* fades, which is why screening must replace criteria-waiting.

## 7. Symptoms: the clinical faces

**Alcohol.** The geriatric presentation is atypical and masked: **falls, confusion (delirium), self-neglect, "dementia progressing", depression, insomnia**, rather than drunk-and-disorderly. The full morbidity map: gastrointestinal (fatty liver → hepatitis → cirrhosis → malignancy; gastritis, ulcers, bleeding, varices, pancreatitis); malignancies (mouth, pharynx, larynx, oesophagus, liver, colorectal, pancreas); cardiovascular (ischaemic heart disease, hypertension, arrhythmias, heart failure, cardiomyopathy); haematological (macrocytosis, both direct and B12/folate-mediated; anaemia); musculoskeletal (falls and fractures, reduced bone density, myopathy); metabolic (hypoglycaemia, hyperuricaemia, lipids, unstable diabetes); neuropsychiatric (cognitive impairment and dementia, frontal impairment, Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome, cerebellar degeneration, central pontine myelinosis, Marchiafava–Bignami, depression, psychosis, withdrawal; harder to treat when old, suicide risk); socio-demographic (male, divorced/widowed/single, isolation, both ends of the socio-economic spectrum); other (alcohol-drug interactions, aspiration pneumonia, accidents). Note the J-shaped honesty: light-to-moderate drinking may *protect* against dementia while disorders raise dementia risk.

**Medications.** Benzodiazepines: oversedation, confusion, delirium, incontinence, **falls**, with a special Indian-family irony: "sleeping tablet" dependence begun for grief or pain decades earlier and renewed at every counter. Opioid analgesics: pain-driven use drifting into disorder; monitor dose, taper deliberately. OTC drugs and interactions: the polypharmacy multiplication. In cognitively impaired patients, disturbed behaviour may be the presenting feature of medication toxicity.

## 8. Diagnosis

**Clinical assessment**: thorough interview (quantity, frequency, beverage, context, sensitively and non-judgementally; patients disengage when threatened); mental state; physical examination; collateral history with consent; dependence features explored if indicated. **Screening**: CAGE (most widely studied and best recognised; quick; sensitivities vary by population, the chapter recommends at least this instrument), AUDIT, and biophysical measures (MCV, liver function, carbohydrate-deficient transferrin); the last less reliable in the elderly because comorbidity produces false positives, but useful adjunctively and for monitoring treatment. **Investigations as directed by clinical status**: urea/electrolytes, FBC, LFTs, B12 and folate; brain CT/MRI; GI imaging and endoscopy; ECG and beyond as indicated. **MUD assessment**: the list-all-medications-with-indications rule, and **ask the patient to bring all medications in their containers** (yielding the adherence picture simultaneously); record adverse effects; screen for the at-risk patterns (long-term benzodiazepines, escalating analgesics, PRN overuse, alcohol on top). **Differential**: every late-onset atypical presentation (new falls, new confusion, new self-neglect) in an older person earns a substance screen, and, conversely, apparent "dementia progression" may be alcohol or benzodiazepines in disguise.

## 9. Management

**Prevention layers.** *Primary*: population measures (access, advertising, education), currently aimed at the young, wrongly; individual education for older people (especially given one-third late-onset). *Secondary*: target at-risk drinkers facing the classic geriatric precipitants, **bereavement, social isolation, adjustment to retirement, new physical or psychiatric illness**. *Tertiary*: treatment of established disorders.

**Alcohol withdrawal: the geriatric adjustments.** Benzodiazepine-assisted withdrawal carries elevated risks of oversedation, confusion and falls; no elderly-specific guidelines exist. **Lorazepam is the safest choice** (age and liver disease barely affect its metabolism; IM absorption predictable), though clinical experience with chlordiazepoxide is broader; choose by patient characteristics (previous treatments, hepatic status) and guide dosing with an objective withdrawal measure (CIWA-Ar: mild <10, moderate 10–20, severe >20). **Thiamine** to prevent Wernicke–Korsakoff: oral administration is as effective as parenteral in the emergency-department setting (no elderly-specific guidance, individualise by general health, oral tolerance, compliance).

**Relapse prevention.** FDA-approved agents: disulfiram, acamprosate, naltrexone, **disulfiram best avoided in older people** (limited efficacy, worse side-effect profile); **naltrexone and acamprosate suitable**.

**Psychosocial.** Address contributing circumstances (finances, housing); psychotherapy evidence is thin, but **older people respond better in same-age settings**; support groups including AA. 

**Prognosis.** Older people are at least as likely, if not more likely, **to benefit from treatment** as younger people, modulated by individual factors, support systems, and the availability of age-tailored services.

**MUDs.** Taper sedatives and analgesics with deliberate, monitored plans; correct iatrogenic over- and under-prescribing; in institutional settings, discipline the PRN culture and caregiver overuse; treat the insomnia, pain and anxiety that drove the prescriptions in the first place.

## 10. Indian practice

- **Ask, always, in the geriatric OPD.** The underdiagnosis table applies with Indian intensifiers: families shield ("he only drinks at functions"), doctors skip the question ("what use at this age"), and ageist benevolence calls drinking "understandable". One respectful question about quantity-frequency-context plus a CAGE takes under a minute.
- **The benzodiazepine inheritance.** Decades of easily renewed prescriptions, and pharmacy self-purchase, make long-term hypnotic use one of India's commonest geriatric addiction problems; every cognitive or falls complaint in an elderly patient warrants a tablet-bag review (the bring-all-containers rule, with the family's help).
- **Falls + confusion = screen for substances before ordering the CT.** The masked presentations arrive at Indian emergency departments nightly as "weakness" and "dementia has worsened"; bloods (MCV, LFTs, B12, folate) and a collateral drinking history redirect management more often than imaging.
- **Family as treatment ally.** The joint family that conceals the drinking can also deliver the secondary-prevention package: structured company, meals, activity scheduling around the drinking hour, and a shared relapse plan, the Indian version of same-age social settings.
- **Withdrawal management reality.** Lorazepam availability is good in India; chlordiazepoxide remains the familiar agent; CIWA-Ar-guided dosing is implementable on any ward with a nursing chart; thiamine is cheap, the B1-before-carbohydrate discipline from the amnesic-syndromes note applies to every elderly withdrawal.
- **Costs (approx 2026)**: CAGE costs nothing; bloods are inexpensive; naltrexone and acamprosate are available in India at moderate cost; the deaddiction-centre network is young-adult-oriented, elderly-specific programmes barely exist, reinforcing the primary-care-first approach of the R1 note.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: The widow who "took to her bed".** A 74-year-old widow, six months after her husband's death, is referred for "depression not responding to two antidepressants". She has stopped going to her morning walks, is falling at night, and her son has noticed she "mixes up her words after dinner". Collateral (with consent): a half-bottle of whisky nightly since the bereavement, previously a social drinker; her "sleeping tablet" continued unchanged. Examination: mild withdrawal tremor in the morning, macrocytosis, raised GGT. Formulation: late-onset alcohol use disorder (bereavement-triggered; the one-third rule) with benzodiazepine co-use, presenting as treatment-resistant depression and falls. Management: grief-focused and alcohol-focused work together; supervised home withdrawal with lorazepam (low, CIWA-guided) and thiamine; tapering the old hypnotic separately and later; the son as medication supervisor; same-age social reconnection (temple/morning-walk group). At 3 months: abstinent, antidepressant working, no falls. Teaching points: late-onset AUD follows loss; "resistant depression" in the elderly deserves a drinking history; withdrawal sedation risks demand lorazepam logic and separate tapers.

**Case 2: The medicine bag.** An 81-year-old man attends with his daughter for "memory loss". He brings, at your instruction, a bag containing 14 medications from four prescribers, including two benzodiazepines (one long-acting, prescribed 11 years ago "for nerves after bypass"), an opioid-plus-paracetamol combination escalating since knee pain, and an antihistamine sleep aid purchased OTC. Cognitive testing: borderline, worse in the evening. Formulation: medication-use disorder and toxicity amplifying (possibly causing) the cognitive complaints, not yet a dementia diagnosis. Management: consolidation of prescribers to one; staged taper of the long-acting benzodiazepine (10% per fortnight with the daughter holding the strips); opioid switch to scheduled paracetamol with pain-review; stop the OTC sedative; re-test cognition at 8 weeks: improved by one education-equivalent. Teaching points: the bag-in-the-container rule exposes adherence, duplication and abuse at once; geriatric "memory loss" is a medication review before it is a scan; taper one drug at a time.

## 13. FAQ

**"Isn't drinking a bit at his age just one of his few pleasures?"**: The risk-benefit genuinely changes with age: light drinking may even carry some protection, but the harm threshold falls (body water, metabolism, interactions, frailty), and one-drink-a-day guidance is not puritanism, it's pharmacokinetics.
**"She only takes her tablets as prescribed, how can that be a disorder?"**: Medication use disorder in the elderly is mostly *prescribed*: years of renewals, several prescribers, interactions, and effects (confusion, falls) at "correct" doses. The bag review settles it.
**"He's 78, is treatment even worth it?"**: Yes: older people do at least as well in treatment as younger people, and the late-onset group (one in three) often has the best prognosis of all when the trigger (grief, isolation, illness) is addressed.
**"Grandmother takes a sleeping tablet every night for years and seems fine."**: Long-term benzodiazepines raise falls, confusion and dependence in the elderly, especially the long-acting kinds; a slow, supervised taper with replacement sleep strategies is safer than a decade more of the same.
**"Could her confusion just be old age?"**: New confusion is never just old age: screen for substances (alcohol, sedatives, anticholinergics) alongside infection, metabolic and drug causes before accepting a dementia label.
**"What if he gets withdrawal shakes if he stops?"**: Withdrawal is treatable and older people can do it safely: low, measured doses of the right benzodiazepine, guided by a severity scale, with thiamine on board, usually at home with family supervision.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **The threshold principle**: pharmacokinetics + comorbidity + interactions → problems at lower intake; NIAAA ≤1 drink/day; population limits inappropriately high.
- **The prevalence ladder (2-4 / 16 / 14 / 18 / 23%)**, community, loose criteria, ED, nursing home, psychiatric inpatient.
- **Late-onset AUDs = one-third**, triggered by bereavement, isolation, retirement, illness, the classic vignette setup.
- **The underdiagnosis table**: patient (won't volunteer, can't recall, atypical features), service (don't ask, don't refer, wrong tools, therapeutic pessimism), family/society (don't perceive, ageism makes it "understandable", less noisy).
- **Withdrawal pharmacology**: lorazepam safest (metabolism unaffected by age/liver; predictable IM absorption); CIWA-Ar thresholds <10 / 10–20 / >20; oral thiamine = parenteral (emergency setting).
- **Relapse pharmacology**: naltrexone + acamprosate yes; disulfiram no.
- **MUD numbers**: 13% of population / >30% of prescriptions / 35% of OTC; 17% community benzodiazepine prevalence (Irish study); female excess; 52% long-acting.
- **Indian exam corner**: bereavement-triggered late-onset AUD vignette; the tablet-bag review before dementia workup; CIWA-guided home withdrawal with family supervision.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**Q1.** The primary reason population "safe drinking" limits are inappropriately high for older people is:
- (a) Older people hide their drinking better
- (b) Pharmacokinetic changes (higher fat-to-lean ratio, reduced metabolic efficiency), comorbidity, and drug interactions make the same intake more toxic
- (c) Doctors dislike elderly drinkers
- (d) The limits were designed for women only

**Answer: (b).** The threshold problem: distribution, metabolism and reserve all shift, so harm arrives at lower intake; one drink per day is the appropriate geriatric ceiling.

**Q2.** Approximately what proportion of older people with alcohol use disorders develop them for the first time in later life?
- (a) 1 in 100
- (b) 1 in 10
- (c) 1 in 3
- (d) All of them

**Answer: (c).** One-third are late-onset, typically bereavement- or illness-triggered, with neuroticism and depression rather than the antisocial pattern of early-onset AUD.

**Q3.** In a nursing-home population, the expected prevalence of alcohol use disorders is approximately:
- (a) 1%
- (b) 5%
- (c) 18%
- (d) 50%

**Answer: (c).** The setting ladder: community 2–4%, ED 14%, nursing home 18%, psychiatric inpatients 23%.

**Q4.** The benzodiazepine identified as the safest choice for alcohol withdrawal in older people, because ageing and liver disease barely affect its metabolism, is:
- (a) Diazepam
- (b) Lorazepam
- (c) Chlordiazepoxide
- (d) Nitrazepam

**Answer: (b).** Lorazepam's metabolism is unaffected by age and hepatic impairment and its IM absorption is predictable; chlordiazepoxide retains the broader clinical experience.

**Q5.** Which relapse-prevention medication is best avoided in older people?
- (a) Naltrexone
- (b) Acamprosate
- (c) Disulfiram
- (d) Thiamine

**Answer: (c).** Disulfiram's limited efficacy and more severe side-effect profile make it unsuitable; naltrexone and acamprosate are the suggested agents.

**Q6.** The instruction "bring all your medications in their containers" to an older patient serves, in one act, to:
- (a) Test memory only
- (b) Verify the medication list, reveal adherence levels, and expose duplication, OTC use and potential misuse
- (c) Charge the patient for the visit
- (d) Satisfy pharmacy regulations

**Answer: (b).** The container audit is the core MUD assessment, listing prescriptions with indications while reading the reality of compliance and accumulation.
