# Bereavement & Complicated Grief

## 1. In one line + overview

**Grief is the healthy, necessary work of rebuilding a life around a loss, it hurts by design, moves in waves not stages, and in most people it slowly finds its place; prolonged grief disorder is the name for the minority whose grief stays frozen in its first intensity, disabling them for months and years.**

Losing a parent, a spouse, a child rearranges a person from the inside: identity, daily routine, future plans, even appetite and sleep are all built around someone who is now absent. Grief is the rebuilding process, and it is supposed to hurt, oscillate, and take longer than anyone around the griever expects. The clinical task is mostly to protect the process: reassure families that waves of sorrow at six months are normal, protect sleep and nutrition, keep the bereaved connected, and honour the rituals that cultures evolved precisely to carry this work. But grief can also go wrong in two directions, and both need real treatment: it can freeze into **prolonged grief disorder** (the person remains as shattered at the first anniversary as on the first day; ICD-11 since 2019, DSM-5-TR since 2022), or it can mask/mate with **major depression** (a different illness needing its own treatment). Indian mourning culture (the 13-day rites, the terahvin, the feeding of others, the white clothes) is a sophisticated, community-delivered grief programme that modern evidence largely validates; the clinician's job is to work with it, not around it.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Describe normal grief's true shape (waves, tasks, continuing bonds, not tidy stages)
- Differentiate normal grief, prolonged grief disorder, and grief-masking depression with confidence
- State the DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 duration gates for prolonged grief disorder
- Use the dual-process model to explain to families when grieving and when resting are both correct
- Recognise disenfranchised grief and high-risk bereavements (child loss, suicide loss, stigmatised deaths)
- Deliver grief counselling principles; know when complicated-grief therapy and antidepressants enter
- Harness Indian mourning rituals as clinical allies
- Screen bereaved children, elderly widows, and suicide-bereaved families for the complications that matter

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Everyone loves; roughly 5–10% of bereaved people develop prolonged grief disorder (higher after child loss, spouse loss in late life, and violent/untimely deaths). Prevalence figures vary by instrument and gate, 2–4% at population level for death-related prolonged grief, with much higher conditional rates after children's deaths (roughly 1 in 5 in some series). DSM-5-TR adopted 12 months as the diagnosis gate for adults; ICD-11 uses 6 months. Bereavement itself carries transiently elevated all-cause mortality (the "dying of a broken heart" has a real substrate, stress cardiomyopathy peaks in the early widowhood weeks) and elevated suicide risk, with elderly widowers the classic high-risk group.

**India.** No dedicated national prevalence data (NMHS 2015–16 did not measure grief). The Indian framing must be different: the country has one of the richest ritual-mourning infrastructures in the world, and the majority of Indian bereavement is processed through it, community presence, structured rites with roles for the griever, food and sleep imposed by relatives, and a graded return to ordinary life. The Indian complications instead cluster at the social fractures: deaths without rites possible (COVID-19's isolated hospital deaths and cremation queues produced a documented wave of frozen grief: Indian psychiatry departments reported persistent caseloads for years), suicide-bereaved families facing stigma and blame, and disenfranchised losses (miscarriage, cohabiting partners, same-sex partners, estranged sons) where no ritual or recognition exists for the griever.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**The loss (definitional):** death of an attachment figure. The relationship's texture matters more than the category: dependent, ambivalent ("I never reconciled with him"), or identity-defining relationships break harder.

**Which bereavements go complicated:**
- Death of a child (the strongest predictor across all literatures)
- Sudden, violent, untimely deaths (accident, suicide, disaster), especially with the trauma layer of finding/witnessing
- Deaths with guilt or unfinished business, "I insisted on discharge that night"
- Highly dependent or ambivalent attachments
- Prior mental illness (depression history), prior complicated grief, insecure attachment patterns
- Disenfranchised grief: losses society does not recognise or permit (stigmatised deaths, secret relationships, estrangement, pregnancy loss)
- Weak support: isolated migrants who cannot attend rites (an Indian-specific trap: the tech worker who misses his father's terahvin because the visa came too late)
- For children: unexplained and unsupported losses; children grief in bursts, are failed by silence

**Protective:** rituals with roles, one confidant, meaning frameworks (religious/philosophical), and being needed (a task, a dependent, a community role) in the first months.

## 6. What happens in the mind/brain

**Story 1: the amputation phantom.** A spouse of 40 years is not a person you knew; they are a load-bearing wall in the architecture of your day. Remove the wall and the whole house shifts, the tea made for two, the side of the bed, the name you will never call out again without an answer. Grief is the house learning to stand differently, and phantom sensations are part of the work: sensing the person's presence, hearing the scooter at the gate. These are normal in the first months, misread as "hallucinations of illness" by frightened families, and over-pathologised by junior clinicians.

**Story 2: the oscillating labourer.** The dual-process model pictures the griever alternating between two rooms: the **loss room** (confronting the pain, sorting the belongings, crying the memories) and the **restoration room** (bank work, cooking, jokes with a cousin, a nap). Healthy grief OSCILLATES. Pathology has two shapes: permanent residence in the loss room (prolonged grief) and permanent flight into the restoration room (masked, postponed grief that often erupts years later in somatic form). Families benefit from this map more than any advice: "she laughs at a wedding" is not disrespect; it is the oscillation working.

**Story 3: the unfiled and the unspoken.** When the death is sudden and violent, grief shares an engine with PTSD: the memory of the death itself is hot and intrusive. When the death is stigmatised or secret, the grief is disenfranchised; there is no room, no ritual, no listener, so the work cannot run. Indian clinical experience: the migrant who missed the rites, the COVID family who never saw the face, the suicide-adjacent in-laws trading blame; each carries the loss with no machinery to process it. Complicated-grief therapy's logic is to finally run the work that never ran: retelling, approaching the avoided, restoring roles.

## 7. Symptoms

### Normal grief: the honest picture (warn families it is messier than the pamphlets)
- **Waves**: sudden sobbing at the mangoes he liked, triggered, arriving and passing, distinct from depression's constant flat grey
- **Physical**: chest tightness, hollow stomach, appetite and sleep disturbance (weeks, not months of collapse)
- **Sensing presence**: feeling him in the kitchen, dreams that feel like visits (comforting, usually welcomed)
- **Preoccupation with the deceased**: wanting to talk about him repeatedly; this is the work, not a symptom
- **Guilt in normal proportions**: "could I have been there more", modifiable by facts and comfort, not the global self-condemnation of depression
- **Role rebuilding**: learning the accounts, the ration card, the driving; restoration-room work
- **Continuing bonds**: rituals of connection (the tulsi water offered, the favourite dish cooked on the death anniversary) healthy lifelong practices in most Indian families

### Prolonged grief disorder: the frozen picture
- Identity-level absorption: "a part of me died with her"; disbelief the death is real, months and years on
- Intense yearning/longing daily, at the same rawness as the early weeks
- Preoccupation with the deceased or the circumstances of death that crowds out function
- Avoidance of reminders OR the opposite, shrine-level fixation with no other life resuming
- Intense sorrow, bitterness, guilt that does not soften, feeling life is meaningless since the death ("the world ended for me")
- Inability to trust, emotional numbness, feeling detached from the living
- Marked impairment (work, self-care, relationships) beyond cultural norms for the mourning period
- **DSM-5-TR gate: ≥ 12 months since the death (adults), ≥ 6 months (children/adolescents); ICD-11 gate: ≥ 6 months**
- Beyond cultural-bereavement norms is the comparative clause: the Indian clinician calibrates against what this family's culture would expect at this point

### Grief-masking major depression: the masquerade that needs medicine
- Persistent anhedonia about EVERYTHING (not only the loss domain); the wedding laughter is gone too
- Worthlessness and self-loathing beyond guilt about the death ("I am a worthless person", not "I should have driven him myself")
- Neurovegetative collapse that does not ripple but flattens: months of 3-hour sleep, 10% weight loss, psychomotor slowing
- Suicidal ideation of the "join him" or "escape" kind, a red line demanding depression-level care
- Psychotic features (guilt-laden, nihilistic): an emergency
- Failure to function at ALL at any point (even the restoration room is empty)

## 8. Diagnosis

**The logic.** Grief itself is not a disorder; diagnose nothing, support the process. When the picture freezes (intensity of yearning/identity absorption persists past the ICD-11 6-month or DSM-5-TR 12-month gate, with functional collapse beyond cultural norms), name it prolonged grief disorder and treat it. When the picture meets major-depression criteria, treat depression regardless of the death's recency. When trauma-features dominate (intrusion of the death scene, avoidance, arousal; threat-level traumatic death), treat the PTSD component too; complicated grief after violent deaths is often a hybrid.

**The interview craft:**
- "Tell me about him/her", and let the silence do its share
- Map the oscillation: "When you did the bank work, how did it go afterwards?": the person who cannot enter the loss room AND the person who cannot leave it both tell you the work is jammed
- Quantify function, not tears: eating, weight, sleep architecture, work attendance, self-care; function is the diagnostic railway
- Direct suicide screen for the bereaved (see the Suicide note), especially widowers and suicide-bereaved parents
- Instruments by name: the PG-13 / Prolonged Grief Disorder-13 scale and the ICD-11 Traumatic Grief Inventory; PHQ-9 as the depression-gate measure (scores used, not diagnose-by)

### Differential table

| Picture | Read it as |
|---|---|
| Waves, triggers, presence-sensing, function returning, guilt proportionate | Normal grief: reassure and protect the process |
| Constant flat grey + anhedonia for all + worthlessness + vegetative collapse | Major depression: treat as depression, medicine and all |
| Frozen first-day intensity past the gate, yearning/identity absorption, function collapsed | Prolonged grief disorder: grief-specific therapy |
| Intrusions of the death scene, hypervigilance, startle after violent death | PTSD component alongside grief: treat both |
| Somatic storm years after unprocessed loss (postponed grief) | Masked grief: unmask gently, run the work |
| Manic or psychotic flare within weeks of loss | Underlying bipolar/psychosis unmasked by grief: urgent care |

## 9. Management

**For normal grief: protect the process (the prescription is a programme, not a pill):**
- Legitimise the waves and the timetable: "the second year is often harder than the first" is the single most-reassuring true sentence in grief medicine (the first year is run on shock, relatives, and first-anniversary rituals; the second runs on her alone)
- Sleep, food, movement guarded by the family, assign ONE relative to each
- Roles and tasks: the bereaved do better when needed (the account work, a grandchild's homework, the temple duty rotation)
- Ritual participation encouraged, not forced; continuing bonds honoured
- The GP/family-physician check at 6–8 weeks and around the first anniversary (the anniversaries (birthday, death day, festival) are predictable flare windows)

**For prolonged grief disorder, grief-specific treatment:**
- **Complicated grief therapy (Shear)**, the best-tested protocol: elements of exposure (retelling the death story, approaching avoided places/belongings), restoration work (goal-setting, re-engagement), and memory-evoked emotional connection; 16 sessions typical
- Grief-focused CBT variants: equivalent in spirit; the Indian district psychologist can deliver the structure after training (DMHP/NIMHANS modules)
- Support groups: suicide-bereaved groups, widows' networks, parents' circles, unmatched for disenfranchised grief
- **Medication, the honest position:** SSRIs treat the depressive component and global distress, but do NOT specifically dissolve grief; grief-specific therapy outperforms antidepressants for the core syndrome. Say this plainly to families who arrive asking for "tablets to stop the pain": the pain is the work, and we can help the work run.

**For grief-masking depression:** treat as depression (see Depressive Disorders note), antidepressants, and ECT for psychotic-level presentations. Do not let "she is grieving" delay treatment of a treatable depression by a year.

**For bereaved children:** truth in age-shaped doses (euphemisms like "went to sleep" breed fear of sleep), routines preserved, one grieving adult who can talk, school informed; bursts of play between sobbing are normal. Watch for: school collapse, regression persisting, somatic complaints as their grief dialect.

*India note: the ritual calendar IS the treatment architecture for most families, the 13 days of imposed company and roles, the terahvin's formal closing, the sraddha/annual rites that institutionalise continuing bonds, the first festival re-entry. Where the clinician adds value: (1) families who ask "how long should she grieve?": answer with the oscillation map, not a number; (2) COVID-era bereaved without rites, offer a constructed substitute (a delayed 13-day observance, a family letter ritual, feeding guests as she would have), delayed rites work because the work never ran, not because the date matters; (3) costs: grief-specific therapy private ≈ ₹600–1,500/session, DMHP/district psychologists nominal; antidepressants per the Depressive note's India ledger (approx 2026).*

## 10. Indian practice

- **Read the ritual map of the family in front of you.** Hindu families vary regionally (13-day/terahvin in the north, shorter observances and different feeding rules elsewhere); Muslim families observe the 3-day iddat-based mourning with 40-day markers; Christian families hold novenas and month's-mind masses; Sikh families the path recitations. Asking "what have your family's observances been?" is both diagnosis and treatment: the answer maps their support structure and the griever's role in it.
- **The missed-rites migrant.** Indian grief complications increasingly involve the NRI/interstate worker who reached after the cremation, the frozen-work cohort of our era. Offer constructed rites: a delayed observance with community (temple/gurudwara/mosque will schedule one), a written-letter ritual, a feeding of guests. Do not dismiss it as "just symbolic"; symbolism is the machinery.
- **Suicide-bereaved families.** Stigma and blame (in-law fault-finding is a recurring pattern) suppress the work; the family needs an early normalisation contact, and children need to be told the truth in protected doses. See the Suicide note's bereavement section.
- **Widow health in late life.** Indian elderly widows carry elevated risks for depression, malnutrition, and abandonment to ashrams/children's households; the geriatric review should ask the three function questions and weigh her. Widowers' suicide risk: the under-recognised twin.
- **Pregnancy and child loss.** Disenfranchised in much of India ("you can have another"); the parents' grief is real and long; neonatal units' bereavement-care programmes (seeing, naming, memory-making) are evidence-based and under-used. Miscarriage grief likewise: name it, schedule the review.
- **The "she is not crying at all" alarm.** Two readings: the protected strength-position of the eldest son who must run the rites (give him a scheduled breakdown slot (the barber's touch, the last flower) and someone watching him after day 13), or masked/postponed grief. Distinguish by function over weeks.
- **Medical funerals of the future.** Pandemic lessons learned: hospitals should have a bereavement protocol (viewing where possible, a staff member who speaks, no paperwork ambushes at the exit). Write for your institution: it is mental-health prevention at source.

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: the son who ran everything.** A 41-year-old eldest son in Jaipur ran his father's entire 13-day funeral flawlessly, priests, feeding a hundred relatives daily, bank and property paperwork on day 10. On day 16 he developed insomnia, chest tightness, and panic at the father's scooter keys; by month 3 he was functioning at work but sleeping 4 hours, avoiding the father's room, and "grieving wrong; I have not cried once, I must not have loved him enough". Assessment: grief frozen in the restoration room; the work never ran because he was the ritual administrator. Intervention: referral to the district psychologist for grief-focused work (scheduled loss-room time, the room re-entered with his younger brother, the scooter eventually donated with a family ceremony); sleep bridge for 10 nights. By month 9: two full crying sessions ("the work finally ran"), sleep restored, function full. Teaching points: (1) the competent administrator of rites is a high-risk griever, not a protected one; (2) "not crying" can mean the work is scheduled, not absent; (3) behavioural re-entry (the room, the scooter) unlocked what reassurance could not.

**Case 2: the COVID widow at year three.** A 58-year-old Ludhiana teacher lost her husband in the 2021 wave: he was taken by ambulance, admitted through the emergency of a hospital she could not enter, and died unseen eleven days later; the cremation was attended by five people with a time slot. At year 3 she remained unable to enter the bedroom, daily "he will walk in any moment" expectations, teaching on autopilot, 5-hour sleep, 6 kg lost, and bitter refusal of all rituals ("what rituals, nobody came"). Assessment: prolonged grief disorder (well past every gate) with a trauma component (the unseen death). Intervention: grief-specific therapy with retelling of the constructed last days (hospital records requested and read together with the therapist, fact reconstruction), a family-constructed substitute rite at year 4 with feeding guests and a delayed observance, SSRI for the depressive layer, son as the assigned food/sleep guardian. At 18 months of follow-up: bedroom re-entered, teaching with presence again, annual sraddha observed. Teaching points: (1) deaths without rites manufacture prolonged grief at industrial scale; the machinery was missing; (2) constructed/delayed rites are legitimate medicine; (3) fact-reconstruction retelling replaces the missing death-scene knowledge.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way patients and families ask it

**"How long does grief last? When should we worry?"** There is no expiry date, only a direction: healthy grief keeps moving, waves, but the sea between waves slowly widens. As a rough Indian-cultural compass: waves are expected in the whole first year, anniversaries flare yearly, and the scent of it stays lifelong. What we watch for medically is FREEZING, the same rawness at the first anniversary as the first day, sleep and weight still failing, life still stopped.

**"Is it normal that he still talks to her photograph?"** Yes, and it may stay healthy for life. Keeping an inner relationship with the dead (the tulsi water, the favourite dish, the photograph spoken to) is called continuing bonds, and it is what most healthy grievers do worldwide. It is illness only when the bond crowds out the living.

**"She was fine at the function, then cried all night. Is she getting worse?"** That is grief's actual shape, waves, not a slope. The oscillation between facing the loss and living the life is the healthy mechanism itself. Worry, rather, about the person who cannot do either of the two rooms.

**"Doctor, give her something for the sadness."** The sadness is not a malfunction to switch off; it is the mind doing the work. Medicines help when the picture turns into depression (sleep and appetite collapse, worthlessness, no laughter anywhere); that is different and treatable. For grief itself, the treatment is people, rituals, time, and sometimes structured grief therapy.

**"The priest said the terahvin must close the mourning. She is still crying after it."** Rituals mark the community's arc, not the heart's. Indian observances close the formal mourning period, and many families find the waves persisting for months after, which is normal. If year one still feels like week one at the anniversary, come to us; that specific frozenness has a name and a treatment now.

**"He has started drinking since his brother died."** That is the route Indian men often take, alcohol as a nightly anaesthetic that strips the deep sleep which processes loss. Grief plus alcohol is the commonest escalation we see in bereaved men; bring him in on the drinking itself, and the grief work follows.

**"My son died by suicide. People say such families are punished."** Nothing about your family is being punished. His death was the outcome of an illness of unbearable pain, not of anything you did or failed to do, and the shame that silence builds is the second injury. Grief after suicide is the hardest grief, and it needs the most support; we have people trained specifically in it.

**"She is 78 and a widow now. Should we shift her to her son's house in another city immediately?"** Not urgently. Identity, temple, neighbours and routine are her load-bearing walls; stripping them all at once adds relocation loss to attachment loss. A staged plan (visits first, winter stays, then deciding) protects her better than a heroic rescue.

**"The baby died at 7 months of pregnancy. Everyone says 'forget it, you will have another'."** This loss was a person to you, and your grief deserves its own name and time. The family's minimising is the reason we schedule a review for parents like you, come at six weeks, tell us about her, and we will watch the grief run its course properly.

**"We never did the rites properly, the lockdown, the queues. Is that why she cannot move on?"** Yes, in part: that is one of the clearest findings of these years. Rites are the machinery that runs the work; when they are impossible, the work jams. The good news is the machinery can be run late: delayed observances, feeding guests, the letter ritual; whenever the family is ready, the work can still run.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**Gates to memorise:** ICD-11 prolonged grief disorder ≥ 6 months; DSM-5-TR ≥ 12 months after the death in adults (≥ 6 months in children/adolescents). "Beyond cultural norms" is the comparative clause both systems use, cite the family's own mourning frame in your written answers.

**Models to name in theory answers:** Kübler-Ross (historical stages, quote it, then critique it: grief does not run in fixed stages); Worden's tasks of mourning; **Stroebe & Schut's dual-process model** (the modern exam favourite); continuing bonds (Klass et al.); attachment theory (Bowlby/Parkes lineage).

**High-yield facts:**
- Prolonged grief conditional rates: highest after child death (up to ~20% in some series) and violent death
- Grief-specific therapy (Shear's CGT) outperforms antidepressants for the core syndrome; SSRIs treat the depressive component
- Elderly widowers = classic elevated-suicide-risk group; early widowhood weeks = stress-cardiomyopathy window ("broken heart" has an ICD code)
- Death of a parent by natural causes is NOT, by itself, DSM-5 trauma; the violent/witnessed death can be
- Sensing the dead's presence in early grief is normal, not psychotic; an exam trap worth three marks

**Classic traps:**
1. Writing "major depression" because the person cries at 2 months; waves with preserved function are grief
2. Writing "normal grief" for the 2-year frozen picture with weight loss, missed prolonged grief disorder
3. Prescribing antidepressants for grief per se: the honest position is component-treatment
4. Treating presence-sensing as psychosis
5. Missing the alcohol in the bereaved man
6. Forgetting the direct suicide screen in widowers

**Indian exam corner:** "normal grief vs pathological grief; differentiate" (evergreen), "prolonged grief disorder, the new diagnosis (ICD-11/DSM-5-TR), criteria logic", "cultural factors in Indian mourning and their psychiatric significance", "complicated grief therapy, principles". The COVID-19 unritualised deaths are the modern Indian-context viva vignette.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. In a 58-year-old widow 14 months after her husband's death, the finding most specific for PROLONGED GRIEF DISORDER rather than normal grief is:**
A. Crying when passing his favourite shop
B. Daily intense yearning and identity-level preoccupation at first-day intensity, with function still collapsed
C. Dreams of him
D. Sadness on the anniversary
**Answer: B.** Frozen intensity + functional collapse beyond the gate, the two-hallmark picture.

**2. The ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR duration gates for prolonged grief disorder are:**
A. 3 months both
B. 6 months (ICD-11); 12 months in adults per DSM-5-TR
C. 12 months both
D. 24 months both
**Answer: B.** Know both systems, a favourite "compare ICD vs DSM" question.

**3. The feature that tips a grieving picture toward MAJOR DEPRESSION needing medication is:**
A. Triggered waves of crying
B. Presence-sensing experiences
C. Persistent anhedonia for ALL domains + worthlessness + neurovegetative collapse
D. Talking about the deceased daily
**Answer: C.** Global anhedonia and worthlessness belong to depression, not grief.

**4. Complicated grief therapy's core components are:**
A. Hypnosis and age regression
B. Retelling/approaching the avoided + restoration and goal re-engagement
C. Long-term supportive listening without agenda
D. Family scapegoat resolution
**Answer: B.** The exposure-plus-restoration structure (Shear).

**5. Sensing the presence of the deceased in early grief should be:**
A. Treated as first-rank psychosis
B. Reassured as a normal experience of early grief
C. Referred for ECT
D. Managed with antipsychotics PRN
**Answer: B.** Normal in the first months; a classic misdiagnosis trap.

**6. The Indian clinical situation most strongly associated with prolonged grief in the current era is:**
A. Terahvin observance
B. Unritualised COVID-era deaths (unseen deaths, restricted cremations)
C. Feeding guests on the death anniversary
D. Annual sraddha rites
**Answer: B.** The missing-machinery cohort; rites in the other options are protective.

**7. Among bereaved groups, the classic elevated-SUICIDE-risk demographic is:**
A. Young widows
B. Elderly widowers
C. Bereaved teenagers
D. Granddaughters
**Answer: B.** Elderly widowers carry the long-recognised risk; screen directly.

**8. The dual-process model describes grief as oscillation between:**
A. Anger and bargaining
B. Loss-orientation and restoration-orientation
C. Denial and acceptance
D. Attachment and detachment
**Answer: B.** Stroebe & Schut, and the "she laughed at the wedding" reassurance flows from it.
