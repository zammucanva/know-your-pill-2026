# Schizophrenia

## 1. In one line + overview

**Tagline:** Schizophrenia is a long-term, treatable brain illness that blurs the line between what is real and what is not, while quietly draining energy, drive and social life.

**Overview, in plain words:** Schizophrenia affects roughly **1 in 300 people** at any given time, about 24 million people worldwide, of whom an estimated 4–10 million are in India. It usually begins between **ages 15 and 30**, earlier in men than women. It is a disorder of the brain's signalling, not a character flaw, not "madness", and, despite the misleading name (Greek for "split mind", meaning a split *from reality*), it is **not split personality**; the split-personality condition is a different, rare illness called dissociative identity disorder. With early antipsychotic treatment, family support and psychological care, most people improve substantially, and many study, work and marry like anyone else. The three biggest enemies of recovery are **delayed treatment, stopping medicines too soon, and cannabis**. In India, most day-to-day care happens at home, so this note treats the family as part of the treatment team.

## 2. Learning objectives

After working through this note, you should be able to:

1. Define schizophrenia in one sentence and correct the "split personality" myth with confidence.
2. Sort symptoms into **positive, negative and cognitive** groups, and explain why the grouping changes treatment choices.
3. Explain the dopamine story (and the glutamate add-on) in under a minute each.
4. State the DSM-5 duration rules (**1 month active phase, 6 months total illness**) and how ICD-11 differs.
5. List the first-episode workup and the five most common differential diagnoses.
6. Choose, dose and monitor a first-line antipsychotic, and state when clozapine becomes the answer.
7. Name the three cheapest relapse-prevention tools available anywhere in India.

## 4. Epidemiology

### Global

- Lifetime prevalence about **0.3–0.7%** (most syntheses land near 1 in 200); point prevalence about 0.2–0.4%.
- Men and women are affected equally overall, but men present earlier (peak **18–25**) and women later (**25–35**, with a second, smaller peak after 45).
- Mortality is **2–3 times** the general population; average **10–20 years of life lost**, mostly from heart disease, diabetes and smoking, not from psychosis itself.
- About **5% die by suicide**, and 25–50% attempt at least once. Asking about suicidal thoughts at every visit is part of the treatment, not an optional extra.

### India

- Indian meta-analyses converge on about **3–5 per 1,000 (0.3–0.5%)**, essentially the same as global rates. The old teaching that schizophrenia is rarer in India is wrong; what differs is *detection*, not frequency.
- The real Indian problem is the **treatment gap**: half or more of people with psychosis never reach a psychiatrist. The National Mental Health Survey 2015–16 found overall treatment gaps of 70–92% across mental disorders.
- Duration of **untreated psychosis (DUP)** commonly exceeds 6–12 months, usually because the first stops are faith healers and general physicians.
- Encouragingly, the classic WHO international follow-up studies (IPSS, ISoS) found **better-than-average outcomes at Indian centres**; credited to family caregiving, preserved work roles and social inclusion. Treat that as encouraging, not guaranteed.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

No single cause. Think of a vulnerable brain meeting a loaded environment, usually in late adolescence.

**Genetic (the strongest single factor):**
- Heritability around **80%** in twin studies. Risk rises in steps: general population ~0.5%; one affected parent or sibling ~10%; identical twin ~40–50%; both parents affected ~45%.
- Risk is polygenic (spread across hundreds of common variants), plus rare copy-number variants. **22q11.2 deletion syndrome** carries the highest single genetic risk; roughly a quarter develop psychosis by adulthood.

**Biological:**
- Obstetric complications and birth hypoxia; advanced paternal age; prenatal infection or inflammation; neurodevelopmental "soft signs" (minor motor and sensory oddities) visible from childhood.

**Psychological:**
- Childhood trauma and bullying roughly double-to-triple risk and worsen the course; schizotypal traits run in families.

**Social:**
- Urban birth and upbringing (dose-response with city size); migration (2–3x, strongest for visible minorities in unwelcoming host societies); social isolation; unemployment and poverty.
- **Expressed emotion (EE)**: households high in criticism, hostility or emotional over-involvement roughly double relapse rates; the most modifiable social factor we know.

**Environmental:**
- **Cannabis** is the strongest avoidable risk: about 2x for any use, **3–4x for daily, high-potency use starting in the teens**.
- Stimulants (amphetamine, cocaine) can trigger psychosis; significant head injury adds some risk.

**Indian context:**
- Market cannabis is getting stronger (high-THC, seedless varieties) in metros, the same shift that raised psychosis rates in European cities.
- Care pathways run through temples and folk healers first; this delays treatment by months and should be engaged respectfully, never mocked, or families stop coming back.
- Joint families cut both ways: close monitoring spots relapse early, but criticism and over-control ("just snap out of it") raise relapse risk.
- Vegetarian diets make **vitamin B12 deficiency** common; it worsens fatigue and can mimic negative symptoms. Check and treat.
- In many districts, **neurosyphilis and HIV** still belong on a first-episode differential.

## 6. What happens in the brain

Four short stories explain almost everything you need clinically.

**Story 1: Dopamine (the engine of positive symptoms).** Dopamine is the brain's "this matters, act now" signal. Too much of it in the mesolimbic circuit (deep limbic reward pathway) makes the brain flag irrelevant things as urgent: a stranger's cough feels like a message about you; the brain's own inner speech gets mislabelled as coming from outside and becomes a voice. Antipsychotics block D2 receptors and calm exactly these symptoms. But the same drugs also block dopamine in the mesocortical circuit (to the prefrontal cortex), which is already *under*-active in schizophrenia, which is why cranking the dose higher can deepen negative symptoms and cause stiffness. Clinical rule: **enough D2 blockade, not maximal.**

**Story 2: Glutamate (the missing half).** PCP and ketamine, which block NMDA-type glutamate receptors, produce the full schizophrenia-like picture in healthy volunteers, hallucinations *plus* negative *plus* cognitive symptoms, a fuller mimicry than any dopamine drug. Low NMDA signalling on inhibitory interneurons lets cortical activity become "noisy", which explains disorganised thinking and poor memory. It also explains why clozapine (which touches glutamate and several other systems, not just D2) can succeed where pure D2 blockers failed.

**Story 3: Neurodevelopment (why this age, why this person).** Genes plus early insults build a brain that develops slightly differently. Adolescence then prunes synapses aggressively; in a vulnerable brain this pruning over-trims useful connections, and the illness declares itself. That is why onset clusters at 15–30, why brain scans show slightly larger ventricles and thinner cortex from the very first episode, and why "he was odd and quiet as a child" is so often in the history.

**Story 4: Salience (how a delusion is born).** The brain runs an "importance alarm". In psychosis it misfires (aberrant salience), so trivial events feel loaded with personal meaning. The mind, doing its job, constructs an explanation for that feeling, and the delusion *is* the explanation. This is why logical argument rarely works, and why medicine plus gentle CBT works better than debate.

**The summary line:** dopamine excess = positive symptoms; cortical dopamine deficit and glutamate noise = negative and cognitive symptoms; a decades-long developmental trajectory = why this person, at this age, under this stress.

## 7. Symptoms

### Positive symptoms: things *added* that should not be there

- **Hallucinations.** Hearing voices is most common (60–75%). Typical patterns: a voice **commenting** on actions like a running commentary ("now he is locking the door"), two or more voices **arguing about** the person, or thoughts heard aloud. Vision, smell, touch and taste hallucinations occur but should raise suspicion of an organic cause.
- **Delusions.** Persecutory are commonest ("I am being followed"). Referential delusions make TV, strangers or songs feel like personal signals. **Passivity phenomena** feel the most alien: thoughts inserted, withdrawn or broadcast, and feelings or actions "made" by an outside force. Schneider's "first-rank symptoms" (this family of experiences) used to be a fast lane to diagnosis in ICD-10; DSM-5 and ICD-11 no longer give them special status, but examiners still love them.
- **Disorganised thinking** shows in speech: derailment (answers slide onto an unrelated track), loosened connections, made-up words. If you cannot follow the thread of a sentence, you are already describing it.
- **Disorganised or catatonic behaviour:** agitated, childlike or ritualised behaviour, or the stillness of **catatonia**, mutism, posturing, waxy flexibility. Catatonia responds to lorazepam and, if needed, ECT; remembering this saves lives.

### Negative symptoms, things *subtracted*: the five A's

| Domain | What the family sees |
|--------|---------------------|
| Blunted **Affect** | face still, voice flat, little smiling or crying |
| **Alogia** | short, empty answers; conversation dries up |
| **Avolition** | no drive to start anything; sits for hours |
| **Anhedonia** | nothing feels enjoyable anymore |
| **Asociality** | avoids friends; drops WhatsApp groups; keeps the room dark |

These affect up to 60% of patients, respond least to dopamine-blocking medicines, and are routinely mistaken for laziness, attitude or depression, by families *and* by doctors. Being the one person who says "this is the illness, not his character" changes the household.

### Cognitive symptoms: the hidden core

Problems with **attention, working memory, processing speed and planning**. They predict work and study outcomes better than voices or delusions do, they are often present for years before the first psychotic episode, and they barely improve with standard antipsychotics. This is why "treat the voices" is only half the treatment plan.

### Mood, insight and safety

Depression affects roughly a quarter after psychotic episodes; anxiety is common; insight is usually absent early ("there is nothing wrong with me" is part of the illness, not stubbornness). Smoking is strikingly over-represented (60–70%+). **Command hallucinations** telling the person to harm themselves or others are a safety emergency; ask directly.

### How it unfolds: prodrome → first episode → course

For 1–2 years before the first clear episode, most patients show a **prodrome**: falling grades or work performance, social withdrawal, sleep upset, odd or over-personal interpretations of events. Then the active phase declares itself. The classic rough prognosis thirds still teach well: about **one-third do well, one-third relapse and recover between episodes, one-third remain significantly impaired**, and modern early treatment plus Indian family caregiving shifts people upward through those thirds. **Every month of untreated psychosis at the start worsens the long-term outcome**, so shortening DUP (duration of untreated psychosis) is a genuine treatment priority, not a slogan.

## 8. Diagnosis

**The one rule: diagnosis is clinical**, history plus mental state examination, tracked over time, with collateral from family. No blood test, scan or EEG confirms schizophrenia; investigations exist to *exclude mimics*.

### The DSM-5 logic (paraphrased, not quoted)

The DSM-5 asks, in sequence: are there **two or more** characteristic symptoms (delusions, hallucinations, disorganised speech, disorganised or catatonic behaviour, negative symptoms), **at least one from the first three**? Was the active phase present for **a month or more**, and has the whole disturbance lasted **six months or more**? Did functioning decline? And is nothing else better positioned to explain it, a mood disorder, a substance, a medical illness? All yes = schizophrenia.

### The ICD-11 logic (and ICD-10)

ICD asks for clear psychotic symptoms for **at least one month**, without mood episodes dominating the picture, and grades severity and course with specifiers. Where ICD-10 counted first-rank symptoms and "atypical" features, ICD-11 replaces the old paranoid-catatonic subtypes with **dimensions** (positive, negative, depressive, manic, motor, cognitive). Indian exams may still test ICD-10 wording; know both, and know which system you are quoting.

### The duration ladder (a student favourite)

| Label | Active psychosis | Total illness |
|-------|-----------------|---------------|
| Brief psychotic disorder (DSM-5) | 1 day – 1 month | under 1 month |
| Acute and transient psychotic disorder (ICD) | — | under 3 months |
| Schizophreniform disorder (DSM-5) | 1 – 6 months | under 6 months |
| Schizophrenia | 1 month or more | 6 months or more |

### Severity scales: named, never copied

**PANSS** (30 items) is the research gold standard; **BPRS** is its shorter cousin; **CGI** gives a global impression; **NSA/BNSS** grade negative symptoms; **SOFAS/WHODAS** track functioning. These instruments are licensed/validated tools, so KYP names them and their purpose but does not reproduce their items. Ordinary Indian OPD practice does not require them; good serial mental state examinations do the clinical job.

### First-episode workup (before you commit to the label)

- History from patient **and** family (collateral is half the diagnosis); full mental state and physical/neurological examination.
- Urine drug screen; CBC; fasting glucose; TSH; **B12 and vitamin D** (India); liver and renal function; HIV and syphilis serology where plausible; pregnancy test where relevant; ECG before starting antipsychotics.
- EEG and/or MRI if there is focal neurology, new headache, onset below 13 or above 40, seizures, or rapid cognitive decline.
- Keep **anti-NMDA-receptor encephalitis** on your radar: young women, days-to-weeks onset, seizures, orofacial dyskinesia, autonomic swings. It is treatable with immunotherapy, and it is missed every year.

### Differential diagnosis: what else does this?

| Mimic | The clue that separates it |
|-------|----------------------------|
| Bipolar mania with psychosis | prominent, sustained mood elevation; grandiosity fades as mood settles |
| Schizoaffective disorder | mood episodes occupy most of the total illness duration |
| Delusional disorder | one fixed, plausible-sounding theme; life otherwise intact |
| Substance-induced psychosis (cannabis, stimulants, steroids) | intoxication history; tox screen; clears within days to weeks |
| Depression with psychotic features | nihilistic/guilty content tracks the mood; psychomotor slowing |
| Anti-NMDA encephalitis, SLE, thyroid disease, temporal-lobe epilepsy | abrupt or atypical onset; neurological soft signs; investigate |
| Neurosyphilis, HIV, Wilson's disease (young + movement signs) | exam and serology; still worth their place in Indian districts |

## 9. Management

### The whole plan in one box

**Acute phase:** safety, exclude organic causes, calm the situation, start an antipsychotic. **Recovery phase:** consolidate, psychoeducate, rebuild routine and study/work. **Maintenance phase:** continue medication 1–2 years minimum (first episode), family intervention, functional recovery, physical health. Relapse prevention is the name of the long game.

### Acute agitation

Oral or IM **lorazepam 1–2 mg**, or an antipsychotic (IM haloperidol 5 mg with promethazine, or IM olanzapine). **Catatonia: lorazepam 2 mg test dose first, ECT if it does not move.** Physical restraint is a last resort done properly or not at all; chaining or confinement is illegal and wrong (Mental Healthcare Act 2017).

### Pharmacotherapy: the core table

| Drug | Start → usual adult dose | Watch for | Generic cost/month (approx) |
|------|--------------------------|-----------|------------------------------|
| Haloperidol | 1–2.5 mg → 2–10 mg | stiffness, tremor, akathisia, tardive dyskinesia | ₹20–80 |
| Risperidone | 1 mg → 2–6 mg | EPSE above 6 mg; raised prolactin (periods stop, breast changes) | ₹60–250 |
| Olanzapine | 5 mg → 10–20 mg | weight gain, blood sugar and lipids | ₹100–350 |
| Aripiprazole | 5–10 mg → 10–30 mg | akathisia; least weight/prolactin trouble | ₹150–500 |
| Quetiapine | 25–50 mg → 400–800 mg | sedation, metabolic, postural drops | ₹150–600 |
| Amisulpride | 50–100 mg → 400–800 mg | prolactin rise; moderate EPSE | ₹150–400 |
| Clozapine | 12.5 mg → 200–600 mg | see the clozapine box below | ₹300–700 + labs |
| LAIs (depot) | flupenthixol decanoate 20–40 mg IM every 2–4 weeks; risperidone microspheres 25–50 mg every 2 weeks (keep oral cover for the first 3 weeks); paliperidone monthly | injection-site, cost | ₹150–400 (flupenthixol) to ₹2,000–4,500 (newer) |

**The rules that matter:**
1. **First episodes are sensitive.** Start low, titrate slowly, judge benefit only after **4–6 weeks at an adequate dose**. Switching early is usually impatience, not pharmacology.
2. **One antipsychotic at a time.** Combining adds side effects, not efficacy.
3. **Clozapine after two adequate, adhered-to failures.** Response in roughly half of resistant patients, the only drug with proven superiority there. Mandatory ANC (absolute neutrophil count) monitoring: weekly for 18 weeks, then monthly; Indian district-hospital labs make this practical. Counsel the three dangerous-but-preventable risks: **agranulocytosis** (~1%, caught by the blood counts), **myocarditis** (fever, chest pain or racing heart in the first ~4–6 weeks = emergency, check troponin/CRP), and **constipation** (clozapine bowel obstruction kills quietly; every patient gets a bowel rule: no motion for 2–3 days means a phone call).
4. **Maintenance durations:** first episode 1–2 years after recovery, then a slow, shared-decision taper; second episode 2–5 years; three or more relapses; plan for indefinite treatment. Stopping abruptly after a first episode relapses roughly **3 in 4 patients within a year or two**; staying on treatment roughly halves relapse risk.
5. **Do not chase negative symptoms with ever-higher D2 blockade.** That road leads to stiffness, masked faces and secondary negative symptoms. Reduce dose, consider aripiprazole or clozapine, and invest in psychosocial care instead.

**Routine monitoring:** weight and waist (baseline, 12 weeks, then 6–12-monthly), blood pressure, fasting glucose and lipids; prolactin only if symptoms appear; ECG if risk factors; ANC schedule for clozapine as above.

> *India note (medicines):* risperidone, olanzapine and haloperidol are cheap generics everywhere; haloperidol is the deepest-stocked option in primary health centres; **flupenthixol decanoate is the most practical depot for most Indian districts**, for patients who travel home to villages, a two-to-four-weekly injection beats daily tablet supervision. Detailed costs live in section 10.

### Psychotherapy and psychosocial care: the forgotten half

- **Psychoeducation**: the illness model, the relapse signature (each patient's personal early warning signs), why medicines continue after improvement. Delivered in minutes at every visit, it is the cheapest effective intervention in Indian psychiatry.
- **Family intervention**: structured sessions over 6–9 months teaching calm, low-criticism communication and problem-solving. In high-EE homes it can **nearly halve relapse** (from ~50%+ to ~25% at 9 months).
- **CBT for psychosis (CBTp)**: works on the distress attached to voices and beliefs rather than debating their truth.
- **Social skills training and cognitive remediation**; modest, real gains in thinking and social function; computerised Indian adaptations exist from NIMHANS.
- **Supported employment (IPS model)**: competitive work with coaching beats sheltered waiting; in India, family-occupation-based livelihoods and NGO programmes do the same job.
- **Physical health**: treating smoking, weight and diabetes is core psychiatric work; this is what actually kills people early (see section 4).

> *India note (psychosocial):* trained CBTp therapists remain scarce outside metros, but family intervention can be delivered by any motivated mental-health worker and fits the joint-family reality perfectly. Tele-MANAS (14416) counselling supports families between OPD visits; it supports, but never replaces, specialist care.

### Lifestyle

Regular sleep (sleep loss is a relapse trigger); fixed daily routine with sunlight and a 30-minute walk (protects heart, brain and mood); **zero cannabis and careful alcohol**: treat every use as a medical event in this illness; and a household communication style that is warm, curious and low-criticism ("ask about the voice, don't argue with it").

> *India note (lifestyle):* yoga as an **add-on** has small supportive Indian RCTs for negative symptoms and functioning, useful adjunct, never a replacement for medication.

### Brain stimulation

- **ECT** is the fastest option for catatonia, high suicide risk, refusal of food and drink, postpartum psychosis, and as a later option in resistant illness. In India it is cheap and available in district hospitals; a course is ~8–12 sessions, usually bilateral.
- **rTMS** (repetitive magnetic stimulation) is a metro-only adjunct for persistent voices or negative symptoms, modest benefit, no role in emergencies.

> *India note (ECT):* the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 governs it, informed consent, always under anaesthesia with muscle relaxation, never as punishment. Modern ECT memory difficulties are usually brief; the untreated illness damages memory far more.

### Other

Vocational rehabilitation, peer support, relapse-prevention planning, **advance directives** (MHA 2017 lets patients record treatment preferences for future episodes), and treating the common companions, depression, substance use, obesity, diabetes. High-dose vitamins, "detox" rituals and unmonitored herbal "cures" are not treatments for schizophrenia; families spending on them should be redirected kindly but clearly.

## 10. Indian practice

### Which guidelines are actually followed?

India has no dedicated national schizophrenia guideline. Day-to-day practice blends **APA 2020** and **NICE CG178**, softened by IPS consensus material and NIMHANS manuals. At the primary level, the WHO **mhGAP** psychosis module *is* the guideline: recognise, start basic treatment, educate the family, refer.

### The government pathway

PHC or sub-centre (identification and basic treatment under the **District Mental Health Programme, DMHP**, now covering ~750 districts) → district hospital psychiatry OPD (free or near-free medicines, depot injections, lab support) → medical college psychiatry departments → tertiary centres like NIMHANS and AIIMS. Acute beds exist at district and medical-college level; long-term care happens overwhelmingly at home. **Tele-MANAS (14416 or 1-800-891-4416)** provides 24×7 counselling in multiple Indian languages through 50+ cells.

### Private practice reality

Most specialist care in cities is private: consultations ₹300–1,000, follow-ups ₹200–600, therapy ₹500–2,000 per session in metros. **Insurance**: the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 mandates parity, and IRDAI (2024) made explicit that mental illness must be covered like physical illness, but check individual policies for exclusions, waiting periods and whether outpatient pharmacy is included at all.

### Primary care: the make-or-break tier

A PHC doctor or MO can legitimately: recognise psychosis with three questions (*unusual beliefs? hearing voices? decline in function?*); start risperidone 1–2 mg or haloperidol; arrange a baseline weight and ECG where feasible; register the patient under DMHP; review monthly for weight, stiffness and adherence; use ASHA/ANM visits for home follow-up; and psychoeducate the family. Referral to district level is for clozapine, depot initiation, ECT, pregnancy or diagnostic doubt.

### Patient and family education: the real medicine

Three sentences every family should hear before leaving the first consultation: **(1)** this is a medical illness of the brain, and it is treatable; **(2)** medicines must continue for at least 1–2 years even after things improve; **(3)** criticism, over-control and cannabis are the three relapse engines; warmth, routine and medicines are the three relapse brakes.

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

### Case 1: the first episode hiding behind "study stress"

Rahul (name changed), 21, second-year engineering student in a Karnataka college town. Over ten months he stopped eating at the mess with friends, then attending classes; kept his room dark; told a cousin that TV anchors discussed him; began hearing a male voice scoring his actions "like a cricket commentary". The family first called it study stress; after he stopped bathing for days they tried two temple visits and a ritual; a perceptive college counsellor finally routed them to the district psychiatry OPD. Examination: ideas of reference, second-person running commentary, blunted affect, no insight. Urine cannabis positive (occasional use). Routine bloods normal; B12 borderline-low and corrected. DUP about 8–10 months. Started risperidone 1 mg titrated to 4 mg over two weeks; two 30-minute psychoeducation sessions for the parents naming exactly which changes to report early. By week 6 the voice had faded to a whisper; by month 5 he returned to classes part-time, negotiated by the family with the college.

**Teaching points:** the prodrome masquerades as adjustment or laziness; faith-healer contact is a delay, not an offence; engage respectfully or the family disappears; and the treatment decision that mattered most was starting low, going slow, and keeping the family on the team.

### Case 2: "treatment-resistant" that needed the right next step

Sunita (name changed), 34, homemaker in Bihar, schizophrenia for 11 years. Three relapses, each after stopping medicines within months of feeling better. Two adequate trials (risperidone 6 mg for 16 weeks, olanzapine 20 mg for 14 weeks, adherence verified by tablet counts and family) left abusive voices and periods of refusing food. Started clozapine 12.5 mg at night, titrated to 400 mg over five weeks; ANC weekly for 18 weeks at the district hospital lab, then monthly. Constipation managed with fluids, fibre and lactulose, plus the explicit rule: no bowel movement for 2–3 days means a phone call. By month 4 the voice was a whisper and she was eating and cooking; by month 8 she was attending family functions.

**Teaching points:** "resistant" first requires proving that past trials were adequate *and taken*; clozapine's serious risks are preventable with three counsellable rules (blood counts, early-week fever/chest pain = emergency, bowel rule); and ANC logistics in India are solvable by pairing the patient with the nearest district lab.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way patients ask it

**Is schizophrenia split personality?**
No. Split personality (dissociative identity disorder) is a completely different and rare condition. In schizophrenia the "split" in the name refers to a split from reality; voices or beliefs that others do not share. It does not mean two people live in one body.

**What causes it, did we do something wrong as parents?**
No. It is a brain illness caused by genes plus brain development plus environment; none of which is anyone's fault. Parenting style does not cause schizophrenia. That said, a calm, low-criticism home measurably lowers relapse, so families powerfully help recovery even though they did not cause the illness.

**Can it be cured?**
Not "cured" like an infection, but controlled like diabetes or thyroid disease. Most people improve substantially with treatment; many study, work, marry and raise families. The word to aim for is *recovery*, not cure.

**Will the medicines have to continue for life?**
For a first episode: at least 1–2 years *after* feeling fully better. After a second relapse, several years; after three relapses, doctors usually recommend staying on long-term. This is decided with your doctor, but never stop abruptly on your own, because sudden stoppage is the fastest road back to hospital.

**Are antipsychotic medicines addictive? Do they make you "high" or drowsy forever?**
They are not addictive, no craving, no tolerance, no "high". Some cause sleepiness in the first days which usually settles. If drowsiness persists, the dose or drug can be adjusted; do not silently stop.

**Will my children develop it?**
If one parent has schizophrenia, the child's risk is roughly 10%, meaning a 90% chance of not developing it. If both parents are affected, it approaches 40–50%. Genetics is one factor among several, and genetic counselling can help families plan without fear.

**Can he marry and work?**
Many people with schizophrenia do both, once stable. The fair sequence is stability first, then decisions about disclosure and marriage made honestly with the partner's family. Supported employment programmes help people return to work faster.

**Is cannabis really a problem? He says it calms him.**
Cannabis is the strongest known avoidable trigger of this illness and of relapse. In schizophrenia, "calming" comes at the cost of the next episode. This is the one recreational substance with a clear answer: none.

**Are people who hear voices dangerous?**
Most are not; they are frightened. Risk of violence rises mainly with untreated illness plus substance use, which is exactly why continuing treatment is the safety plan. Stigma harms more patients than patients harm anyone.

**Do we need to admit him to hospital?**
Only for specific reasons: danger to self or others, refusing food and drink, catatonia, or when home care has genuinely broken down. Most treatment is outpatient in India, and the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 protects his rights, no chaining, no confinement, treatment with consent wherever possible.

**What about ECT, is it a punishment?**
No. Modern ECT happens under anaesthesia, is painless, and memory wobbles usually settle within weeks. In catatonia or severe refusal of food it can be life-saving within days. The popular image from old films is simply out of date.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**Mnemonics and micro-rules worth memorising:**
- Negative symptoms, the **five A's**: Affect flattening, Alogia, Avolition, Anhedonia, Asociality.
- Duration ladder, **"1 day, 1 month, 6 months"**: brief psychotic (<1 month), schizophreniform (1–6), schizophrenia (6+).
- **Catatonia? Lorazepam first, think ECT next.**
- Clozapine's killers: the **three C's, Counts (ANC), Carditis (early weeks), Constipation**.
- First-rank symptoms (still examined under ICD-10 in India): audible thoughts/echo, voices arguing or commenting, thought insertion/withdrawal/broadcast, made feelings/impulses/acts, somatic passivity, delusional perception, *no longer privileged in DSM-5/ICD-11* (classic trap).

**High-yield facts:**
- DSM-5 asks two-or-more symptoms with at least one of the top three; 1 month active; 6 months total; functional decline; exclusions.
- Clozapine after two adequate trials; ~1% agranulocytosis; ANC weekly ×18 weeks then monthly; the only drug superior in resistant illness.
- Risperidone above 6 mg/day produces EPSE approaching haloperidol; highest prolactin rise (shared with amisulpride). Aripiprazole is the prolactin-sparing option.
- Weight-gain ranking (classic viva): clozapine ≈ olanzapine > quetiapine ≈ risperidone > aripiprazole.
- Relapse off medicines after a first episode: roughly 75% within 1–2 years; maintenance roughly halves it.
- Mortality 2–3×, 10–20 years lost: driven by physical illness; hence metabolic monitoring is a psychiatric duty.
- Akathisia (restlessness) after dose increase is mistaken for worsening illness; the wrong move is raising the dose again.
- Good prognostic signs: acute onset, clear precipitant, mood symptoms, good premorbid functioning, positive-symptom-dominant picture, family history of *mood* (not schizophrenia) disorder. Poor: insidious onset, long DUP, negative-dominant, poor premorbid function.
- 22q11.2 deletion: highest single copy-number risk (~25% psychosis by adulthood); think of it in early-onset cases with congenital anomalies.
- Late-onset (after 40) and very-late (after 60) schizophrenia: more women, sensory impairment association, better symptom profile, lower doses needed.
- Indian exam corner: Mental Healthcare Act 2017 (rights-based; decriminalised attempted suicide; advance directives; ECT with consent), DMHP (~750 districts), tele-MANAS 14416, NIMHANS as the apex national institute, mhGAP for primary care.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1.** A 24-year-old man has three months of running-commentary voices and referential delusions, with declining college attendance; no substance use or mood syndrome. DSM-5 diagnosis today:
A. Schizophrenia B. Schizophreniform disorder C. Brief psychotic disorder D. Schizoaffective disorder
**Answer: B.** One month of active symptoms but less than six months of total illness = schizophreniform. Schizophrenia requires 6 months. Watch the "1–1–6" ladder.

**2.** After full recovery from a FIRST episode, the minimum evidence-based maintenance duration is:
A. Stop at 3 months B. 1–2 years C. Lifelong always D. Stop when insight returns
**Answer: B.** Guidelines converge on 1–2 years before any shared, slow taper. Stopping early relapses roughly 3 in 4.

**3.** Two adequate, adhered antipsychotic trials fail. The next step is:
A. Combine two antipsychotics B. A third newer antipsychotic C. Clozapine D. Long-term benzodiazepine
**Answer: C.** Clozapine: the only drug with demonstrated superiority in treatment-resistant schizophrenia; combination therapy adds side effects, not efficacy.

**4.** Three weeks on risperidone 5 mg, a patient paces, cannot sit still and says "something inside is restless". Most likely explanation and the wrong move:
A. Worsening psychosis: increase risperidone B. Akathisia: the wrong move is increasing the dose C. Anxiety disorder: start an SSRI D. Tardive dyskinesia: stop immediately
**Answer: B.** Akathisia after dose titration is routinely misread as deterioration; the trap answer is raising the dose. Manage with propranolol, dose reduction or a switch.

**5.** A young woman presents over five days with new psychosis plus seizures, orofacial grimacing and blood-pressure swings. First thought:
A. Brief psychotic disorder B. Anti-NMDA-receptor encephalitis C. Schizophrenia D. Malingering
**Answer: B.** Organic first: abrupt onset with seizures and dyskinesia in a young woman = autoimmune encephalitis until excluded (CSF antibody testing, immunotherapy). It is treatable, and it is missed every year.

**6.** Under India's District Mental Health Programme, the intervention a primary-care medical officer can correctly deliver without referral is:
A. Starting clozapine B. Starting risperidone 1–2 mg with family psychoeducation C. ECT D. psychoanalysis
**Answer: B.** PHC level: recognise, start basic antipsychotic, psychoeducate, follow up. Clozapine, ECT and formal psychotherapy belong to district and tertiary levels.
