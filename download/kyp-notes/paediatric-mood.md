# Mood Disorders in Children & Adolescents

## 1. In one line + overview

**In young people, depression rarely looks like sadness wearing black (it looks like IRRITABILITY, slamming doors, marks that fall, meals skipped, sleep that shifts, friends dropped, and the words "nothing is fun anymore") and it is common, dangerous if missed, and genuinely treatable, while "bipolar disorder" in children is mostly a label applied too fast to what is actually chronic irritable misery, a distinction that decides between very different treatments.**

The developmental translation is the whole game. Adult depression's sadness arrives in children as cranky, snappy, touchy, out-of-proportion rage, parents bring them for "attitude problem" or "phone addiction", not for "depression". The anhedonia arrives as dropping cricket, quitting dance, "boring" as the universal verdict on everything once loved. The energy and concentration changes arrive as falling marks, incomplete notebooks, teacher complaints that read as laziness. By adolescence the picture adultifies, and adolescence itself supplies so much mood turbulence that clinicians historically under-diagnosed real depression as "just being a teenager" and now, in the opposite error, over-diagnose severe mood states too fast. The numbers say this is not rare: roughly 2% of prepubertal children and a mid-single-digit share of adolescents have major depression, and by adulthood roughly one in five people has had a depressive episode by 18–20. Suicide is the second leading cause of death in Indian adolescents (see the Youth Suicide note for that half of the topic). The bipolar question in youth: TRUE juvenile mania exists (mostly adolescent, episodic, euphoric-or-irritable grandiose energy with decreased sleep) but is rarer than the referral letters suggest; the child with years of near-daily severe tantrums and chronic rage is much more often the DSM-5's disruptive mood dysregulation disorder (DMDD) or depression-with-irritability, and the difference decides whether a child gets psychotherapy-and-watch versus mood stabilisers and antipsychotics. Get this distinction right and you have mastered the core of youth mood work.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Recognise child/adolescent depression through its developmental costumes: irritability, anhedonia-by-attrition, school decline, somatic complaints, social withdrawal
- Apply the diagnostic logic (2 weeks, 5+ symptoms including mood/anhedonia with irritability as the child-sign) with the impairment gate
- Distinguish depression's irritability from DMDD (chronic non-episodic) and from true juvenile mania (episodic + euphoria/grandiosity + decreased-sleep), and understand why that triage matters
- Explain the three mechanism stories: the sensitised stress-response system, the pubertal divergence (why girls overtake boys at 13), and the sleep-social-media vortex
- Screen every depressed adolescent for suicide (no exceptions) and for the drivers, abuse, bullying, identity stress, exam pressure, substance
- Deliver treatment in the evidence order: mild → psychotherapy (CBT/IPT-A); moderate-severe → psychotherapy + fluoxetine-class SSRI; know the TADS logic and the honest suicidality conversation
- Handle the youth-bipolar question with the episodic gate: adolescent onset, family history, decreased-sleep-mania, and the treatment anchors (lithium-antipsychotic tier, valproate's pregnancy rules in teenage girls)
- Navigate Indian realities: board-exam and coaching-pressure presentations (Kota-type crises), parental denial, the ECT-in-adolescents reality, and the NIMHANS-era service map
- Give families the prognosis honestly: most episodes remit; relapse-prevention is the real skill; the child is more than the diagnosis

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Prevalence by stage: prepubertal children ~1–2%, early adolescence ~3–5%, late adolescence ~6–8% point prevalence with cumulative rates by young adulthood approaching 15–20%. The sex ratio flips at puberty (boys and girls equal before, girls roughly 2:1 after) the divergence driven (in the leading accounts) by girls' earlier pubertal social-stress exposure plus a rumination-style processing that puberty amplifies. Mean episode length without treatment is months (6–9 typical range); recurrence is the rule rather than the exception (a good share of first-episode adolescents will relapse within 2–5 years; maintenance planning is not optional). The bipolar side: juvenile bipolar disorder is genuinely rare before puberty in community terms; specialised clinics see it more; adolescent-onset bipolar disorder often begins with a depressive episode and is misdiagnosed as unipolar depression for years; the adolescent treated for depression who later declares mania is one of psychiatry's classic case-series.

**India.** The National Mental Health Survey 2015–16 put adolescent (13–17) depression prevalence around 1 in 20 with higher figures in urban metros; the clinical Indian reality is channelled through pressure structures, the Std 9–12 band, board results days, the Kota/coaching-city complex where several thousand kilometres from home, hostel isolation, weekly test rankings and parental investment create a near-laboratory of depressive onset; NCRB data show student suicides numbering in the tens of thousands over recent years and rising (the full safety-side treatment lives in the Youth Suicide note). Indian detection filters run through schools (marks as the vital sign: a 20-mark fall is often the FIRST sign a teacher sees), paediatric OPDs (somatic costume: body pain, appetite loss, "weakness"), and the tutoring economy (the "not studying" complaint). Parental denial and the marriage-market-academic-shame complex delay help; the child-guidance infrastructure is thin outside metros but growing in school-counsellor form.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**Genetic / biological**
- Heritability of major depression ~35–40%; early-onset cases run more familial loading; bipolar family history raises both depression-with-later-mania risk and treatment-planning stakes
- HPA-axis sensitisation: early adversity + chronic stress calibrate the stress system hot, the diathesis layer
- Puberty itself: the sex-hormone surge interacts with social-stress exposure, the female divergence's engine
- Sleep architecture fragility: the adolescent phase delay (biology) meeting the phone (behaviour): the vortex below

**Psychological**
- Rumination (the thoughts-that-go-round style) predicts onset and persistence, and is directly targetable in therapy
- Cognitive triad in teens: unlovability-uselessness-hopelessness expressed as "no one would notice if I disappeared"
- Perfectionism and the marks-identity fusion (the Indian star-student's fragile self: "I am my percentage")
- Non-suicidal self-injury as an affect-regulation behaviour, not a suicide attempt, but a strong risk marker (see Youth Suicide note)

**Social / environmental**
- Bullying (traditional and cyber), among the strongest modifiable risk factors
- Abuse, neglect, family conflict, parental mental illness, parental substance
- Academic pressure systems: the Std 10/12 board culture, coaching-hostel isolation, NEET/JEE repeat-years
- Identity-related stress: sexual/gender minority adolescents carry several-fold elevated depression risk; rarely asked in Indian clinics, and the question itself is therapeutic
- Loss and bereavement (see the Bereavement note's child-adjacent rules)
- Chronic illness (diabetes, asthma, epilepsy: the two-way street with mood)

**Indian context**
- The phone-paradox: the connection device that delivers both rescue (supportive peers, identity communities) and threat (night-scroll, comparison, cyberbullying), audit both directions
- The joint-family's double edge: more eyes to notice, more pressure to perform, more adults whose conflicts land on the child
- The exam-institution complex: coaching cities as depressive-onset incubators (sleep loss, rank-postings, isolation, fear of the "wasted year")

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: the sensitised alarm.** The stress system (the HPA axis) is calibratable by experience. A child raised with warmth and predictable care ships with a system that responds and recovers. Early adversity (abuse, chronic conflict, humiliation) sets the system's baseline hot: cortisol responses to daily stress are exaggerated, recovery is slow, and the brain's mood circuits (prefrontal-limbic balance) sit closer to the depressive threshold. This is why the same exam setback flattens one adolescent for a weekend and another for a year: the question is never "what happened" alone, but "what system received it".

**Story 2: the pubertal divergence.** At roughly 13, girls' depression rates take off while boys' do not, the best-supported account braids three threads: (a) girls' social-stress exposure rises (relational aggression, body-image pressure, harassment exposure), (b) puberty's hormones interact with the stress system, and (c) girls disproportionately develop the rumination style, problems turned over and over rather than acted out. Each thread is a treatment handle: reducing exposure, treating the body-image and safety issues, and teaching active coping over rumination. Boys' depression, meanwhile, HIDES: irritability, aggression, substance, risk-taking; the male presentation is the one the disciplinary system sees before the clinic does.

**Story 3: the sleep vortex.** The adolescent brain naturally delays its sleep clock (biology pushing bedtime later), while Indian school start-times (7–7.30 a.m. classes, coaching from 6 a.m. in some cities) fight it from the other side, and the phone enters the vortex: late-night scrolling (light + content) postpones sleep further, sleep loss degrades next-day mood regulation, worse mood drives more night-scrolling for comfort. Within weeks, a chronically sleep-short, dysregulated adolescent meets the first exam setback, and the vortex becomes the depression's launching pad. Sleep assessment is therefore not a formality in youth depression: it is a treatment target with its own evidence.

## 7. Symptoms

**The depressive episode, child edition (DSM logic in developmental costume):**
- **Mood**: irritable, cranky, easily explosive (OR sad, tearful, "flat") for most of the day, nearly every day, 2+ weeks (in under-12s, irritability can be the ONLY mood sign)
- **Anhedonia**: hobbies dropped (the cricket kit gathering dust, the guitar in the corner), friends faded, the universal "boring" verdict, not enjoying the family's own Goa trip
- **Appetite/weight**: skipped meals, no hunger for favourite foods, or stress-eating and weight gain (both directions seen)
- **Sleep**: can't fall asleep (night rumination + phone), broken sleep, hypersomnia weekends (the 14-hour weekend sleeps), morning dread on school days
- **Energy/psychomotor**: the exhausted after-school collapse, slowing of work speed, restless agitation in some
- **Worthlessness/guilt**: "I am a burden", "I ruin everything", marks-as-self fusion ("I failed, so I am a failure")
- **Concentration**: notes incomplete, chapters unread, "I read and nothing enters", the marks-decline engine
- **Somatic costume**: headaches, body pain, "weakness", menstrual-cycle amplification, the paediatric-OPD route
- **Suicidal ideation**: passive ("I wish I wouldn't wake up") to active with plan: ALWAYS asked directly, every episode, every review (see the Youth Suicide note for the full safety architecture)

**The DMDD picture (the chronic-irritability mimic):** severe recurrent temper outbursts (verbal rages, aggression) out of proportion to triggers, 3+ weekly for a year+, with persistent irritability/anger BETWEEN outbursts, onset before 10, across settings, no episodic-free-intervals, and the mania-gates absent. Long-term risk runs toward depression and anxiety, NOT toward bipolar disorder, the longitudinal evidence that killed the "paediatric bipolar epidemic" over-diagnosis in the US and should keep Indian practice careful.

**The juvenile mania question (what TRUE looks like):** a DISTINCT EPISODE of days-to-weeks of elevated/irritable energy: grandiosity (not just confident, genuinely inflated: "I'll top NEET without studying, I have special ability"), decreased need for sleep (3–4 hours, waking energetic, the key sign: not insomnia, wakefulness with energy), racing thoughts, pressured speech, impulsivity spikes (spending, risky riding, sexual disinhibition), often psychotic features in severe adolescent mania. The family history (a first-degree relative with bipolar disorder) roughly doubles the alert level; adolescent bipolar usually starts with DEPRESSION, a first-episode depressed adolescent with strong bipolar family history and psychotic/hypersomnic/atypical features deserves a documented "watch" note in the file.

**The comorbid tourists:** anxiety disorders (the classic companion; treat both), substance (self-medication or its own diagnosis), ADHD (concentration double-book-keeping), eating disorders (the weight/appetite axis), self-harm behaviour, and the body-dysmorphic/identity layer in the unasked questions.

## 8. Diagnosis

**The logic (DSM-5-TR paraphrased):** a major depressive episode = 5+ symptoms in 2 weeks, including depressed OR IRRITABLE mood or anhedonia; impairment (school, home, social); not attributable to substance or a medical condition. Child-gates: irritability counts as mood-sign; school decline is the functional vital sign; somatic presentation common. Persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia) applies in youth at ≥1 year (vs 2 in adults), the low-grade chronic misery often mistaken for "personality". DMDD: the chronic-irritable template above, onset 6–10, and it CANNOT be diagnosed alongside ODD/conduct, or inside a bipolar episode's alternative explanation. Bipolar-I: the distinct mania episode (1 week, or any duration with hospitalisation), in youth treated with the episodic-gate discipline. Schizoaffective/psychotic-depression distinctions for the severe end.

**The assessment structure:**
1. **Multi-source**: adolescent alone (validated by confidentiality boundaries), parents separately (parents report irritability and sleep; adolescents report anhedonia, self-worth, suicidal ideation; the sources DISAGREE by design, both are needed), school reports (marks trajectory, notebook audit, teacher observations)
2. **The suicide screen, every time, worded directly**: "In the last two weeks, have you felt that life isn't worth living? Have you thought about ending it? Have you done anything toward it?" Then the means/plan/history triad. (Full protocol in the Youth Suicide note.)
3. **The bipolar-gate audit** (before any antidepressant in youth): episodicity (distinct periods of elevated energy + decreased sleep?), family history of bipolar, past antidepressant-triggered activation/switching, psychosis, hospitalisation history. Positive gates → mood-stabiliser-first thinking and specialist referral
4. **The drivers hunt**: bullying (cyber included), abuse (the screen applies in adolescent girls AND boys, somatic-depressive presentations hide abuse at high rates), identity stress (the one respectful question about sexual orientation/gender saves lives in this population), exam-pressure architecture, substance (cannabis is a frequent hidden companion, it both mimics and deepens the picture), sleep audit (the vortex)
5. **Rating scales (named only)**: PHQ-A, CDI-2 lineages, severity and tracking tools, never diagnostic substitutes for the interview
6. **Labs where indicated**: TSH, CBC, vitamin D/B12 in the fatigued-somatic picture, pregnancy test in relevant contexts before SSRIs (a legal and safety necessity that Indian clinics skip too often)
7. **The developmental and family map**: parental depression (the untreated parent is both cause and maintenance), recent losses, family conflict level, the academic-pressure contract

### Differential table

| Looks like youth depression | The distinguishing feature |
|---|---|
| Normal teen moodiness | Still enjoys friends/hobbies; reactive hour-scale swings; function intact; the "weekend-competence" test (functions fine in chosen contexts) |
| DMDD | Chronic baseline anger + outbursts for a year+, onset pre-10, no clear episodes, no anhedonia-depth, no sleep-appetite syndrome |
| Juvenile bipolar (depressed phase) | Episodic pattern + family history + decreased-sleep-energy episodes historically + atypical/psychotic/hypersomnic features → document the watch-status before starting SSRIs |
| ADHD with demoralisation | Chronic concentration issues since childhood, mood low specifically around school failure; the mood clears with success experiences |
| Substance-induced mood | Cannabis/alcohol timeline; intoxication windows; withdrawal lows; the friends-change sign precedes mood change |
| Hypothyroid/anaemia states | The labs; slower-onset global picture; cold intolerance/menstrual clues |
| Anxiety disorders' school decline | The worry-content precedes the anhedonia; somatic time-pattern; fear-engine rather than mood-engine |
| Grief (loss reactions) | Wave-like, meaning-centred, function partially preserved; see the Bereavement note |
| Personality-emerging instability (late teens) | Long-standing interpersonal chaos pattern, identity diffusion: a psychotherapy-first, diagnosis-careful zone |
| Bullying/abuse reactions | Event-mapped onset; the hunt precedes any prescription |

## 9. Management

**The frame: severity-first, safety-always, family-included, school-liaised.**

**1: Safety (the step-zero that precedes everything):** the risk assessment, means-restriction plan with parents (pesticides, ropes, medicines locked; the Indian house-hold audit in the Youth Suicide note), the crisis card with Tele-MANAS (14416) and iCall/helplines, the follow-up interval set BEFORE the family leaves, and hospitalisation criteria held honestly (active intent, plan, recent attempt, unsafe home).

**2: Psychotherapy (the mild-moderate backbone, the evidence spine):**
- CBT (the adolescent version: thought-records, behavioural activation, problem-solving, rumination-work), the first-line for mild-moderate; 12–16 sessions; the TADS-era evidence base
- Interpersonal therapy for adolescents (IPT-A): equal-grade evidence, often better-suited to the interpersonal theatre of Indian adolescent life (friendship fractures, family conflict, the role-transition of board years), 12 sessions targeting 1–2 problem areas
- Behavioural activation as a homework-culture-compatible early module: scheduled pleasant events (the cricket match, the aunt's visit, the music hour) BEFORE mood improves; action first, mood follows
- The sleep module: the CBT-I-lite protocol for the vortex (fixed wake-time, phone-bedroom divorce, the wind-down ritual), often a substantial improvement within 2–3 weeks
- Family sessions: the "validating vs fixing" coaching, conflict de-escalation, the parental-depression treatment linkage
- Digital/tele-CBT is acceptable-tier evidence and an Indian reach-solution

**3: Pharmacotherapy (the moderate-severe tier):**
- **Fluoxetine** is the evidence-anchored SSRI in youth (the TADS/individual-trial logic: response superiority and the only one with two positive paediatric trials); sertraline and escitalopram as reasonable alternatives (the paediatric data thinner, tolerability good); start low (fluoxetine 10–20 mg), review weekly for the first month, the activation/suicidality watch (see the FAQ-honest box below)
- The suicidality-class-warning conversation, held without terror: in aggregated paediatric trials, the SSRI-attributable suicidal-ideation signal was small (roughly 4% vs 2% ideation, no completed suicides in the trial pools), while treated depression reduces completed suicide at population scale; in the clinic this translates to weekly early reviews, the family taught the activation signs (agitation, insomnia, impulsivity spike, worsened mood in week 1–2), and the honest bottom line: untreated depression is the bigger risk
- Paroxetine: not a paediatric choice (the adverse-trial history); venlafaxine: avoid first-line in youth (the same signals); mirtazapine as an appetite/sleep-restoring option in the insomniac-anorexic picture
- Duration: 9–12 months post-remission for a first episode (the relapse-prevention rule), taper never abrupt, reviews maintained
- **The bipolar-gated patient**: if the episodic gates are positive, mood stabiliser-first (see below), NO antidepressant monotherapy (the switch-risk and rapid-cycling lessons), and specialist referral
- *India note (approx 2026): fluoxetine ₹30–100/month; the child-psychiatry specialist density problem makes paediatrician co-management the real-world route; the weekly review protocol (even telephonic) is the safety architecture; the "pause the SSRI at exam time" family request deserves the clear NO (the exam season is the relapse season)*

**4, The DMDD treatment direction (the over-diagnosis rescue):** psychotherapy (CBT for anger and mood, parent management training for the outburst structure), treating comorbid depression/anxiety/ADHD as their own targets, NOT antipsychotic stacking for "suspected bipolar"; the stimulant question if ADHD is confirmed; the antipsychotics only for genuinely dangerous aggression episodes, briefly, monitored.

**5: Juvenile bipolar disorder (when the gates ARE positive):** lithium's adolescent evidence base (including its anti-suicidal signal), the atypical-antipsychotic tier (olanzapine/quetiapine/risperidone/aripiprazole, with weight-and-metabolic monitoring discipline in growing bodies), valproate with the teenage-girl pregnancy-prevention conversation held explicitly (teratogenicity, the IEC/FDA-style rules apply; see the Bipolar note's fuller pharmacology), the school accommodation package, psychoeducation for family AND teenager (the chronic-relapsing frame introduced at a readable age), and the transition-to-adult-services architecture.

**6, School liaison (the Indian reality module):** the marks-trajectory normalised to the school (the 20-mark fall reported as symptom, not as indiscipline), workload adjustment during the episode (homework caps, missed-work recovery plans instead of failure threats), the counsellor as the in-school monitor, and the re-entry plan after any absence, schools respond better to written medical plans than to verbal assurances.

**7, What does NOT work:** rhinoceros-horn-tier: "strictness" (the punitive home response deepens episodes), "distraction" as sole treatment (the Goa trip prescription; anhedonia travels), unmonitored polypharmacy, and the hostel-transfer-as-treatment ("a change of atmosphere"); the coaching-hostel transfer specifically is a risk-amplifier, not a therapy.

*India note (approx 2026): service spine, child/adolescent psychiatry units at medical colleges and NIMHANS/state centres; school counsellors as the expanding detection layer; the Tele-MANAS tier (14416) for crisis; private child psychologists ₹800–2,500/session in metros; ECT in adolescents: a legitimate, rarely-needed severe-case tool (psychotic/ catatonic/ treatment-refractory melancholia); Indian practice keeps an outdated terror of it; modern ECT under proper anaesthesia with full consent architecture is sometimes the most life-saving and safest choice in malignant adolescent depression, and refusing it on age alone is not caution, it is neglect of evidence.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **Marks are the vital sign.** The Indian adolescent's mood often declares itself academically before verbally: the notebook gap, the 20-mark fall, the blank answer sheet of a bright child. Teach the school-teacher version ("report the fall, not the failure") and the parental version ("the marks dropped BEFORE the attitude did; the order matters").
- **The coaching-city crisis (the Kota pattern).** The 16-year-old, 1,500 km from home, in a hostel with weekly rank-postings, whose parents have mortgaged the plot for the fees, presenting with insomnia, appetite loss, and "I am wasting their money" rumination; this is a recognisable Indian syndrome, and it arrives at your clinic in one of three ways: the academic-decline route, the self-harm route, or too late. The clinical package: the episode treated on evidence, the parents called into a decision conference (continue/transfer/return, the mental-health frame legally and ethically outranks the fee sunk-cost), the hostel warden briefed as a monitor, and the return-to-normal-school route de-stigmatised as strategy, not defeat. The family script that matters: "an engineer who took a gap year exists; a dead child does not re-attempt".
- **Irritability is not attitude.** The Indian disciplinary reflex (scold harder, restrict more, confiscate the phone) applied to a depressed adolescent is iatrogenic fuel, restriction deepens isolation, scolding deepens worthlessness. The clinic's parent-coaching converts the reflex: the validation sentence ("I can see this is heavier than attitude; we are getting help") before the boundary sentence.
- **The sexual-orientation/gender question.** Indian clinics skip it; the risk data (multi-fold depression and suicidality in queer adolescents, especially in unsupportive households) make skipping it a safety omission. Ask respectfully, alone, without parental presence, and with the confidentiality boundaries pre-explained; the one question that precedes the prescription sometimes matters more than the prescription.
- **The parent's untreated depression.** The heritability-and-household transmission braids: the mother with her own unspoken low years, the father who self-medicates with work or alcohol. The joint plan (adolescent's therapy + parent's assessment/treatment) doubles outcomes; frame it as logistics, not blame ("the household weather affects the crop, we fix both").
- **The board-year medication myths.** Three Indian myths to kill: "SSRIs dull the brain for exams" (untreated depression dulls it far more), "exam season is the wrong time to diagnose" (it is the best time; the condition is visible), and "we will treat after results" (the result may be a crisis).
- **Post-result season (May–June) is the national risk weather.** Indian clinics and counsellors should anticipate it: the helpline-card distribution in schools before results, the re-attempt-option scripts ready, and the media's reporting discipline around student suicides (no method details, no romanticisation, the contagion mechanics live in the Youth Suicide note).

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: the attitude problem of Standard 9.** A 14-year-old Jaipur boy was brought with the referral sentence "attitude problem, back-answer, phone addiction, marks fell from 82 to 54 in one term." Parental account: snappy, door-slamming, awake at 1 a.m. (phone confiscated twice, no change), food refused at dinner, cricket abandoned mid-season, two fights at school. Teacher: "was a leader, now sits alone; homework incomplete for weeks." Interviewed alone: initially monosyllabic, then the dam: "nothing is fun; I feel like a wet rag; my parents would be better without my marks ruining their name; sometimes I think I should just not wake up." He denied plan/intent, no prior attempt, no self-harm; he described 3 months of early-morning waking (his own words: "the worst hour is 6 a.m."), no appetite, weight loss visible on the chart, hopeless ideation. Substance screen negative (cannabis ruled out by history and collateral); bullying denied (school verified quietly); no episodic elevated-energy episodes (the bipolar gate closed: no decreased-sleep-energy, no family history). Diagnosis: major depressive episode, moderate (with passive death wish), adolescent onset, athletic-depressive presentation. Management: safety architecture first (means audit, the bathroom stored the pesticide; moved to locked shop box with the father; the crisis card with Tele-MANAS; the 3-day first-week review scheduled); fluoxetine started at 10 mg with the week-1 activation-watch briefing, weekly telephonic reviews for a month (dose later to 20 mg); CBT over 14 sessions (behavioural activation as module one, the cricket restart as the first scheduled activity; rumination work for the "wasted investment" thoughts; the marks-identity defusion); the parents coached across two family sessions (the validation-before-boundary script; the phone negotiated to a schedule rather than confiscation; the 6 a.m. wake-misery explained as symptom, not laziness); the school letter converting the failure-threat to a missed-work recovery plan; the sleep protocol (phone out of the bedroom, fixed wake-time). Ten weeks: appetite restored, playing again, marks stabilising; six months: remission with medication continued for the planned 12; the relapse-prevention plan written with him ("the wet-rag early-warning list"). Teaching points: (1) "attitude problem" was the depressive costume; the marks fell before the attitude did; (2) the alone-interview found the passive death wish the parents had no idea of; (3) the means audit at bathroom level (pesticide access) is Indian practice's cheapest life-saver; (4) the phone-confiscation reflex treats a symptom's surface; (5) the school letter changed the environment from threat-source to ally.

**Case 2: the girl with three psychiatrists and no episodes.** A 9-year-old Delhi girl arrived with her parents carrying three previous files: "paediatric bipolar disorder, treatment-refractory, currently on risperidone and valproate". The history assembled differently: since age 6, near-daily severe tantrums (screaming, hitting, throwing objects) triggered by homework frustration, transitions, and NO (to sweets, TV); between outbursts she was "grumpy, negative, easily annoyed", but the parents' timeline showed NO distinct well-periods, NO episodes of elevated energy; sleep during "good weeks" unchanged; no grandiosity ever (the "she thinks she's a princess" family anecdote was the tantrum cry "I am the queen of this house, you can't order me", attitude, not mania); family history: a paternal uncle with alcohol problems, none of bipolar. She had been given the bipolar label after a 20-minute consultation during a severe tantrum week, then two medications escalated over 18 months with 8 kg weight gain (visible: striae, the child hiding her arms, a new social wound the medicines created). Assessment: DMDD gates met (outbursts 4+ weekly for 3 years, persistent between-episode irritability, onset before 10, across home/school; the bipolar gates: all closed); comorbid ADHD-inattentive and a writing-learning-disorder underneath (the homework frustration's engine: 90 minutes of painful writing daily; the rage was the job's cost). Management: the medication unwinding (valproate stopped; risperidone tapered over 8 weeks with behaviour-structure in place first); parent management training (the coercive-cycle work: she cried because it worked, the tantrum's audience-payoffs removed); the learning-disorder remediation and homework caps (40 minutes, breaks); teacher letter (seating, writing-load reduction, the "first-then" transitions at school); CBT-lite anger module (feelings thermometer, the 3-step cool-down practiced when calm); ADHD treated with atomoxetine (the family's anti-stimulant stance respected); the weight-restore plan (the endocrine follow-up for the antipsychotic-era gain). One year: outbursts to weekly-or-less, no rages for the last 4 months at review; weight stable; a diary entry her mother photographed and brought: "I am not a bad queen. I was a girl whose hand hurt." Teaching points: (1) chronic non-episodic irritability + outbursts in an under-10 = DMDD's template, not bipolar; the episodic gate is the differential's spine; (2) the tantrum-week consultation manufactures the bipolar label in Indian practice, take the timeline, not the moment; (3) the writing-learning disorder was the engine nobody had looked for; (4) antipsychotics in growing children create their own iatrogenic morbidity (the weight, the striae, the shame); (5) "attitude-anecdotes" (the queen declaration) are not grandiosity; ask for the sleep, the energy, the flight of ideas.

## 12. Active recall prompts

- State the child-specific translations of the depressive criteria: the mood-sign that substitutes for sadness, the functional vital sign, the somatic front.
- Give the episodic gate for juvenile mania (the three findings that must ALL be there historically) and explain what DMDD's chronic picture gets called instead.
- Recite the pubertal divergence and its three braided mechanisms, then name the treatment handle each provides.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way parents and teenagers ask it

**"Isn't this just teenage moodiness? Her cousin is the same."** Moody is weather; depression is climate. Moody teens still enjoy their friends, their food, their weekends; the moodiness is hourly, reactive, and vanishes on the Goa trip. Depression is most days for two-plus weeks, takes the enjoyment WITH it, and drags marks, sleep, appetite and self-worth along. When the cricket, the food and the friends all go quiet together; that is not adolescence, that is an illness.

**"He gets angry, not sad. How is that depression?"** In young people, depression very often wears anger. The irritability, door-slamming and short fuse are the mood sign; the inner state is emptiness and worthlessness, but it comes out as snapping at everyone near. Ask what is UNDER the anger: no fun, no hope, too-heavy school bag feeling, and thoughts of not wanting to be here, the answers write the diagnosis.

**"Won't the antidepressant increase suicide risk? We saw it online."** This needs the honest numbers: in the trials, suicidal THOUGHTS appeared in about 4 in 100 treated adolescents versus 2 in 100 on placebo, no deaths occurred in the combined trials. And at population level, treating depression with these medicines has coincided with FALLING youth suicide rates. Our safety plan follows the risk that exists: weekly early reviews, the family watching for specific warning signs (agitation, insomnia, sudden energy after flatness), and my direct number. The untreated illness is a bigger risk than the medicine; that is the evidence-based bottom line.

**"The marks already fell. Is treatment not just an excuse?"** The marks fell BECAUSE of the condition; concentration, sleep and drive are depressive symptoms. Treating the depression is how the marks get their engine back. The choice is not "treat or push harder"; the push IS failing because the machinery it pushes on is what is sick.

**"Should we change schools / send him to our native place for a change of atmosphere?"** A transfer never treats an episode, and transferring an untreated depressed adolescent away from friends and familiar adults adds loss to illness. Change the atmosphere AFTER treatment has stabilised, if the environment itself is part of the cause (a bullying school, a coaching hostel). And the coaching-hostel transfer specifically can worsen things; isolation plus pressure is the depressive incubator, not the cure.

**"She says she is fine now. Can we stop the medicine?"** Feeling fine is the medicine working. Stopping at the first sunny week is how relapse gets scheduled; the course is 9–12 months after full recovery for a first episode, tapered slowly, never abruptly. If side-effects are the reason, we adjust the plan; we don't fire the treatment.

**"Is bipolar disorder hereditary? His uncle has it, should we worry?"** A bipolar first-degree relative raises the risk meaningfully, it earns vigilance, not fear. Watch for the three episodic signs historically: days-to-weeks of unusual energy, grandiosity, and a reduced need for sleep with energy intact. If those appear, we reassess before any medication decision. Most adolescents of bipolar parents never develop it.

**"The school counsellor is already doing counselling. Isn't that enough?"** For mild episodes, good counselling with structure may indeed be enough, we grade severity first. For moderate-severe episodes (weight loss, morning waking, worthlessness, any death-wish), the evidence says psychotherapy PLUS medication recovers faster and more fully than either alone. We combine forces; we don't choose.

**"Will ECT damage her brain? They mentioned it for a severe case."** Modern ECT under anaesthesia does not damage the brain, and in the rare malignant adolescent depressions (psychotic, catatonic, refusing food), it can be the fastest life-saving treatment we have. The memory side-effects of the course are mostly short-lived. Age alone is not a reason to withhold evidence; refusing it reflexively can cost a life.

**"How long until she is back to herself?"** Most adolescents respond meaningfully within 4–8 weeks of proper combined treatment, with full recovery over 3–6 months. The last things back are concentration and the enjoyment of studies. And "back to herself" is the right goal; she keeps the diagnosis, not the identity.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**The criteria skeleton (child version) for viva:** 2 weeks + 5+ symptoms including irritable-mood-or-sadness / anhedonia; impairment; child-translations listed; dysthymia ≥1 year in youth; DMDD template (onset pre-10, 1+ year, outbursts + between-irritability); bipolar episodic gates.

**Mnemonic, "SAW-MICE" (the episode cluster)**: Sleep, Appetite, Worthlessness, Mood(irritable/sad), Interest(lost), Concentration, Energy, 2 weeks, 5+, impairment. For the bipolar-vs-DMDD gate: "Episodic means the WELL weeks exist; chronic means they never did"; the sleep-and-energy pair (decreased need, energy intact) is mania's fingerprint.

**High-yield facts:**
- Irritability = the child-sign; anhedonia-by-attrition = the often-missed one; the 20-mark fall = the school's vital sign
- Prevalence ladder: ~1–2% prepubertal → 6–8% late adolescents; cumulative ~15–20% by young adulthood
- Pubertal divergence: girls overtake boys at ~13; rumination is the targetable thread
- DMDD: chronic, pre-10 onset, longitudinal risk toward DEPRESSION/ANXIETY not bipolar, the anti-overdiagnosis point
- Juvenile mania: mostly adolescent, often begins DEPRESSIVE, family history doubles alert, antidepressant monotherapy danger
- Treatment evidence order: CBT/IPT-A for mild; + fluoxetine-class for moderate-severe (TADS logic: combination superiority); weekly early reviews on SSRI start
- Suicide screen: every episode, every review, asked directly; means-restriction with the Indian pesticide-and-medicine audit
- ECT in adolescents: legitimate for malignant depression; age alone is not a contraindication
- Valproate in teenage girls: pregnancy conversation mandatory
- Indian high-risk: coaching-city structure; post-result season; "attitude problem" as the mask

**Classic traps:**
1. Reading irritability as indiscipline and intensifying punishment (iatrogenic deepening)
2. Diagnosing bipolar from the tantrum-week snapshot (the chronic-vs-episodic gate skipped)
3. Starting SSRIs without asking the bipolar-family-history gates (the switch story you will regret)
4. The missing suicide screen at follow-up reviews (episodes evolve)
5. Missing cannabis under the "depressive" presentation
6. Skipping the alone-interview: parents' reports alone miss anhedonia AND death-wishes
7. Stopping the SSRI at the first good month (the relapse scheduling error)

**Indian exam corner:** expected patterns ("a 14-year-old with irritability, falling marks, sleep disturbance) diagnose and manage" (the full ladder: safety, severity grading, drivers, CBT ± fluoxetine, school and family work); "DMDD vs bipolar disorder in children" (the episodic-gate essay); "adolescent depression: risk factors with Indian context"; "ECT in children: indications and ethics". Indian-context marks: NMHS adolescent numbers, coaching-city syndrome, post-result risk season, the marks-as-vital-sign teaching.
