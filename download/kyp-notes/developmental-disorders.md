# Specific Developmental (Learning) Disorders

## 1. In one line + overview

**A specific learning disorder is a narrow, brain-based difficulty in one academic channel (reading, writing or arithmetic) that runs far below what the child's overall intelligence, effort and schooling would predict, and it is nobody's fault: not the child's laziness, not the parents' upbringing, not the school's failure.**

The child is bright in conversation, solves puzzles, remembers stories, asks clever questions, and yet reads a paragraph like a child three classes younger, or writes mirror-reversed letters with aching slowness, or cannot hold the multiplication table despite months of tutoring. This "unexpected underachievement" is the signature. The difficulty is developmental (present from early school years, not newly lost), and it sits in ONE or a few channels while general intelligence is average or above. Left unidentified, the predictable chain is: falling behind → private scolding and public embarrassment → anxiety and school avoidance → "I am stupid" internalised → depression or conduct problems by adolescence. Identified early, the same child reads adequately with the right method, learns through strengths, carries accommodations into board exams, and reaches medicine, engineering, literature; the prognosis of a well-supported learning disorder is genuinely good. In India the label is legally actionable: the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016 recognises specific learning disabilities, which unlocks scribes, extra time and curriculum exemptions in school and board examinations.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Define learning disorders through the "unexpected underachievement" frame (ability intact, one channel failing)
- Distinguish dyslexia, dysgraphia, dyscalculia and developmental coordination disorder by their signatures
- Explain the phonological-core story of dyslexia and the magnitude-core story of dyscalculia in plain words
- Separate learning disorders from their four great mimics: poor schooling, intellectual disability, sensory problems, and language exposure (including English-medium for first-generation learners)
- Run the assessment logic: screening → formal psycho-educational testing → functional classroom evidence
- Deliver the treatment triad: remediation (structured phonics/multisensory methods), accommodations (extra time, scribe, reduced writing), and comorbidity treatment (ADHD above all)
- Counsel the Indian family: it is not laziness; the tuition treadmill will not fix it; the RPwD certificate route and what it unlocks
- Recognise the emotional career of an unidentified learning disorder and interrupt it

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Learning disorders are among the commonest neurodevelopmental conditions: broadly 5–15% of school-age children depending on definitions and cutoffs. Reading disorder (dyslexia) is the most frequent, ~5–10% by strict criteria; mathematics difficulties ~3–7%; writing difficulties frequent and often co-occurring. Boys are identified more often than girls (roughly 1.5–3:1 for reading), partly reflecting referral bias; girls with quiet struggle are under-detected. Onset is by definition early school years; a substantial share persists into adolescence and adulthood (dyslexia is lifelong, even when reading becomes adequate; fluency and spelling give it away). Heritability is high, reading-disorder heritability estimates around 40–70%; a parent with a school-history of "hating reading" is a standard finding.

**India.** Systematic prevalence data are thin; school- and clinic-based studies suggest roughly 3–10% of Indian school children have significant learning difficulties, likely under-detected rather than absent. The Indian twist: many children are being taught to read in English while their home language is Hindi, Marathi, Tamil, Bengali or another tongue, a first-generation English-medium cohort whose "reading delay" reflects bilingual acquisition load, not dyslexia, in a large share of cases. Detection typically happens late (Standard 4 or beyond, when the textbook gap has widened into a canyon) because early-primary "slow start" is tolerated, and Std 1–3 work can be passed on oral memory. Board-exam accommodation requests and RPwD certifications have risen sharply since 2016, which reflects both growing awareness and growing pressure.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**Genetic / biological**
- Strong heritability (40–70% for reading); family history in a parent or sibling is the single most useful risk factor to ask about
- Associated with variation in genes affecting neuronal migration and reading-circuit development (the DYX1C1/DCDC2/KIAA0319 lineages, names for the exam-oriented, not for clinical use)
- Brain-imaging findings: atypical left-hemisphere language-network organisation and reduced grey matter/activation in temporo-parietal reading areas, research findings, not diagnostic tests
- Prematurity, low birth weight, prenatal alcohol/tobacco exposure raise risk

**Neurodevelopmental / cognitive**
- The core deficits: phonological processing (breaking words into sounds), rapid naming speed, working memory, and for dyscalculia the approximate number system
- DCD co-travels with dyslexia and with ADHD (the "soft sign" cluster)

**Psychological / social**
- No causal role for parenting style or "not trying"; worth stating explicitly to families because it is the commonest home theory
- Environmental DEPRIVATION can mimic the picture (severe under-stimulation), but typical Indian middle-class "screen time" does not cause dyslexia

**Environmental / Indian context**
- The language gap: English-medium instruction for children without English at home, a false-positive machine (and, when reading fails in BOTH languages, a true signal)
- High-stakes, writing-heavy examination culture makes the disability VISIBLE and expensive (one bad board exam is a life event here)
- Large classrooms, late detection; remedial-educator scarcity outside metros
- Protective: rich oral storytelling culture (many dyslexic Indian children have strong oral memory and narration), grandparents' vernacular literacy, and now legal accommodations

## 6. What happens in the brain

**Story 1: the sound-splitting machine.** Reading an alphabetic script is a technological trick the brain was never built for: it must map printed symbols onto SOUNDS and blend them (ka + aa = caa). The child with dyslexia has a sound-processing machine that runs grainy: rhymes come late, "bat" and "pat" sound the same at speed, and breaking "elephant" into e-le-phant is genuinely effortful. If sounds blur, print stays blurred; the problem is upstream of the eye. That is why the myth of "the eye problem" fails: vision is normal; it is the phonological channel that is narrow. Remediation works precisely here; systematic, multisensory phonics re-trains the mapping, which is why the right METHOD (not more tuition) changes the trajectory.

**Story 2: the number-line that never calibrated.** Dyscalculia's core is a magnitude glitch: the approximate number system (the mental number-line that lets any toddler judge "9 dots is more than 5 dots") calibrated poorly. Numbers then become a set of memorised words without a felt quantity behind them; arithmetic facts are learned like a poem in an unknown language. Subitising (instantly seeing "4" without counting), magnitude comparison and number bonds are the training targets.

**Story 3: the slow scribe and the clumsy athlete.** Writing recruits a whole motor-program cascade: form the letter, size it, space it, spell the word, hold the sentence in memory, a juggling act. In dysgraphia/DCD one or two balls keep dropping: handwriting is slow, painful and illegible, and the writing LOAD eats the ideas (the child who tells a brilliant story but writes three dull lines, the cost of the channel leaves nothing for content). DCD's same motor fog shows on the playground, in cycling, in buttoning; often socially punished before it is ever clinically named.

**Story 4: the emotional scar that becomes the disability.** The academic skill is fixed by remediation; the IDENTITY injury from years of "stupid/lazy" labels can outlast it. A Standard 5 child has already had thousands of small humiliations, read-aloud failures, red-ink workbooks, relative comparisons. This is why every management plan must include an emotional component: naming the condition to the child in accurate, non-shaming words ("your brain is built differently, like left-handedness; reading needs a different route, not more scolding").

## 7. Symptoms

**By channel, the classroom signatures:**

*Reading (dyslexia)*
- Slow, effortful, word-by-word reading; guessing from first letters or context; "was" read as "saw"
- Letter/sound confusion (b–d, p–q), reversal of letter order that persists beyond Std 1–2 norms
- Poor spelling that does not improve with repeated dictation; bizarre, non-phonetic spellings
- Reading avoidance behaviour: the child who "hates" reading aloud, volunteers last, develops sudden stomach-aches on library day
- Comprehension intact when material is READ ALOUD (the diagnostic tell: ask the parent to read the chapter, the child then answers everything)

*Writing (dysgraphia)*
- Illegible, slow, cramped or sprawling handwriting; inconsistent letter sizes; hand pain
- Cannot copy from the board in time (finishes last, weeps over homework hours)
- Spelling errors, missing words, fused sentences; oral answers dramatically better than written
- Avoids writing tasks, "forgets" notebooks, prefers objective-type exams

*Arithmetic (dyscalculia)*
- Finger-counting in higher classes; errors in +/− columns (misaligning place value)
- Tables refuse to stick despite drilling; "6 × 7" recalculated each time
- Poor number sense: quantity comparisons, estimation, money change, time-telling struggles
- Word-problems collapse (often compounded by reading difficulty)

*Coordination (DCD, the frequent companion)*
- Clumsiness: cycling late, ball games avoided, tying shoelaces/buttons difficult
- Handwriting affected (overlaps dysgraphia); cutlery, art/craft class struggles
- Being picked last, teased, the social layer

**The emotional career (the symptom set clinicians miss):**
- School avoidance, Sunday-evening dread, somatic complaints (stomach-ache, headache) on school mornings
- Self-statements: "I am dumb", "I hate school", internalised by 9–10 years of age
- Secondary anxiety (especially exam-specific), then adolescent depression or acting-out
- Compensatory brilliance: exceptional oral skills, memory, drawing, verbal wit; the strengths that MISLEAD teachers ("he can't be dyslexic, he talks so well")

## 8. Diagnosis

**The logic (DSM-5/ICD-11 paraphrased):** difficulties learning and using academic skills, persisting for at least 6 months despite targeted intervention, substantially below age expectation and causing functional impairment, not better explained by intellectual disability, uncorrected vision/hearing, other neurological conditions, inadequate instruction, or language-of-instruction mismatch. DSM-5 names the impairment domains (reading accuracy/fluency/comprehension; spelling/written expression; number sense/calculation/mathematical reasoning) with severity specifiers; ICD-11 classifies developmental learning disorders similarly. Both systems have retired the rigid IQ-achievement discrepancy formula.

**The Indian assessment sequence:**
1. **Screening trigger**, the teacher/parent observation of a gap: bright child, poor written output; do not wait for Std 6
2. **Rule out the mimics first**, vision and hearing testing; school-attendance and instruction-quality history; language-of-instruction mapping (which languages at home, which script taught when, and TEST READING IN THE VERNACULAR TOO, not only English); a careful developmental history (speech delay is frequent)
3. **Intelligence testing**: an individually administered IQ battery (named tools exist for Indian children in multiple languages; WISC-lineage and Indian adaptations) to establish that general ability is at least average, this also reveals the child's STRENGTH profile for teaching
4. **Achievement testing**: standardised reading/spelling/arithmetic batteries normed for the child's language/class; fluency (speed) measured, not just accuracy: fluency is the sensitive marker
5. **Process testing** (phonological awareness, rapid naming, working memory, copying speed) the WHY behind the WHAT
6. **Functional evidence**: notebooks, exam papers, homework hours, teacher reports (the cheapest and most honest data in India: look at the actual written output and the board-copy speed)
7. **Comorbidity screen**: ADHD symptoms (present in ~1/3), anxiety, depression, DCD items; a home-behaviour questionnaire

**The formal certificate (Indian legal layer):** diagnosis for boards requires assessment by a multidisciplinary team as per the RPwD Act 2016 framework, typically clinical/educational psychologist plus medical input, issued by the designated district/medical authority. Schools use this to register the child as "child with disability" (usually benchmark-level decisions only matter for severe cases) and boards use it to grant accommodations.

### Differential table

| Looks like a learning disorder | The distinguishing feature |
|---|---|
| Poor schooling / attendance gaps | History of missed instruction; catches up rapidly with instruction, unlike the persistent course of SLD |
| Language-of-instruction mismatch (English-medium, first-generation learner) | Vernacular reading also tested; if vernacular reading is age-appropriate, this is acquisition load, not dyslexia |
| Intellectual disability | Global, not channel-specific; adaptive functioning also below; full-scale IQ below ~70 with pervasive delays |
| ADHD (inattention) | Attention lapses across ALL channels; performance improves with interest/stimulation; stimulant trial improves attention, not reading mechanics |
| Vision/hearing impairment | Corrected by glasses/ENT management; the screening tests |
| Anxiety or depression in school | Recent onset, situation-linked; earlier school years were normal |
| Borderline intellectual functioning | The near-miss: slightly below-average IQ with commensurate (not GAP) achievement, different label, different counselling, same kindness |

## 9. Management

**The triad: remediation + accommodations + comorbidity treatment.** Nothing else has evidence; everything else is sold.

**1: Remediation (the direct skill work).**
- For reading: structured, systematic, multisensory phonics programmes (the Orton–Gillingham lineage and its derivatives), letter-sound links taught incrementally with simultaneous seeing/saying/tracing; repeated oral reading with feedback for fluency; syllable patterns, morphology (affix families) in later stages. One-to-one or small-group, typically 2–4 sessions weekly for 1–2 school years by a trained remedial educator; the DOSE matters; casual "extra classes" are not remediation.
- For writing: handwriting fluency drills (not punishment copying; that worsens the relationship with the pencil), dictation with phonetic spelling instruction, typing introduced early as a parallel track (typing is often the liberation skill for Indian board exams and college).
- For arithmetic: number-sense work (concrete materials, number-line games, dot patterns), overlearned facts in small sets with retrieval practice, errorless money/time practice in daily life.
- For DCD: occupational therapy, motor skills, handwriting posture/grip, daily-living skills (buttons, cycling); task-specific practice works better than generic "motor drills".
- *India note: remedial educators are concentrated in metros (₹500–1,500 per session approx 2026, often more; district towns may have none). Legitimate alternatives: trained special educators at registered centres, school-based resource rooms where they exist, and structured parent-delivered remediation with a proper programme and periodic professional review; parent-mediated practice with correct materials beats an untrained tutor's well-meaning dictation.*

**2: Accommodations (remove the penalty, not the standard).**
- Extra time in examinations (the RPwD framework standard, commonly 20% extra, board-sanctioned)
- A scribe/amanuensis for board exams where writing is the impaired channel (conditions per board circulars; the child should be trained to work WITH a scribe beforehand, a live skill, not a last-day arrangement)
- Reduced writing load day-to-day: photocopied notes where copying is the bottleneck, homework capped by TIME not completeness, oral testing permitted in primary classes
- Exemption/substitution of the third language where the boards allow (the classic accommodation for SLD in India)
- Seating, larger print, and reading-aloud of maths word-problems in lower classes where comprehension is intact but decoding is not
- The National Institute of Open Schooling (NIOS) route for Std 10/12, flexible subjects and pace, a genuine Indian solution for the child crushed by board rigidity
- *India note: accommodations require the RPwD certificate route via the designated authority; CBSE/ICSE/State boards all have standing circulars; the school's special-educator or counsellor files them. If the school "does not believe in it", show the circular, not the argument.*

**3: Comorbidity treatment.**
- ADHD: treating it (behavioural parent training; medication where indicated: see the ADHD note) often doubles the value of every remediation rupee: an inattentive child cannot use remediation.
- Anxiety/depression: brief CBT, school liaison, family work; treating mood is treating learning.
- Speech-language therapy where comorbid language impairment exists.

**4: The family and child conversations (medicine in words).**
- Name it to the child: "your reading circuit is wired differently; smart brain, one narrow gate; we teach it differently, not louder."
- Kill the three home theories explicitly: laziness, bad upbringing, "father was also weak in studies so fate" (the last contains a truth, heredity, wrapped in fatalism).
- Stop the counterproductive: public read-aloud shaming, extra nightly dictation, tuition multiplication.
- Feed the strengths deliberately (narration, art, sport, debate, practical intelligence); the child's identity needs a place where they are competent EVERY week.
- Homework hygiene: fixed short duration, parent as helper not examiner, teacher coordination to cap volume.
- Screen time is not the cause; but a drowning child who escapes into screens loses practice time, structure without moralising.

**5: Follow-up.**
- Re-assess the gap annually; adjust accommodations as demands change ( Std 4 problem = reading; Std 10 problem = exam logistics; college problem = note-taking and typing).
- Watch for the adolescent mood dip; the transition years ( Std 8–10, board year) are the danger zones.

*India note (approx 2026): costs vary wildly, full psycho-educational assessment ₹3,000–15,000 in metros (government hospital psychology departments and district diagnostic centres far cheaper, sometimes free); remedial sessions ₹500–1,500 each; occupational therapy ₹400–1,000 per session; the RPwD certificate route itself costs little but needs persistence. Therapy-wise: there are no medicines for learning disorders; any "memory syrup/brain tonic" prescription is placebo commerce, a sentence worth saying verbatim to Indian parents.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **The detection bottleneck is Std 3–4, not the clinic.** The child who will be your SLD patient is sitting in a classroom being called lazy right now. The highest-yield Indian intervention is teacher sensitisation (the "bright child, bad notebook" pattern) and one screening question to parents: "does he understand the chapter when YOU read it aloud?" A yes with poor independent reading is the two-minute triage.
- **The English-medium trap.** Assess in the home language. A first-generation English-medium learner reading a year behind in English but at level in Marathi does not have dyslexia; the child behind in BOTH does. This single discipline prevents both false certificates and false reassurance.
- **The RPwD 2016 legal channel.** Specific learning disability is a recognised benchmark disability category; certification is via the designated authority (usually a medical board with a clinical/educational psychologist); the certificate unlocks board accommodations, NIOS flexibility, and anti-discrimination protection. Schools increasingly have a process; the gap is uniform enforcement and teacher attitude.
- **Board-exam engineering.** The Std 10 board is the Indian SLD family's Everest. Plan by Std 8: certificate in place by Std 9 registration, scribe training begun early, typing as a parallel skill, third-language exemption applied for, school's written file built. A scribe introduced one month before boards is a half-measure; the accommodation works when the child has practised the FORMAT as much as the content.
- **The tuition economy works against these children.** The default Indian response to academic failure, more tuition, multiplies written work for a child whose problem IS written work, and hands them to teachers who read their slowness as disrespect. Redirect the tuition budget toward remediation and accommodations.
- **Family systems.** The grandfather who asks "in our time everyone read fine" needs one sentence: "in your time, the ones who couldn't read left school at Std 5 and farmed; they are not in your memory because the school threw them out, not because they didn't exist." The anxious mother doing nightly dictation needs the homework-cap conversation. Siblings should be briefed to stop the "stupid" label at home.
- **Adulthood.** Indian adults with unrecognised SLD land in careers selected by survival, not aptitude. In clinic practice, a 25-year-old with exam-related panic and a childhood story of shame notebooks is worth screening, adult certification exists for professional-course accommodations too.

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: the lazy boy of Standard 4.** An 8-year-old boy in an English-medium Nagpur school was brought with the school's ultimatum: "remove him or hold him back." Teachers described him as bright in discussion but "lazy in written work". Homework took three weeping hours; he read the textbook word-by-word with guessing; notebooks were cramped and incomplete; he had begun saying "I am the dumb one of the class." Father: an engineer who admitted, unprompted, "I also hated reading, I only manage because I memorise." On assessment: verbal comprehension above average, reading fluency in English 2.5 standards below, reading fluency in Hindi (his home language) 2 standards below, phonological awareness weak (could not segment "elephant"), rapid naming slow; vision and hearing normal; ADHD screen positive (teacher-rated inattention high), but the academic pattern predated and exceeded the attention story. Diagnosis: specific learning disorder with impairment in reading and written expression (moderate), with comorbid ADHD (inattentive presentation) and secondary school-related anxiety. Management: the school shown the "bright in oral, poor in print" map; parent conference replaced read-aloud punishment with private reading practice; structured phonics remediation 3×/week with a trained educator; accommodations: photocopied notes, homework capped at 40 minutes by teacher agreement, oral testing permitted; ADHD treated with parent-training plus morning methylphenidate on school days with weight/BP monitoring; the mother coached on the "aloud comprehension test" so she could prove to herself the mind was intact. One year later: reading at one standard below, notebooks legible, "dumb" self-talk gone, best subject declared "maths when the teacher reads out the problems". Teaching points: (1) the two-minute parent triage question ("does he understand when read aloud?") reframed the family's theory in one evening; (2) both-language testing prevented the pure-English misdiagnosis; (3) treating ADHD was not optional; remediation does not attach to an inattentive child; (4) the emotional plan is not decoration; the self-concept was the second disease.

**Case 2: the girl who needed the scribe.** A 15-year-old Std 10 student in Kochi was referred by her school counsellor for assessment "as late as possible": severe dysgraphia (brilliant oral answers, marks that crashed in written exams) first identified informally in Std 6, never certified, and now facing the board in five months with a writing speed at one-third of class average and boards demanding three-hour papers. Assessment confirmed above-average intelligence, reading comprehension intact, spelling impaired, graphomotor speed and processing speed markedly below norms; DCD history (cycling late, poor cutting in craft). Management: crisis triage, the RPwD certification route expedited through the district medical board with the school's documentation; scribe permission obtained per board circular with a computer-typing scribe option explored; scribe TRAINING sessions organised (practice exams dictating to the actual scribe format, answer-structure commands, time discipline); typing parallel classes begun (for internal use); writing load reduced through photocopied notes and a note-sharing classmate arrangement; the anxiety treated with three sessions of exam-focused CBT and a study timetable honouring her oral-learning style (self-recorded revision audio). Results: boards written with scribe and extra time, score 20 percentage points above her mock-exam trajectory, stream of choice secured. Teaching points: (1) late identification happens; the rescue still works, but five months is triage, not care; Std 8 is when the board plan should start; (2) scribe is a SKILL pair; train the child and the scribe together; (3) accommodations did not lower her standard; they finally measured her knowledge instead of her hand; (4) the school counsellor who pushed the late referral deserved the credit; one staff member who knows the law changes a school's whole decade.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way parents ask it

**"His eyes were tested, they are fine. So why can't he read?"** Because reading happens in the language-sound part of the brain, not the eye. His difficulty is in splitting words into sounds and mapping letters to them, an upstream wiring difference. Glasses fix the eye; phonics teaching fixes the mapping.

**"Is he lazy? He finishes his video games fast enough."** No. Effort on games with instant reward and no writing is easy for him; effort that produces humiliation is avoided by every human, including you and me. His avoidance is the INJURY showing, not the cause of the problem.

**"Will he grow out of it?"** The reading becomes manageable with the right teaching, many dyslexic adults read well, but the brain difference is lifelong, which is why spelling and slow reading-under-pressure remain the tell. What he can absolutely outgrow is the "stupid" label. What he should never have to outgrow is the accommodations he needs.

**"We tried tuition for two years. Why didn't it help?"** Tuition gives MORE of the same instruction that already failed him. Remediation gives a DIFFERENT instruction, phonics taught step by step, multisensory, at his level. It also caps the writing load; tuition multiplies it. That is why the results differ.

**"Is it because we put him in English-medium too early?"** If he is behind ONLY in English but reads his mother tongue at level, then yes; that is a language-exposure gap and it closes with support. If he is behind in both languages, the school did not cause it; the wiring did. We test both before answering this question, not after assuming it.

**"The teacher makes him read aloud to 'build confidence'. Is that right?"** Public read-aloud for a dyslexic child builds the opposite of confidence; it is the single most reliably humiliating practice in his week. Reading practice belongs private, with parent or educator, with material he can already mostly read. Tell the teacher this specifically; give her the replacement job (let him answer the comprehension questions, which he can win).

**"What exactly does the disability certificate get us?"** Three things in boards: extra time (commonly one-fifth more), a scribe where writing is the problem, and exemption or substitution of the third language where allowed, plus the NIOS open-schooling option and legal protection against discrimination. The certificate route goes through the designated medical/assessment authority and the school files the board paperwork.

**"Are there any medicines or brain tonics?"** No. There are none, and the syrups sold for this are commerce. The only medicines that ever help are ones for OTHER conditions riding along, like ADHD, which we screen for in every child.

**"Will she manage college and a job?"** With typing, accommodations, and a self-aware choice of stream, yes, and often brilliantly. Courts, engineering, medicine, design, business: dyslexic graduates populate all of them. The predictor of adult outcome is not the severity of the reading gap; it is whether the child reached adulthood with self-esteem and skills intact.

**"His father also struggled. Did we pass this on?"** Largely, yes: this condition runs strongly in families; it is inherited wiring, not inherited fault. The useful part of that fact: it lets the father finally understand his own school years, and that understanding changes how he speaks to his son.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**The one-line definition for theory answers:** specific learning disorder = persistent difficulty acquiring accurate/fluent reading, writing or arithmetic skills, ~6 months despite intervention, below age-normative expectations, out of proportion to overall ability, with sensory/intellectual/instructional causes excluded.

**Mnemonic ("RWA + C" channels:** Reading, Writing, Arithmetic) plus Coordination (DCD, the co-traveller). Or the Indian classroom mnemonic "the 3 notebooks": the reader who can't read the text, the writer who can't fill the page, the counter who can't keep the columns.

**High-yield facts:**
- Dyslexia's core = phonological processing deficit, NOT visual/eye disease (the classic exam trap)
- Prevalence 5–15% broadly; reading ~5–10%; male predominance with referral bias
- Comorbid ADHD in roughly one-third, and it gates remediation response
- Emotional/behavioural cascade of unidentified SLD: anxiety → avoidance → depression/conduct
- Best-evidenced remediation: structured systematic multisensory phonics (Orton–Gillingham lineage)
- DSM-5/ICD-11 have dropped IQ-discrepancy formulas; persistence despite intervention + functional impairment is the logic
- Indian legal anchor: RPwD Act 2016 → certificate → board accommodations (extra time ~20%, scribe, third-language exemption; NIOS route)
- No pharmacotherapy for SLD itself; "brain tonics" are not medicine

**Classic traps:**
1. Labeling the first-generation English-medium learner dyslexic without vernacular reading testing
2. Missing ADHD riding underneath (treating reading while ignoring attention)
3. Missing vision/hearing basics before psychological testing
4. Mistaking borderline intellectual functioning for SLD (profile: consistent, not gapped)
5. Recommending "more writing practice" for dysgraphia (compounding the disability with the punishment)
6. Certifying at Std 10 board-eve with an untrained scribe (the accommodation needs rehearsal)

**Indian exam corner:** expected patterns, "a 9-year-old with average intelligence and reading 2 years below class level: diagnosis, aetiology and management" (the vignette nearly writes itself); "specific learning disabilities under RPwD Act 2016"; "role of remedial education"; "learning disorder vs intellectual disability differentiation". Indian-context marks: bilingual assessment discipline, the tuition-economy critique, board accommodations, NIOS.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. The core cognitive deficit in developmental dyslexia:**
A. Defective visual acuity
B. Phonological processing (sound segmentation and letter-sound mapping)
C. Reduced overall intelligence
D. Poor motivation
**Answer: B.** The phonological channel: upstream of the eye; the "eye problem" is the classic distractor.

**2. A Std 3 first-generation English-medium child reads a year below level in English. Before diagnosing dyslexia you must:**
A. Start phonics immediately
B. Test reading in the home/vernacular language as well
C. Prescribe glasses
D. Repeat the same test after 6 months
**Answer: B.** Vernacular testing separates bilingual acquisition load from dyslexia, the Indian assessment discipline.

**3. The most common comorbidity multiplying academic damage in learning disorders:**
A. Epilepsy
B. ADHD
C. Asthma
D. Enuresis
**Answer: B.** Present in roughly a third; untreated, it gates remediation response.

**4. The best-evidenced remediation approach for reading disorder:**
A. More after-school tuition in the same method
B. Structured systematic multisensory phonics
C. Vision exercises
D. Repeated public reading-aloud
**Answer: B.** The Orton–Gillingham lineage: a different method, not a bigger dose.

**5. Under RPwD 2016 certification, an Indian board may grant:**
A. Nothing: SLD is not recognised
B. Extra time, a scribe and third-language exemption (per board circulars)
C. Automatic promotion without exams
D. Only NIOS admission
**Answer: B.** SLD is a recognised disability; accommodations are examination-logistics changes, not standard reductions.

**6. An 11-year-old with dysgraphia, identified late, faces boards next year. The pair that best describes correct action:**
A. Certificate route + trained scribe with practice exams
B. Nightly extra writing practice + no certificate
C. Withdrawal from school + no exams
D. Brain-tonic course + dictation tutoring
**Answer: A.** Accommodations work when rehearsed, the scribe pair trains together; certificates precede board registration deadlines.

**7. "My son is not lazy" is best supported in clinic by which finding?**
A. Above-average verbal comprehension with reading fluency far below class norms
B. All subjects uniformly below average
C. Scores improving with any teacher who shouts less
D. Reading problems starting only this year
**Answer: A.** The GAP between intact oral comprehension and impaired print processing is the "unexpected underachievement" signature.
