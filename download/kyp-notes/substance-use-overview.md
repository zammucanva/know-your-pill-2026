# Substance use — the pharmacology and psychology overview

## 1. In one line + overview

**Tagline:** Every addictive substance works by **hijacking one of the brain's three reward currencies** (dopamine's "wanting", opioid "liking", or the calm of the stress systems) and every addiction syndrome, from arrack to heroin to vapes, then follows the same **wheel: use → tolerance → withdrawal → craving → relapse**, which is why one set of treatment principles covers them all.

**Overview, in plain words:** This is the umbrella note for the whole substance section; the drug-specific notes (alcohol, opioids, stimulants, cannabis, benzodiazepines, nicotine, hallucinogens, inhalants, MDMA) hang off it. Two ideas carry everything. **First, the pharmacology is simpler than it looks:** pleasure and motivation run on a handful of chemical systems; drugs are just chemicals that push those systems harder and faster than life ever can, and the brain, being adaptive, recalibrates (that recalibration is tolerance and withdrawal). **Second, the psychology is deeper than the chemistry:** people *start* for pleasure or belonging, but they *stay* for relief (of pain, shame, sleeplessness, trauma) and any treatment that ignores the relief-function relapses. India's pattern matters here: alcohol dominates the burden by far, followed by opioids (a genuine epidemic belt through the north-west), cannabis and sedatives; tobacco sits silently inside almost every other case; and the youngest wave is now vapes and internet-adjacent habits. The through-line of good care, for every substance: **screen with two shame-free questions, treat withdrawal safely, replace the hijacked reward with structured recovery and connection, and treat the pain underneath.**

## 2. Learning objectives

1. Draw the three-system reward map: mesolimbic dopamine ("wanting"), opioid endorphin ("liking"), and stress-relief (anti-reward) systems.
2. Define tolerance, withdrawal, craving, and the hijacked learning cycle; explain reward set-point shift in plain words.
3. Recite the DSM-5/ICD-11 move from "abuse vs dependence" to the **severity-graded single disorder** and its 11-signal logic.
4. Screen efficiently with two questions, and take a shame-free drug history (route, amount, pattern, consequences).
5. Build the management skeleton that all substances share: brief intervention → detoxification → relapse-prevention pharmacotherapy → psychosocial recovery → comorbidity treatment → long-term monitoring.
6. Explain the comorbidity rule ("self-medication is a hypothesis, not a diagnosis") and how to sequence dual-diagnosis treatment.
7. Map the Indian substance landscape and the National programmes and laws that shape practice.

## 4. Epidemiology

### Global
- Roughly **240–290 million people** live with drug use disorders worldwide (WHO/UNODC estimates of recent years), with alcohol the largest single contributor of illness and death among all substances (driven by volume, not by dangerousness per litre).
- The median global treatment coverage for drug use disorders is about **1 in 9 people**, one of the largest treatment gaps in medicine.
- Comorbidity is the rule: depression, anxiety, PTSD and personality difficulties travel with substance disorders at rates far above chance.

### India
- The Alcohol Information System/National surveys lineage: roughly **one in three adult men drinks**; about **a quarter to a third of current drinkers** meet hazardous or harmful use thresholds; alcohol is a leading risk factor for Indian male mortality and disability.
- **Opioid belt:** the north-western states carry the highest opioid use and misuse rates in the country; pharmaceutical opioids (tramadol-type, then heroin) drive the current wave.
- Cannabis, sedatives/whitener-type inhalants among street youth, and injection use with HIV/HCV transmission complete the pattern; **tobacco co-use is near-universal among the rest** and must be treated alongside, not separately.
- The treatment infrastructure reality: a small number of de-addiction centres (mostly government and NGO), one national tele-line (14446, NASHA Mukti helpline) and tele-MANAS linkage; rural availability is scarce.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

- **Genetic/biological:** heritability of addiction liability roughly 40–60%; variations in metabolism (e.g., the flushing ALDH2 variant common in East Asians, present in many Indians, makes heavy drinking aversive but does not make everyone safe); early-onset use; co-occurring mental disorders; chronic pain (for opioid misuse).
- **Psychological:** relief-seeking (dysphoria, anxiety, shame, insomnia); impulsivity and low distress tolerance; trauma and adverse childhood events (dose-response with later addiction); expectancies ("it helps me socialise/sleep").
- **Social/environmental:** availability and price; peer and family norms (drinking as male bonding); occupation (military, transport, hotel industry, migrant labour away from family); displacement, unemployment and poverty; marketing (toddy-shop economies, social media for vapes).
- **Developmental:** age of first exposure (the earlier the use, the higher the later disorder risk; the prefrontal control system is still under construction till the mid-20s).
- **Indian context:** all of the above plus easy non-prescription access to benzodiazepines and codeine/cough-syrup products in pharmacies; state-level prohibition policies creating surrogate/unsafe supplies; and family concealment delaying care by years.

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: the three currencies.** The brain pays for motivation in three currencies. **Dopamine (wanting)** says "that, again", every drug of dependence floods this system 2–10 times beyond natural rewards. **Endorphins (liking)** give the warmth of comfort. And a stress-relief system lets danger fade. Addictive drugs cash all three accounts at once; ordinary life (a meal, a cricket innings, a grandchild's laugh) pays in rupees where drugs pay in lakhs. The brain's response to being overpaid: it **resets the exchange rate** (receptors down-regulate) so that normal rewards now feel grey. That reset is the disease.

**Story 2: the recalibrated thermostat (tolerance, withdrawal, allostasis).** Think of reward as a thermostat set at "content". Chronic drug money forces the system to lower its set-point to survive the flood, ordinary joy now reads as loss. When the drug stops, the thermostat sits *below* content: that is withdrawal, not a punishment, but the system still paying for the overdraft. With each detox cycle the alarm fires earlier (kindling), which is why "he can stop, he's done it twice" is the misunderstanding of the century: each stop is harder than the last.

**Story 3: the school of cues.** The dopamine system is a learning machine: it stamps "important; repeat!" on whatever coincided with the high, the friend, the lane, the smell, the paycheck day, the evening mood. Years later those cues re-fire craving before conscious thought. This is why recovery is the management of *conditions*, not only of the person: new routes, new evenings, new friends, the treatment world calls it stimulus control; the family calls it staying away from the old crowd.

## 7. Symptoms (the universal syndrome, any substance)

**Impaired control:** using more or longer than intended; failed rules ("only weekends"); strong craving; cycles of cut-down-and-return.

**Pharmacological adaptation:** tolerance (need more for the same effect, or clearly diminished effect at the same amount); withdrawal syndrome that is substance-specific (the notes that follow give each pattern) and relieved by taking the drug again.

**Harm despite harm:** continued use with physical complications (liver, lungs, infections, injuries), psychological ones (paranoia, depression), and social ones (job loss, debt, violence, court cases, family rupture), the "risk" cluster that also includes using in situations of danger (driving, operating machinery).

**Course markers worth naming aloud:** first-use age, route escalations (oral → smoking/injection), years to daily use, longest clean period ever and *what ended it*; the single most informative question in relapse planning.

## 8. Diagnosis

### The DSM-5 / ICD-11 logic (paraphrased)
DSM-5 merged "abuse" and "dependence" into **one substance use disorder graded by signal count** (2–3 mild, 4–5 moderate, 6+ severe) across four clusters: **impaired control** (larger/longer use, failed cutting, craving), **social impairment** (role failure, conflict, withdrawal from activities), **risky use** (hazardous contexts, physical/psychological harm despite knowledge), and **pharmacology** (tolerance, withdrawal). Intoxication and withdrawal are coded separately when present. ICD-11 keeps a similar single-disorder architecture with dependence's core (a strong desire, impaired control, tolerance, withdrawal, continued despite harm) at its centre. One clinical translation: **"dependence" today means the moderate-to-severe end of one dimension, not a different species.**

### Assessment package
- **Two shame-free screeners** (any setting): "In the last year, have you found you needed more to get the same effect?" and "Have you had times when you used more than you meant to?": positive = full history.
- **Full history:** substance by substance (never stop at the referred one); amounts in concrete units (quarters, pints, ₹ of the day); route; pattern; last use; longest clean period and its ending; previous treatments; legal and financial consequences.
- **The relief question (do not skip):** "What does it do *for* you?, the sleep, the nerves, the anger, the memories?": the answer is the relapse-prevention curriculum.
- **Examination:** injection marks, pupils, tremor, liver, nutrition; mental state (mood, psychosis, cognition).
- **Investigations as indicated:** LFTs/GGT and CDT for alcohol; HIV/HCV testing for injection use; urine/blood screens in crises; pregnancy screen where relevant.
- **Screen for comorbidity always** (depression, PTSD, ADHD, psychosis, personality pattern), but treat as hypothesis (see management).

## 9. Management: the universal skeleton

### Step 1: Match the response to the stage
- **Hazardous use without dependence:** a brief intervention (feedback of personal risk, responsibility framed respectfully, menu of options, empathic style, self-efficacy reinforcement, the FRAMES-style consultation, 10 minutes, documented) meaningfully reduces consumption.
- **Dependence:** the full skeleton below.

### Step 2: Safe withdrawal (detoxification)
Substance-specific protocols (each drug note carries its own); universal rules: **medical risk first** (alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal can kill; opioid withdrawal makes you wish it would), hydration/nutrition/thiamine in alcohol, and withdrawal as the *entry door* to treatment, never the treatment itself.

### Step 3: Relapse-prevention pharmacotherapy
The best-kept secret of Indian de-addiction care: maintenance medicines (naltrexone, acamprosate, buprenorphine, methadone, varenicline/NRT, disulfiram) roughly double abstinence odds where prescribed and supervised, they belong in the community, not only in centres. Each class is detailed in its own note.

### Step 4: Psychosocial recovery (the actual engine)
- **Structure:** daily routine, employment, exercise; idle afternoons are the relapse capital of India.
- **Stimulus control:** mapped personal cues (people, places, paydays) with pre-planned alternatives.
- **Skilled helping:** motivational interviewing to open the door, relapse-prevention CBT to walk through it, contingency/award structures where available, family intervention for the household system, peer groups (AA/NA lineage; theistic variants like the Christian-zone and Indian temple/ashram-based recoveries work for those who accept their idiom).
- **Relapse reframed:** a lapse is data, not damnation, "what did it teach us about the map?"

### Step 5: Comorbidity and the pain underneath
Treat depression, PTSD, insomnia, ADHD, chronic pain **in parallel, not in sequence**; "get clean first and then we'll treat the depression" fails both ways. But test the self-medication hypothesis honestly: if mood disorder genuinely antedated and drives use, treating it is relapse prevention; if it is withdrawal painting the mind grey, the picture clears in 2–6 weeks clean. Do not diagnose a new psychiatric disorder inside the first weeks of abstinence unless it is dangerous.

### Step 6: Long-term monitoring
Chronic-disease model: reviews over years, relapse-care plans written in advance (who calls whom first), family education about responding to relapse (calm, fast, non-moralising), and recovery-capital building (work, relationships, meaning) as the ongoing prescription.

*India note: the affordable spine of Indian care is generic pharmacotherapy + family contract + 12-step-adjacent groups + telephonic follow-up (NASHA helpline 14446; tele-MANAS linkage); the scarce items are beds for risky detox and trained counsellors, so the family-doctor-plus-franchise model carries the national load (as in the dementia umbrella note).*

## 10. Indian practice

- **Laws and programmes that shape care:** the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act (NDPS) governs opioids; the COTPA Act governs tobacco; the Ayushman Bharat/PM-JAY covers some hospital detox events; the Ministry of Social Justice-funded de-addiction and District De-Addiction Centres plus NIMHANS-led national surveys form the data backbone.
- **Pharmacy culture:** benzos, codeine syrups and tramadol are still purchasable without prescription in practice; part of every treatment plan is the "one pharmacy for one family, with the family informed" arrangement.
- **Family as treatment infrastructure:** the Indian family will supervise, hide, exhaust and sometimes rescue; engage them from the first visit with a written contract (medicines held by whom; money rules; relapse response) rather than the late "family counselling" of Western textbooks.
- **Stigma and concealment:** presenting complaints are usually sleep, appetite, weakness or "gas"; screen shame-free behind those doors; the respectful two questions beat the judgmental twenty.
- **Occupational pipelines:** transport workers, hotel staff, migrant labour and student hostels; each needs a setting-specific brief-intervention habit; an employer letter converts "termination" into "leave for treatment" (the TBI note's same instrument).

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: the driver who asked for "sleep medicine".** A 38-year-old lorry driver on the Delhi–Mumbai route presented for insomnia; shame-free screening revealed quarter-bottle rum nightly for 12 years, morning shakiness, and two roadside accidents "narrowly missed". GGT was three-fold raised. He had hazardous-to-moderate dependence without organ failure: brief intervention, taper-assisted detox planning for his off-route week, disulfiram discussion with the co-driver as medicine-keeper, family contract with wife, route-cue plan (the dhaba where he always drank, pre-packed dinner alternative), and quarterly telephonic follow-up. Two relapses in year one, both mapped (payday; a breakdown delay), both survived without dropping out; stable in year two.

*Teaching points: presenting complaints conceal; screening must be shame-free; the treatment was 10 minutes of medicine and 50 minutes of conditions; relapse as data kept him in care.*

**Case 2: the student sent for " cannabis psychosis"** is detailed in the cannabis note; here the overview lesson: three comorbidities (use, mood, and study structure) treated in parallel, not sequentially, and the self-medication hypothesis ("smoking to silence racing thoughts") tested and confirmed by treating the underlying anxiety.

*Teaching points: parallel treatment of comorbidity; the relief question ("what does it do for you?") as the diagnostic key.*

## 13. FAQ

**"Is addiction a disease or a weakness of character?"**
It is a treatable condition of the brain's reward and control systems, with real genetics, real brain changes and a real relapsing course, like diabetes with behaviour at its centre. Willpower is part of treatment; it is not the cause of the illness.

**"He stopped twice on his own. Doesn't that prove he can stop again?"**
It proves the illness's signature: the ability to stop is not the same as the ability to stay stopped, and each withdrawal episode typically resets worse. The goal of treatment is not another heroic stop; it is engineering the next month.

**"Why does he say drugs are the only thing that makes life normal?"**
Because his reward thermostat has been re-set by the drug: ordinary pleasures read as grey in early recovery. That greyness is expected, temporary (weeks to months), and it is the most dangerous window, planned for, not judged.

**"Can it be treated with medicines?"**
Yes, depending on the substance, medicines roughly double the odds of staying clean (blocking, substituting or distress-reducing agents), alongside, never instead of; the conditions work.

**"What is our family's job?"**
Hold the medicines, guard the money rules, respond to relapse calmly and fast, protect your own health, and rebuild the relationship around everything except the drug; the family is the treatment infrastructure of India.

**"Is one beer / one smoke after treatment ever okay?"**
For most substances, "just one" is the relapse's opening sentence, because the learned system reawakens quickly. The plan for lapse is written before it happens, not improvised after.

**"Why does he crave at the same corner every evening, years after stopping?"**
Cue learning: the brain stamped that corner with importance. The treatment names those cues and re-routes life around them; this is why recovery is the management of conditions.

**"Are the newer vapes safer?"**
"Less burnt" is not "safe": nicotine dependence, adolescent brain effects and cardiovascular risk remain; and every adolescent nicotine device is a masterclass in cue-learning for future substances.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **Mnemonics:** the three currencies as **"Want, Like, Calm"** (dopamine-endorphin-stress-relief); DSM clusters as **"Control-Social-Risk-Pharma"**; the brief-intervention structure as **FRAMES** (feedback, responsibility, advice, menu, empathy, self-efficacy).
- **Highest-yield facts:** heritability 40–60%; treatment coverage ~1 in 9 globally; alcohol's burden primacy; kindling; cue-induced craving outlasting detox; adolescent prefrontal immaturity as the vulnerability window; pharmacotherapy doubling abstinence odds.
- **Classic traps:** (1) diagnosing major depression in week two of abstinence (withdrawal paints grey); (2) "detox = treatment"; (3) treating tobacco as trivial in an alcohol-dependent patient; (4) accepting self-medication as treatment rationale without testing it; (5) missed codeine/tramadol dependence behind "cough".
- **Viva favourites:** allostasis and reward set-point; agonist-antagonist-aversive pharmacotherapy logic; motivational interviewing's spirit; contingency management evidence; the kindling concept; NDPS/COTPA framing.
- **Indian exam corner:** national survey numbers (male drinking prevalence; opioid belt states); NASHA helpline 14446; district de-addiction centre network; pharmacy-access problem; family-contract model as a short-answer structure.
- **Rehearse one line:** "Addiction is the brain's reward system doing exactly what it was built to do, with the wrong currency; treat the thermostat, the cues, and the pain underneath."

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. The dopamine system most directly encodes:**
A. Pleasure of consumption
B. "Wanting"/incentive salience: the pull toward repetition
C. Physical withdrawal severity
D. Memory storage

**Answer: B.** The wanting/liking dissociation: dopamine drives pursuit; endorphins give the warm liking, and relapse is re-awakened wanting.

**2. DSM-5 changed substance diagnosis by:**
A. Adding more substance categories
B. Merging abuse and dependence into one severity-graded disorder across four clusters
C. Removing tolerance and withdrawal
D. Making dependence purely a social construct

**Answer: B.** One disorder, 11 signals, four clusters (control, social, risky use, pharmacology), graded mild–moderate–severe.

**3. Two questions adequate for screening in any Indian OPD are:**
A. Family history of psychosis
B. "Need more for the same effect?" and "Used more than you meant to?"
C. Weight loss and sleep
D. Occupation and income

**Answer: B.** Shame-free tolerance/control probes with high yield; ask behind presenting complaints like "insomnia" or "weakness".

**4. Kindling refers to:**
A. Cue-induced craving
B. Successive withdrawal episodes becoming progressively more severe
C. Tolerance to euphoria
D. Cross-tolerance between drug classes

**Answer: B.** Especially alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal: the reason early, treated detox beats heroic home stops.

**5. "Self-medication" in a comorbid patient should be used as:**
A. A proven fact guiding prescription
B. A hypothesis to be tested, while treating both fronts in parallel
C. A reason to delay psychiatric treatment
D. A reason to delay addiction treatment

**Answer: B.** Test the direction by treating both; never let the hypothesis sequence the care in either order.

**6. The single most informative relapse-planning question in the history is:**
A. Age at first use
B. Longest clean period ever, and what ended it
C. Preferred brand
D. Family history

**Answer: B.** What broke the longest remission is a map of the patient's personal relapse triggers; everything else is background.
