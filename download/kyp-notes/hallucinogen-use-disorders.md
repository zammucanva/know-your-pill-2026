# Hallucinogens (LSD, Psilocybin, PCP)

## 1. In one line + overview

**Hallucinogens are the great exception among recreational drugs: they hijack no reward circuit, create no physical withdrawal, and rarely produce the addictive syndrome, but they produce psychiatry's most dramatic acute emergencies (the panic-terror bad trip), its most misunderstood after-effect (the flashback disorder), and, in PCP's separate violent kingdom, the most dangerous intoxication in the street catalogue.**

The classic hallucinogens, LSD, psilocybin (the mushroom and its modern synthetic cousins), DMT and ayahuasca, mescaline, act at one receptor (5-HT2A) to loosen the cortex's grip on perception and selfhood: sensory doors swing open, time stretches, the boundary of "I" dissolves. Use is occasional and self-limiting in most people: tolerance builds in days and the experience itself is too demanding to run daily. The clinical load therefore sits in three places: the acute panic or psychotic reaction (managing the terror, not the visions), the persisting perceptual disorder (flashbacks and visual snow after the trip), and the precipitation or unmasking of psychotic illness in vulnerable people. PCP (angel dust, and its cousin ketamine at street doses) is pharmacologically a different family, the dissociatives, included in this note's source chapter and in exams; its intoxication is the one that arrives violent, numb, and nystagmic. The Indian reality: general-population hallucinogen use is negligible, the drugs live in the Goa rave circuit, metro elite party scenes, darknet-postal supply, and the accidental-and-undersuspected category of "that tablet was not what the dealer said"; the practical Indian skill is recognition and calm management, not epidemic management.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Explain why hallucinogens produce dependence rarely: no classic reward hijack, rapid tolerance, no withdrawal, and state the clinical consequence: an alcohol-style addiction framework mostly does not apply
- Describe the 5-HT2A mechanism in plain words: cortical excitation, perceptual loosening, ego-dissolution, and why vital signs stay near-normal (the intoxication that looks worse than it is)
- Manage the acute bad trip: environment, reassurance-and-talking-down, benzodiazepines when needed, restraint avoidance, and the antipsychotic caution in pure hallucinogen crises
- Diagnose and manage hallucinogen persisting perceptual disorder (HPPD): flashbacks, visual snow, palinopsia, with the reassurance-first, medication-sparingly ladder
- Recognise PCP/ketamine dissociative intoxication: the violent-numb-nystagmic triad, the analgesic injuries, and its specific management (quiet room, benzos, avoid haloperidol-only strategies)
- Separate the mimics: NBOMe-sold-as-LSD, MDMA-with-tablet-substitution, temporal-lobe aura, migraine aura, primary psychosis
- Place psilocybin's therapeutic-research era honestly: trials for depression/PTSD/end-of-life distress are research, not clinic, and India's frame for it
- Work Indian realities: the Goa circuit, NDPS scheduling, darknet supply, the undersuspected tablet, and the family's "spiritual journey" presentation

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Lifetime hallucinogen use in Western surveys runs in single digits of adults (US NSDUH-tier around 8–10% lifetime for the class, past-month use under 1%), a pattern of episodic experimentation rather than daily use, concentrated in young men, in party and festival circuits, and in certain spiritual-seeking subcultures. PCP use in the United States is regional and small; ketamine misuse has risen with its own dance-culture and, separately, its depression-clinic off-label fame (the two must not be conflated). The ayahuasca and psilocybin-ceremony circuits have globalised (retreats in Latin America, Europe and now Asia) carrying both low-harm statistics in screened ceremonial contexts and individual psychiatric casualties in unscreened ones.

**India.** Population-survey hallucinogen numbers are negligible; National-survey illicit-drug frames (the Ministry-affiliated AIIMS Magnitude-of-Substance-Use 2019 surveys) show opioids, cannabis and sedatives as the national load, with hallucinogens barely registering in general samples. The realistic Indian map: the Goa and coastal-festival circuit, metro nightclub and elite party scenes, darknet-and-postal supply of LSD blotter and psilocybin to urban professionals and students, occasional mushroom-picking experimentation in the Himalayan belt, and, most important clinically because least suspected, hallucinogenic surprises inside tablets sold as MDMA/ecstasy (the substituted-pill problem: NBOMe-family sold as LSD, PMMA and cocktails sold as ecstasy). PCP is essentially absent from Indian streets; ketamine appears in party circuits and diversion, which matters because dissociative mislabelling travels under tranquiliser or club-drug names.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

- **Psychological**: sensation-seeking and openness (the personality profile of most experimental users), novelty-seeking youth, spiritual-seeking adults (the ayahuasca and "medicine-journey" route), trauma-driven self-experimentation
- **Social/environmental**: festival, rave and nightclub circuits; peer initiation (first dose almost always social); darknet access lowering friction; retreat-tourism
- **Biological vulnerability, the critical risk tags**: personal or family history of psychosis (the precipitation risk), bipolar-spectrum instability (mania and mixed states triggered), unstable cardiac conduction (the sympathomimetic load), PCP's additional medical profile
- **Pharmacological**: dose uncertainty (blotter and pill dosing is the dealer's guess), substitution (NBOMe is sold as LSD at higher toxicity), combination with cannabis, stimulants and alcohol at the same party
- **Indian context**: the undersuspected tablet at a metro party; the returning traveller from an ayahuasca retreat abroad; the Goa-visitor emergency; psychiatric-vulnerable seekers drawn by "hearing the drug cures depression" headlines

## 6. What happens in the body/brain: three short stories

**Story one: the doors that loosen, not the machine that seizes.** Classic hallucinogens are partial agonists at the 5-HT2A serotonin receptor, densely placed on deep pyramidal neurons of the cortex's association areas. Stimulating them excites and disinhibits the networks that build our models of the world; the cortex's prediction machinery runs richer and more chaotic than reality warrants: colours intensify, boundaries breathe, music paints, the self's edges dissolve (via the default-mode network's de-synchronisation, the neuroimaging signature of ego-dissolution). The deep clinical irony: this profound subjective storm rides on nearly normal vital signs; modest pupil dilation, slight pulse rise, minimal measurable toxicity. Unlike stimulants (cardiovascular siege) or opioids (respiratory suppression), the hallucinogen-intoxicated body is usually safe; the emergency is in the MIND's experience and occasionally in its content.

**Story two: why no addiction.** Dependence as a syndrome needs the reward circuitry's hijack, dopamine's mesolimbic capture, with tolerance, withdrawal, and craving's escalation. Hallucinogens do not seize that machinery; their subjective "reward" is not reliably hedonic (a trip is hard work), tolerance develops within days of consecutive use (the receptor's own tachyphylaxis; a second consecutive-day trip is famously weak), and there is no withdrawal syndrome to treat. The pattern that emerges is use like visiting a temple: episodic, deliberate, bounded. The dependence framework's near-absence here is not a moral bonus; it is a pharmacological fact that shapes what services the class needs (crisis and aftercare) and what it does not (detox and agonist programmes).

**Story three: the stuck replay (HPPD).** In a minority, some perceptual loosening persists after the trip ends: visual snow, trailing after-images, halos, geometric shimmering, re-intruding fragments of the experience under stress, fatigue or darkness (flashbacks). The leading model: the cortex's prediction machinery, once shown a more chaotic mode, sometimes fails to fully re-stabilise, a kind of perceptual network scar. It is NOT the drug stored in the body, NOT an ongoing intoxication, and NOT a psychotic symptom, three misconceptions that dominate family (and some clinical) understanding and demand explicit correction.

## 7. Symptoms

**Acute hallucinogen intoxication (LSD/psilocybin/DMT/mescaline):**
- Perceptual: intensification of colour and texture, geometric patterning, walls breathing, synaesthesia (music seen), time dilation (minutes as hours), body-image distortion
- Cognitive-affective: wonder, awe, insight-flush, or the opposite, fear-of-dissolving, paranoid ideation, panic storm (the bad trip: overwhelming terror, fear of going mad, of never returning; occasionally self-harm flight or accidental injury in impaired judgement)
- Ego-dissolution spectrum: from pleasant self-loss to terrifying ego-death
- Physical: wide pupils, mild pulse and BP rise, slight tremor, appetite loss, usually unremarkable; hyperthermia and seizures suggest substitution (NBOMe/MDMA-class), not the classic agent
- Duration: LSD 8–12 hours, psilocybin 4–7, DMT minutes (the short-flight distinction), mescaline longer

**Hallucinogen persisting perceptual disorder (HPPD):** recurring visual distortions (flashbacks) or continuous phenomena, visual snow (static), trailing/palinopsia (images clinging), halos, intensified colour, illusions of movement, in a person with intact reality-testing (knows the phenomena are the eye-brain's, not the world's), causing clinically significant distress; typically after the first or an early trip, unpredictable triggers (darkness, stress, cannabis, fatigue).

**Prolonged psychotic reactions:** in vulnerable individuals, a post-trip psychosis may persist for weeks, paranoid, self-referential, often with a mystical-doom colouring; the distinction from schizophrenia's own debut is temporal and often only made in retrospect (the follow-up duty this note flags).

**PCP/ketamine dissociative intoxication (the exam's violent corner):** the dissociative triad, marked analgesia (self-injury without flinching), nystagmus (horizontal, vertical, or rotatory, the bedside pearl), and violent-agitated strength (the PCP patient who fights six people); plus hypertension, sweating, muscle rigidity, ataxia, vacant staring, disorganised speech, hallucinations; cyclical "re-emergence" (apparent recovery then storm again); at high doses coma, seizures, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis with renal failure. Bizarre behaviour during intoxication (nudity, staring into lights, peculiar postures) is classical and street-legendary.

## 8. Diagnosis

**The logic.** The history IS the test in India: most presenting events lack any confirmable toxicology (routine screens in Indian casualty do not test for LSD-class; the blood was never going to answer). The diagnosis runs on: ingestion history from the patient or friends (blotter, mushroom, tablet, brew), the syndrome's shape (perceptual-storm with quiet vitals vs the PCP violent-numb-nystagmic shape), time-course, and the scene (party, festival, retreat, Goa).

**Structured assessment:**
- Known agent and dose if any; co-ingestions (cannabis, alcohol, stimulants, MDMA-class): always ask, always
- Full vitals + temperature (hyperthermia or hypertensive crisis redirects the diagnosis to substitution or stimulant/MDMA territory)
- Mental state: perception phenomena vs delirium (orientation clouded or preserved?), reality-testing intact or lost, suicidal ideation, panic level
- Vulnerability screen: personal/family psychiatric history, especially psychosis and bipolar
- Physical exam: pupils, nystagmus (PCP's flag), injuries (the analgesic patient must be examined head-to-toe), rigidity, reflexes

**Differential table (the practical version):**

| Presentation | The distinguishing clue |
|---|---|
| NBOMe sold as LSD | Seizures, hyperthermia, hypertension, longer-than-expected storm, severe agitation; treat as medical toxicology |
| MDMA/tablet substitution | Hyperthermia, hyponatraemia-from-water-drinking, bruxism, the "hug-drug" affect |
| Temporal-lobe aura | Stereotyped brief episodes, automatisms, EEG where suspected; no pupil signs |
| Migraine aura | Visual phenomena with headache history, minutes-long, no drug history |
| Primary psychotic disorder | Perceptual phenomena plus delusions and functional decline; intoxication history absent or old |
| Delirium (any cause) | Clouded consciousness, fluctuating attention: the hallucinogen patient is awake and orientated to planet, however far from it |
| PCP intoxication | The triad: analgesia + nystagmus + violent strength; cyclical re-emergence |

**HPPD diagnosis:** the persisting-perceptual criteria paraphrased, re-experiencing phenomena after cessation, reality-testing preserved, clinically significant distress, not better explained by psychosis, delirium, or another cause. No test confirms it; the eye must be examined once (to retire retinal causes honestly) and then the diagnosis is clinical.

## 9. Management

**The acute bad trip (LSD/psilocybin-class):**
- **Environment first**: quiet, dim-not-dark room, one calm familiar person, minimal handling; sensory reduction is the antidote to sensory amplification
- **Talking down**: grounded orientation; name, place, "the medicine is wearing off, hours not forever", breathing, familiar objects; the reassurance script is the treatment
- **Benzodiazepines for the panic storm**: oral or IV lorazepam/midazolam; this resolves most crises that reassurance cannot
- **Antipsychotic caution**: in a PURE classic hallucinogen reaction, haloperidol-class drugs can deepen dysphoria and are not first-line; they are reserved for the genuinely psychotic, persistent agitation after benzo adequacy, or when PCP/NBOMe blur the picture, where low-dose cautious use is the pragmatic toxicology practice
- **Restraint avoidance**: physical struggle amplifies terror and injury; chemical calming with benzos plus environment is the safer path
- **Physical safety audit**: drowning, balcony, road-flight during the "escaping the trip" panic; the death in this class is usually accident, not chemistry; hold the doors, hold the ground floor
- **Medical watch if**: hyperthermia, seizures, cardiac symptoms, unknown tablet (the substitution case runs toxicology rules: cooling, cardiac monitoring, fluids)

**PCP/ketamine intoxication:**
- Quiet minimally-stimulating room (stimulation feeds the storm), benzos generous and titrated; antipsychotics cautiously if psychosis persists (haloperidol can lower the seizure threshold and worsen some PCP states; droperidol-era practice has moved on)
- Full injury survey (the analgesic hides fractures and wounds); rhabdomyolysis screen (creatine kinase), renal function, temperature
- Hypertension management when severe; acidifying-era tricks (the historical forced-acid-diuresis) abandoned as harmful
- Coma: supportive, airway, watch for the cyclical re-emergence; do not discharge the patient who "woke up clear" within the first hours

**HPPD management ladder:**
1. **Explanation and reassurance**: the majority's distress is fear of what it might mean ("the drug is still inside", "I am becoming schizophrenic"); the correct diagnosis calmly given is itself the main treatment, plus abstinence from further hallucinogens and cannabis (the common amplifier)
2. **Reduce the amplifiers**: sleep, stress, alcohol and stimulant hygiene
3. **Medication when distress persists**: benzodiazepines short-course (the longest-standing practical option); the antiepileptic tier (lamotrigine the most-discussed, clonazepam-adjacent practice varies), an honest low-evidence zone, best described as case-series medicine; SSRIs where the anxiety-depression rider dominates; reported worsening with some antipsychotics and with cannabis, say so
4. **Cognitive-behavioural frame for the distress**: acceptance-of-the-signal work, attention-management (the visual-snow literature's practical core)

**Prolonged post-hallucinogen psychosis:** antipsychotic treatment along standard lines, with the honest follow-up note that some of these are first-episode schizophrenia introduced by the trip, and need the full first-episode pathway (see Schizophrenia note), not merely detox-and-discharge.

- *India note*: emergency management happens in casualties near festival circuits and metro hospitals; harm-reduction frames (reagent-test-the-tablet, the "one experienced sitter" rule) belong to NGO and party-medicine outreach rather than official programmes; the NDPS Act schedules LSD, psilocybin and mescaline stringently (and the 2019-era notifications tightened research access), hence India's psilocybin-research silence (2026: no approved Indian clinical trial programme; overseas trials are the only access, and the retreat-tourism patient returns to your OPD).

## 10. Indian practice

The realistic Indian consultation: (1) the metro-college student brought from a party, panicking, pupils wide, vitals quiet; the talking-down-plus-benzo casualty case, with the family's terror to treat after the patient sleeps; (2) the returning ayahuasca or mushroom-"retreat" traveller with either HPPD or a mystical-psychotic question, the psychiatric history-taking where "spiritual emergency" frames fight medical frames and the clinician must speak both dialects; (3) the undersuspected substitution case: the "ecstasy" tablet that was NBOMe, arriving as hyperthermia and seizures into the toxicology pathway, where the psychiatric lesson is to ask, always, what the tablet was supposed to be; (4) rare HPPD presentations: the engineering student with visual snow who has consulted three ophthalmologists and one temple before you; the correct diagnosis-giving is the treatment. Costs (approx 2026): a casualty benzo-calming admission is inexpensive; the HPPD workup is mostly the single ophthalmology screen; the therapeutic-research option does not exist domestically (the honest answer to the family asking "should we take him abroad for the depression-mushroom trial" is the standard: those trials exclude, screen, and monitor) they are not a retreat-ticket, and his psychosis-vulnerability may be the exclusion.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: the Goa hour that lasted nine.** A 24-year-old designer from Pune, first-time LSD at a beach party: within two hours, "the sky is coding me", terror of never returning, flight toward the water stopped by friends; brought to the cottage, lights low, one friend instructed by phone ("tell him his name, tell him it ends by morning, stay with him"); oral lorazepam; sleep by hour six; awake and sheepish by noon, with the follow-up conversations: the vulnerability screen (an aunt with a psychotic episode), the substitution check (his friends' blotter batch, one seizure in another user suggested NBOMe-family), and the harm-reduction script delivered without moralising. Teaching points: environment-plus-talking-down IS the medicine; benzos are the adjunct; the batch check is epidemiology at the cottage level; and the family's fears need one calm explanation, not three consultations.

**Case 2: the snow that stayed.** A 21-year-old engineering student in Bengaluru: after a "spiritual journey" with psilocybin-containing mushrooms at a Himalayan retreat, persisting visual snow and light-trails for four months; three ophthalmology clearances; growing panic that he is "becoming mad" and a cascade of internet-diagnoses. The consultation that resolves more than it prescribes: the diagnosis named and explained (a perceptual-network after-effect, not psychosis, not stored drug), the fear decatastrophised with the natural-course data (many fade or soften over months-to-years), the amplifiers retired (his late nights, his cannabis, his screen-auditing compulsion), brief CBT for the checking, and a review at eight weeks; at which the snow persists but the distress has halved, and he has stopped refreshing the forums. Teaching points: in HPPD, the explanation IS the intervention; reassurance is active treatment; medication is the last rung, not the first; and the "spiritual emergency vs medical event" negotiation is won by a clinician fluent in both languages.

## 13. FAQ

- *"Is LSD addictive like heroin?"*: No, it takes no physical hold: no withdrawal, no daily craving, and tolerance builds so fast that consecutive-day use simply stops working. The harms of this class are different: the panic storm, the flashbacks, and unmasking illness in vulnerable minds.
- *"He is still having the drug inside him after three months, that is why he sees the trails, no?"*: No: the drug is long gone; what remains is a changed perceptual setting in the visual networks; like a room that keeps ringing faintly after the bell stopped. It fades or softens for most, and it is not madness.
- *"Will one trip make him schizophrenic?"*: One trip does not CREATE schizophrenia in a healthy brain, but in a brain already carrying vulnerability, it can bring the illness forward or light it; that is why the family history matters before anyone's first dose, and why the post-trip psychosis gets proper follow-up, not just sleep.
- *"What do we do in the moment, he is terrified and we are far from any hospital?"*: Dark-quiet room, one calm familiar person, his name and the truth ("this ends by morning"), breathing, water, no struggle and no mockery; a benzodiazepine if you can reach a doctor by phone; and physically hold the doors, stairs and water; the danger is the flight, not the drug.
- *"Are these mushrooms used as medicine for depression? I read the trials."*: The trials are real, overseas, screened and monitored, in research settings; the retreat ticket and the dealer's blotter are not the trial; and a mind with psychosis in the family is usually exactly whom the trials exclude.
- *"The temple has used such plants for generations, how is it dangerous?"*: Traditional ceremonial contexts screen participants, dose carefully and hold the container together, a structure, not a chemistry pass; the danger is the unscreened mind and the unstructured dose, which is what the tourist circuit sells.
- *"He fought six policemen and felt no pain, is that LSD?"*: Almost never, that shape belongs to the dissociative family (PCP, street ketamine): the numb-violent-nystagmic patient gets the full injury survey, the quiet room and the benzos, and you do not discharge him when he first "clears", because the storm returns in waves.
- *"Can the flashbacks be removed with medicine?"*: Mostly they are managed, not excised: the diagnosis-explanation halves the suffering, the amplifiers (sleep, stress, cannabis) are retired, and medicines are the last, low-evidence rung, used for the distress, sparingly.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **High-yield anchors**: 5-HT2A agonism; ego-dissolution via default-mode de-synchronisation; no-withdrawal-no-hijack (the non-addiction lecture point); tolerance in DAYS (second-day trip famously weak); HPPD with intact insight; PCP triad (analgesia, nystagmus, violent strength); PCP's cyclical re-emergence; NBOMe masquerade
- **Classic trap questions**: "LSD causes physical dependence" (false); "flashbacks are psychotic episodes" (false, insight intact); "haloperidol is the drug of choice in bad trip" (false, benzos and talking down; antipsychotics cautious/reserved); "PCP intoxication shows pinpoint pupils" (false; nystagmus is the sign; miotic pupils belong to opioids); "hyperthermia is expected in classic LSD" (false, substitution/NBOMe/MDMA)
- **One-liners to bank**: DMT = minutes-long trip; ayahuasca = DMT + MAOI brew (the stomach's chemistry); mescaline = peyote cactus, the classic phenethylamine; visual snow and palinopsia = HPPD's signatures; serotonin-syndrome confusion: hallucinogens alone rarely cause it, MAOI-brew combinations can
- **Indian exam corner**: "A young man brought from a rave, panicking, wide pupils, vitals normal, management" (the environment-talking-down-benzo answer with restraint-avoidance and substitution watch); "visual disturbances for months after one drug exposure with intact insight" (HPPD); "violent analgesic nystagmus" (PCP-class, the injury survey).

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. The chief reason hallucinogens escape the addictive syndrome:**
A. Their doses are too small
B. No reward-circuit capture, no reliable hedonic drive, no withdrawal, and receptor tachyphylaxis within days enforces episodic use
C. They are too expensive
D. Rapid physical dependence prevents reuse
**Answer: B.** The pharmacology decides the service tier: crisis-and-aftercare, not detox-and-agonist.

**2. A party-goer panicking on "LSD" arrives with hyperthermia and a seizure. The clinical redirection:**
A. Expected classic LSD effect: observe only
B. Suspect substitution (NBOMe or MDMA-class) and run toxicology-grade management: cooling, cardiac monitoring, fluids
C. Give haloperidol immediately
D. Physically restrain to control the seizure
**Answer: B.** The quiet-vitals rule of classic agents; hyperthermia and seizures are the substitution signature.

**3. First-line management of a pure classic bad trip:**
A. Haloperidol IM
B. Physical restraint for safety
C. Low-stimulation environment, talking down by a familiar person, benzodiazepines if the panic storm persists
D. Gastric lavage
**Answer: C.** Sensory reduction, orientation and truthfulness are the medicine; restraint amplifies the danger.

**4. Visual snow and light-trails persisting four months after psilocybin, insight intact, distress present. Diagnosis and first intervention:**
A. Schizophrenia: start an antipsychotic
B. HPPD: give the diagnosis and its explanation (the treatment itself), retire the amplifiers, review; medication last
C. Migraine: start a triptan
D. Simple phobia
**Answer: B.** Naming it, decatastrophising it and removing the amplifiers is the majority of the treatment; the medicine runs out long before the care does.

**5. The PCP-intoxicated patient who has "cleared" and jokes with the staff one hour after a violent storm:**
A. Discharge home immediately
B. Observe longer: PCP intoxication is cyclically re-emergent; the storm returns in waves
C. Give disulfiram
D. Start methadone
**Answer: B.** The re-emergence cycle is the classical trap of the dissociative emergency.

**6. A young man with a psychotic aunt asks whether one LSD trip can "give him schizophrenia". The honest answer:**
A. Yes in all cases
B. No risk to anyone
C. The trip does not create the illness in a healthy brain, but in a vulnerable one it can precipitate or advance it; the family history is the question to answer before the first dose
D. Only if cannabis is added
**Answer: C.** The precipitation-and-vulnerability frame: the exact risk conversation the classical twin-street of first-episode clinics is built on.
