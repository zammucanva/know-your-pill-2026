# Dementia with Lewy bodies

## 1. In one line + overview

**Tagline:** Dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) is a dementia where a protein called alpha-synuclein clumps inside brain cells, producing a four-part signature (**fluctuating alertness, vivid visual hallucinations, Parkinson-like slowness, and dream-acting sleep**) plus a sometimes **life-threatening sensitivity to older antipsychotic drugs**, which is the single most important thing a clinician must remember from this note.

**Overview, in plain words:** If Alzheimer's forgets, DLB *drifts and sees*. A man dozes off in the middle of a conversation and minutes later is fully sharp; he describes two small children playing in the kitchen at dusk, detailed and unbothered; his handwriting has grown small and his shuffle has appeared; at night he punches the air, swearing at dream robbers, and has fallen out of bed twice. These are not four separate illnesses; they are one disease, caused by **Lewy bodies** (protein deposits) scattered through the thinking brain and the movement centres. DLB is the **third commonest dementia**, accounting for roughly 4–8% of clinic and post-mortem cases, and very likely **underdiagnosed**, because doctors meet the hallucinations first and stop thinking. It sits midway between Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease: dementia appears **before** or **within one year of** the movement problems (that timing is the diagnostic hinge). Two messages for every family: the visual hallucinations are the disease, not insanity, and (before any operation, emergency or otherwise) every doctor must be told: **"no haloperidol, no risperidone, no old antipsychotics; he can have a catastrophic reaction."** A medical-alert card with that line saves lives.

## 2. Learning objectives

After working through this note, you should be able to:

1. Name the **four core clinical features** of DLB and the supporting features.
2. Apply the **one-year rule** that separates DLB from Parkinson's disease dementia.
3. Explain alpha-synuclein and why DLB, Parkinson's and multiple system atrophy form one family.
4. Distinguish DLB visual hallucinations from delirium, from late-onset psychotic disorder, and from Alzheimer's hallucinations (which come late).
5. Recognise REM sleep behaviour disorder as a **prodrome** that can precede dementia by years.
6. Manage it: acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (the strongest responders of any dementia), cautious movement treatment, sleep treatment, and the antipsychotic rules.
7. Counsel the family on falls, faints, swallowing, driving and the sensitivity card.
8. Explain why this diagnosis is so often missed in Indian practice, and how to catch it.

## 4. Epidemiology

### Global

- DLB accounts for roughly **4–8% of dementia** in clinic series, and up to 10–30% in autopsy series, the discrepancy itself telling us we miss it in life.
- Onset typically **50–90**, with most cases starting after 60. Men are affected somewhat more than women, the reverse of Alzheimer's.
- It is the dementia with the **highest psychiatric presentation rate**: hallucinations, delusions, depression and REM sleep behaviour disorder bring many patients to psychiatrists first, then neurologists, a shared patient by nature.
- Course is progressive; average survival post-diagnosis is broadly similar to Alzheimer's, but hospital admissions are more frequent, driven by falls, faints, aspiration and adverse drug reactions.

### India

- No national figures; memory-clinic and neuropsychiatry series from Indian centres repeatedly report **late recognition**, most often after the patient has already received a typical antipsychotic; sometimes with the catastrophic reaction being the *event that led to the diagnosis*.
- The high prevalence of Parkinson's disease in Indian clinics guarantees a parallel, under-detected DLB population; every Parkinson's OPD will contain some patients whose dementia actually preceded or matched their motor symptoms.
- Sleep clinics are scarce, so REM sleep behaviour disorder (the easiest early clue, elicitable by three questions to the spouse) is almost never asked about. Teaching those three questions to PGs would transform Indian DLB detection: "Does he act out dreams? Does he talk or shout in sleep? Has he fallen out of bed?"

## 5. Causes & risk factors

- **Biological:** age (the main one); male sex; the disease process itself: alpha-synuclein misfolding and clumping inside neurons, with **more cortical spread** than in Parkinson's disease, which is why thinking fails early.
- **Genetic:** mostly sporadic; a small familial fraction exists (variants in the alpha-synuclein gene and GBA pathway genes raise risk; a family history of Parkinson's modestly raises risk of DLB), but routine genetic testing is not part of care.
- **Shared family with Parkinson's:** think of one disease process with two presenting doors. Same protein; door one, the movement centres first (Parkinson's, dementia later: PDD); door two, the cortex first (DLB, movement problems within a year at most). The **one-year rule** is the convention separating the two labels.
- **Environmental:** head injury and pesticide-type exposures are suggested but unproven risk factors, extrapolated from Parkinson's literature.
- **Indian context:** the practical "risk factor" for a bad outcome is not biological; it is **the unlabelled patient receiving haloperidol or risperidone in a casualty or small nursing home** for "agitation" or "hallucinations". Whatever raises that risk (missing the diagnosis, missing the card, missing the history) is the risk worth fighting.

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: the protein that lights up the wrong rooms.** Alpha-synuclein is a normal protein at the tips of neurons, involved in how nerve endings release their chemical messages. In DLB it misfolds and clumps into **Lewy bodies**, spreading through connected networks. Where it lands decides the symptom: in the **cortex** (attention and perception hubs) it produces the fluctuations and hallucinations; in the **substantia nigra** it produces parkinsonism; in the **brainstem sleep centres** it unbolts the paralysis that normally locks our bodies during dreams; in the **autonomic nuclei** it unsteadies blood pressure, gut and bladder.

**Story 2: the dream paralysis that fails.** Healthy sleepers are internally *locked* during dreaming sleep; the brain paralyses the body so dreams stay inside the head. Lewy body disease corrodes that lock years before any dementia. The sleeper begins to **live out the dream**: muttering, shouting, punching the pillow-mate, leaping out of bed to "escape a thief". This is REM sleep behaviour disorder, and remarkably, it can precede DLB or Parkinson's by **5–15 years**, making it one of the most valuable early-warning signs in all of neurodegeneration. In sleep-lab language the muscle-atonia is lost; the spouse can tell you the same thing for free.

**Story 3: the empty acetylcholine tank.** DLB produces the **deepest cholinergic loss of any common dementia**, deeper than Alzheimer's. Two consequences follow. Clinically: attention, perception and arousal wobble alarmingly. Therapeutically: **acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (especially rivastigmine) produce their most visible responses here**; hallucinations soften, attention steadies, some patients look "switched back on". That also means anticholinergic drugs (bladder tablets, old anti-spasmodics, sedating antihistamines) hit DLB patients doubly hard; they can *create* the hallucinations they were meant to sedate away.

## 7. Symptoms

**The four core features (know these cold):**

1. **Fluctuating cognition with pronounced attention swings:** lucid in the morning, dazed by afternoon, almost delirious-looking by evening, and back again. Families say "good days and bad hours." Drowsiness, staring spells and disorganised speech come in waves.
2. **Recurrent, well-formed visual hallucinations:** typically people or animals, small children in the kitchen, a stranger sitting quietly in the corner, cattle crossing the room. Early on, insight is often preserved and the tone is calm or curious ("there are two boys again, they don't bother me"). Later they can turn threatening; distress arrives with the delusions about them.
3. **Spontaneous parkinsonism:** slowness, small handwriting, reduced arm swing, stooped shuffling gait, softer voice. A **prominent early tremor is less typical**, prominent tremor points back toward Parkinson's disease.
4. **REM sleep behaviour disorder:** dream-enactment, shouting, thrashing, falling out of bed, sometimes injuring the bed partner. Often the *first* symptom, years ahead of the rest.

**Supportive features:**

- **Severe sensitivity to antipsychotics**: treated below as a headline because it is also a management rule.
- **Repeated falls and faints:** autonomic blood-pressure drops on standing (postural hypotension), plus impaired balance.
- **Transient, unexplained losses of consciousness**: brief "blackouts" that get mislabelled as seizures.
- **Misidentification delusions** (Capgras-type: "this woman is not my wife, she is an impostor"), systematised, fixed, and much more common than in Alzheimer's.
- **Depression, anxiety, apathy;** severe anticholinergic sensitivity (bladder drugs making them psychotic).
- **Visuospatial failure out of proportion:** getting lost in familiar rooms, difficulty judging steps, drawing tasks collapse early, often before memory formally fails.
- **Memory storage** is relatively better preserved early than in Alzheimer's (retrieval is slow, but the storage cupboard exists), which is why patients can pass casual "memory tests" while clearly unable to manage themselves.

**Course:** progressive over years, typically 6–12 from diagnosis, with fluctuations always present. Any superimposed illness (infection, dehydration) can swing them into a full delirium, so sudden deterioration still deserves the delirium workup, not just "DLB progressing".

## 8. Diagnosis

### The logic of the criteria (paraphrased)

The consensus criteria (updated 2017; ICD-11 and DSM-5 are built on the same ideas) reason like this: there is dementia (a decline from baseline in thinking bad enough to affect independence) plus **core features**. **Probable DLB** when two or more core features are present (or one core feature plus a biomarker, e.g. reduced dopamine transporter uptake on DAT imaging or abnormal sleep study confirming dream-enactment); **possible DLB** with one core feature alone. The timing rule: the dementia must come **before or concurrently with parkinsonism**; dementia arising more than a year after established Parkinson's is labelled **Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD)** instead. Supporting features (falls, faints, delusions, autonomic failure, sensitivity history) raise the index of suspicion but are not counted as core.

### Workup

- **Collateral history across four doors:** fluctuations timeline, exactly what the hallucinations look like, sleep behaviour questions to the spouse, and any past reaction to antipsychotics (dig into old prescriptions; a previous "collapse after haloperidol" entry is a diagnostic gift).
- **Cognitive testing:** clock-drawing and figure-copying tasks collapse early (visuospatial), attention tests swing, memory relatively held; the *pattern* matters more than the total score.
- **Imaging:** MRI mostly to exclude other causes; the DLB clue is *less* medial temporal atrophy than expected for the dementia's severity (a relative-sparing sign). **DAT scan (dopamine transporter imaging)** shows reduced uptake in the basal ganglia, a genuine discriminator from Alzheimer's when available (limited availability and cost in India; largely tertiary/private).
- **Polysomnography (sleep study)** to confirm REM without atonia, where available; in practice the spousal history of dream-acting is accepted clinically.
- **Bloods** as in any dementia (see the Alzheimer's note for the one-time list; one fact, one home).
- **Autonomic bedside tests:** lying and standing blood pressure; a drop of 20/10 or more supports autonomic failure (and explains the falls).

### Differentials: the four traps

| Mimic | Clue that it is actually DLB | Clue that it is the mimic |
|---------|------------------------------|---------------------------|
| Delirium (DLB looks like it!) | Weeks-months pattern, parkinsonism + dream-acting history, known sensitivity | Hours-days trigger, infection/drug cause, resolves with treatment |
| Late-onset psychotic disorder | Well-formed visual hallucinations + cognitive fluctuation + motor signs + sleep history | Pure auditory/ideas, stable attention, no parkinsonism, normal cognition |
| Alzheimer's | Hallucinations *early*, fluctuations marked, parkinsonism, falls early | Hallucinations *late and rare*, smooth decline, memory-first |
| Parkinson's disease dementia | Dementia present before/within 1 year of motor signs | Motor disease for years first; tremor-predominant disease |
| Vascular dementia | No stepwise infarct history; imaging clean of strategic infarcts | Stepwise course, infarct burden, executive-first profile |

**The one habit to install:** *any elderly patient with detailed visual hallucinations gets asked about sleep-acting, gets examined for parkinsonism, and gets the drug-sensitivity history, before a single prescription is written.*

## 9. Management

### (a) The non-negotiable safety rule

- **Avoid dopamine-blocking antipsychotics.** Haloperidol and risperidone (and other high-potency agents) can trigger severe rigidity, hyperthermia, confusion and a malignant-syndrome-like collapse, the classic DLB catastrophe. Up to half of DLB patients show some degree of this sensitivity. The same caution extends, more mildly, to all antipsychotics including atypicals; DLB is the disease where "give something for the hallucinations" is the most dangerous reflex in medicine.
- Give the family a **medical-alert card / note** in local language: "Diagnosed Lewy body dementia, severe sensitivity to antipsychotic drugs. For agitation: contact treating doctor. Avoid haloperidol/risperidone." The wallet card is as important as any prescription.

### (b) Cognition and hallucinations: the chemical that works

- **Rivastigmine** (oral, or patch where affordable) has the best evidence of any dementia drug for DLB: hallucinations, anxiety, attention and daily function measurably improve in a good fraction. Donepezil and galantamine are reasonable alternatives with similar logic.
- **Memantine**: modest benefit; useful add-on, well tolerated.
- If hallucinations are non-frightening and insight preserved: **do not treat them at all.** Reorient gently, keep evening lighting even (shadows worsen them), treat hearing and vision. When distress or dangerous behaviour demands a drug: **quetiapine** in tiny doses (12.5–25 mg, cautiously titrated) is the pragmatic default, weak evidence but the best risk-benefit available in India; **clozapine** has the best efficacy evidence, but needs blood monitoring (agranulocytosis) that makes it tertiary-centre-only; watch its sedation and orthostasis, which DLB tolerates poorly.
- **Strip every anticholinergic** from the chart: bladder drugs, sedating antihistamines, tricyclics; each can amplify both psychosis and confusion.

*India note: rivastigmine capsules are inexpensive as generics (roughly ₹200–450/month at maintenance, approx 2026; the patch is far costlier); quetiapine 25 mg tablets cost a few rupees each. The expensive thing is again not the drug; it is the unmade diagnosis.*

### (c) Movement symptoms: gentle ambition

- **Levodopa** can be tried for parkinsonism, started low and slow, but the response is often disappointing in DLB, and it can worsen hallucinations. Aim to improve walking safely, not to normalise the exam.
- Physiotherapy for gait and balance; home falls-audit (rails, night lighting, floor mattress); review footwear.

### (d) Sleep, faints and gut

- **REM sleep behaviour disorder:** protect the bed partner and the sleeper; separate beds or a padded barrier, remove sharp furniture, mattress on the floor. **Melatonin** (3–12 mg at bedtime) is the best-tolerated medical option; **clonazepam** (0.25–0.5 mg) is effective but watch sedation, falls and breathing (avoid in snorers/possible apnoea).
- **Postural faints:** stand-up training, compression stockings, salt review, medicines review (cut anything that drops pressure; including antihypertensives that were prescribed when autonomic failure changed the rules), teach the "sit before you stand" drill.
- **Constipation** (autonomic, plus anticholinergic drugs): fibre, fluids, routine aperients; impacted bowel is a top hidden cause of sudden agitation.
- Swallowing review when speech and drooling change; aspiration pneumonia is a leading cause of death; hand-feeding techniques and posture help (see the Alzheimer's note's late-stage discussion).

### (e) Family education: the centrepiece

Explain the four features as one illness, especially that hallucinations are a brain-symptom not a mind-symptom; give the sensitivity card; give the fluctuation explanation ("you will have golden mornings and lost evenings; that is the illness, not a new stroke or new madness"); schedule driving/finance conversations early; connect to dementia caregiver support where it exists.

## 10. Indian practice

- **Where patients surface:** psychiatry OPDs with "visual hallucinations" or "acting mad in the evening"; neurology with a Parkinson's-plus picture; general medicine after a collapse from an antipsychotic given in casualty; and sleep-clinic referrals are rare to nonexistent outside metros.
- **Guidelines actually used:** international consensus criteria (2017 DLB consortium) and general dementia/NICE-style frameworks; Indian Psychiatric Society dementia guidance generalises. No India-specific DLB pathway exists, which makes the wallet card and family education even more central.
- **DAT scan reality:** very limited availability and cost; the practical diagnosis is clinical (four features + collateral history + the relative-sparing MRI pattern). Do not make a family chase a scan to believe you.
- **Private practice reality:** the most common request is "give him something so he does not see things"; the correct answer is often rivastigmine (treating the cause of the hallucination chemistry), not a sedative. Explaining that paradox in one minute is the single most useful consultation skill in Indian DLB care.
- **Caregiving:** the spouse with bruises from dream-acting, the daughter managing falls and faints, the distress of misidentification ("who is this woman?") which in India is often interpreted spiritually or as rejection. Naming these as symptom, not sentiment, relieves families greatly.
- **Primary care role:** the three sleep questions, standing/lying BP, the medicine-chart anticholinergic audit, and being the doctor who *knows not to reach for haloperidol* in a confused elderly patient; that alone prevents the worst outcome.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: the boys in the kitchen.** A 71-year-old retired railway clerk was brought by his wife: for a year he had calmly described "two small boys" who played in the kitchen each evening; six months of slowing, smaller handwriting and two falls; and, her phrase, "he fights in his sleep, he once elbowed me hard at 3 a.m." His memory was imperfect but usable; clock-drawing was poor. A previous casual clinic had prescribed low-dose risperidone for the "visions"; within days he had become rigid, mute and feverish, and recovered slowly in hospital. The triad (early visual hallucinations + parkinsonism + dream-acting) plus the reaction history gave probable DLB. Rivastigmine was titrated; hallucinations reduced to brief, non-distressing appearances; melatonin and bed-padding managed the nights; the family carried the sensitivity card. Two years later he remained at home, walking with a stick, "the boys still visit, but they are quiet."

*Teaching points: early detailed visual hallucinations are organic until proven otherwise in the elderly; the risperidone reaction was the loudest diagnostic clue; rivastigmine treats the hallucination chemistry; the card is part of the prescription.*

**Case 2: the night caller misdiagnosed as schizophrenia.** A 68-year-old widow was referred to psychiatry with a one-year history of "hearing and seeing intruders at night", fixed belief that her daughter-in-law had been replaced by a look-alike, and twice-daily episodes of drowsiness and rambling. The referrer had started an antipsychotic; her gait had worsened alarmingly on it. Collateral history from the son revealed the classic pattern: lucid mornings, evening drift, detailed intruders, dream-shouting for two years before all this, and repeated faints on standing. Examination: mild bilateral bradykinesia without tremor, postural BP drop of 25 mmHg. The antipsychotic was stopped; quetiapine was not needed at all once rivastigmine was established; the Capgras belief softened; salt and stocking advice reduced the faints.

*Teaching points: late-onset "psychosis" with cognitive fluctuation is DLB until excluded; the impostor belief is a DLB signature (misidentification delusion); stopping the offending antipsychotic was itself the treatment; autonomic symptoms (faints, constipation) are part of the disease, and managing them prevents the falls that end the story.*

## 13. FAQ

**"He sees people who are not there. Is he going mad?"**
No. This is a specific brain disease; the perception centres themselves are misfiring, producing pictures as real to him as you are. Most patients are calm about them early. It is a symptom, like a limp.

**"The doctor started a memory tablet for the visions, is that a mistake?"**
No: that is precisely the logic. The chemical that is most depleted in this disease is the one those tablets preserve. Steadier chemistry means fewer visions. Sedatives are the riskier path here.

**"Why must everyone know he cannot have haloperidol?"**
Because in this disease, the older antipsychotics can switch the body into a severe rigid, feverish, collapse-like state. It is not an allergy in the usual sense, it is the disease's wiring, but it behaves like one. A card in the wallet works like an allergy bracelet.

**"She calls me an impostor. Does she no longer love me?"**
The bond is not gone; the recognition wiring is. This misidentification is one of the most typical symptoms of this disease. Stay calm, use your name, keep routines; the belief fluctuates with the rest of the illness.

**"He falls for no reason. What can we do?"**
Three things together: review every medicine that drops blood pressure, teach slow sitting-to-standing, and make the home fall-proof (rails, lighting, floor mattress). Falls, not the hallucinations, are what send this disease to hospital.

**"He thrashes at night and I am frightened of being hurt."**
Tell the doctor the exact words "he acts out his dreams"; that is a core symptom. Practical shields: separate mattresses, padding, removing hard furniture, and medicine options (melatonin is the gentlest; clonazepam helps but watch falls and breathing).

**"Some days he is almost his old self. Are we mistaken about the diagnosis?"**
No: fluctuation is the disease's fingerprint. Use the good hours deliberately: conversations, walks, small enjoyments. And if a bad week is *worse than usual*, still ask the doctor to check for infection or dehydration, this illness hides delirium inside its own noise.

**"Is it Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, or both?"**
It is its own disease, a cousin of both. Same protein family as Parkinson's, but arriving through the thinking brain first. That is why movement problems, sleep-acting and faints travel with the memory and perception problems.

**"Should I get the special scan to be sure?"**
The clinical picture (your descriptions of the sleep, the visions, the fluctuations, and any past drug reaction) usually carries the diagnosis. Scans help in unusual cases, but chasing expensive imaging rarely changes the care plan; the care plan is medicine review, blood-pressure management, safety and one prescription.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **The four core features as a checklist to recite:** fluctuation / visual hallucinations / parkinsonism / REM behaviour disorder. Suggest the hook: **"Flips, films, freeze, fights-in-sleep."**
- **The one-year rule:** dementia before or within 1 year of parkinsonism = DLB; dementia after 1+ years of Parkinson's = PDD. Examiners love it precisely because it is arbitrary-but-operational.
- **Highest-yield single fact in the whole note:** severe neuroleptic sensitivity. If an examiner asks "which dementia carries the antipsychotic warning", the answer begins and ends here.
- **Classic traps:** (1) diagnosing delirium repeatedly for fluctuating confusion in an outpatient (no trigger, no resolution; look at the rest of the four); (2) diagnosing late-onset schizophrenia in a 70-year-old with detailed visual hallucinations (age + visual modality + any parkinsonism = organic); (3) missing the spousal sleep history; (4) prescribing a bladder antispasmodic and creating florid psychosis; (5) blaming a levodopa trial's failure on "non-compliance".
- **Viva favourites:** alpha-synuclein family tree (DLB–PDD–MSA); why cholinesterase inhibitors work best here (deepest cholinergic deficit); DAT scan logic (preserved in Alzheimer's, reduced in DLB); Capgras and misidentification; relative sparing of medial temporal atrophy on MRI; melatonin vs clonazepam for RBD; why the hallucinations often need no drug at all.
- **Indian exam corner:** the casualty-haloperidol scenario as an ethics-and-safety short note; "elderly visual hallucinations: differential diagnosis" long answer (DLB, delirium, Alzheimer's late stage, Charles Bonnet syndrome from vision loss, occipital stroke, late-onset psychosis); the three-sleep-questions screening argument; caregiver injury from RBD as a neglected problem.
- **Rehearse one line:** "In an elderly patient with visual hallucinations, ask about sleep-fighting and look at the gait before you write any prescription."

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. A patient develops dementia, and two years later a shuffling gait. Correct diagnostic label by the one-year rule:**
A. Dementia with Lewy bodies
B. Parkinson's disease dementia
C. Alzheimer's with extrapyramidal side effects
D. Vascular dementia

**Answer: B.** Dementia arising well after (more than a year into) established parkinsonism is PDD; DLB requires dementia before or within a year of motor signs.

**2. The most dangerous reflex in suspected DLB is:**
A. Starting rivastigmine
B. Giving haloperidol for hallucinations
C. Prescribing physiotherapy
D. Giving melatonin at bedtime

**Answer: B.** Dopamine blockers can trigger severe, even life-threatening rigidity and collapse; rivastigmine is the evidence-based path, and haloperidol is the iatrogenic catastrophe.

**3. Hallucinations in DLB, compared with Alzheimer's disease, are:**
A. Rarer and mainly auditory
B. More common, well-formed, visual, and earlier
C. Only nocturnal
D. Always frightening

**Answer: B.** Recurrent, detailed visual hallucinations early in the course are the DLB signature; in Alzheimer's they are late and uncommon.

**4. Which feature can precede DLB by many years?**
A. Memory loss
B. REM sleep behaviour disorder
C. Capgras delusion
D. Urinary incontinence

**Answer: B.** Dream-enactment is one of the earliest manifestations of the synuclein process, often years before cognitive decline.

**5. The dementia drug with its strongest evidence in DLB is:**
A. Memantine only
B. Rivastigmine (a cholinesterase inhibitor)
C. Amitriptyline
D. Haloperidol in low dose

**Answer: B.** DLB has the deepest cholinergic deficit of the common dementias, and rivastigmine's trials showed improvement in hallucinations, attention and function.

**6. A DLB patient's spouse has bruises from the nights. First-line best-tolerated medication:**
A. Clonazepam 2 mg
B. Melatonin at bedtime
C. Quetiapine 100 mg
D. Zolpidem

**Answer: B.** Melatonin has the gentlest risk profile for dream-enacting sleep; clonazepam helps but risks falls, sedation and breathing problems; quetiapine treats agitation, not the sleep behaviour itself.
