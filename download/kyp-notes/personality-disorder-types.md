# Specific Personality Disorder Types — Cluster A, B, C and the Borderlines Between Them

## 1. In one line + overview

**Ten recognisable styles of being, the suspicious, the solitary, the odd; the dramatic, the unstable, the grandiose; the fearful, the clinging, the perfectionist, each a lifelong pattern with its own texture, complications, and treatment approach.**

This note walks through the specific personality disorder types cluster by cluster, the way the Oxford chapter does, because the clusters share atmosphere as well as label: Cluster A feels odd, Cluster B feels overwhelming, Cluster C feels anxious. For each type you get the clinical picture, the differential that matters, the course over time, and the treatment gestures, plus the neurobiological research (ch 4.12.5) that increasingly roots these styles in genes and brain systems: dopamine and novelty-seeking, serotonin and anxiety, oxytocin and affiliation, MAO-A and aggression. The chapter's most practically useful gifts are its **discrimination pairs** (borderline vs bipolar, avoidant vs schizoid, narcissistic vs borderline, obsessive personality vs OCD) which decide real prescriptions and real referrals.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Describe each of the ten DSM-IV/ICD-10 personality disorder types in plain clinical language.
- Give for each type: prevalence pattern, one aetiological lead, course, and treatment gesture.
- Work the classic discrimination pairs: borderline vs bipolar II, avoidant vs schizoid vs social phobia, dependent vs borderline abandonment reactions, narcissistic vs histrionic attention-seeking, obsessive-compulsive personality vs OCD, antisocial vs borderline antisocial behaviour.
- Recognise the borderline-specific safety number (suicide risk) and the assessment implications.
- Summarise the gene-trait findings: D4DR and novelty-seeking, 5-HTT short variant and neuroticism, MAO-A × childhood abuse and aggression, V1a/oxytocin and affiliation, with their honest effect sizes.
- Explain "borderline personality organization" (Kernberg) and how it differs from the DSM borderline category.
- Identify which types improve with age and which decompensate in middle life.

## 4. Epidemiology (per type: one-line anchors)

- **Paranoid**: ~1–2% of the community; more often diagnosed in men; rarely presents voluntarily.
- **Schizoid**: ~0.8–1%; the least common clinical presentation of Cluster A (rarely diagnosed in psychiatric settings).
- **Schizotypal**: ~0.5–3% community; 14.6% in relatives of schizophrenia probands (versus ~2% controls); no clear sex difference.
- **Antisocial (dissocial)**: ~3% in men-driven community figures, more precisely ~1.5% median; male:female ratios 2:1 to 7:1; urban, younger, lower socio-economic; 47% of prisoners.
- **Borderline**: 1.5–5% community; 10–15% of outpatients; commoner in women; onset typically 18–35 years.
- **Histrionic**: 2–3% community, 10–15% clinical; more often diagnosed in women (possibly cultural bias in expressiveness).
- **Narcissistic**: ~0.5% community, 1–3% clinical; commoner in males; not a formal ICD-10 category (sits under "other specific PDs").
- **Avoidant (anxious)**: <1% community, up to 10% clinical; equal sexes.
- **Dependent**: ~0.7–0.9% community; structured interviews show no real sex difference (passivity is culture-relative).
- **Obsessive-compulsive (anankastic)**: ~1% community, up to 10% of psychiatric patients (especially with depressive/anxiety disorders); the most prevalent PD in the community survey medians; commoner in men.

*India lens: all figures are Western survey data; Indian settings add strong cultural caution; dependency, obedience and orderliness can be normative, so a disorder is diagnosed only where traits are markedly beyond the culture's average.*

## 5. Causes & risk factors: the biology of the types (ch 4.12.5)

**Genetics with honest effect sizes.** The D4 dopamine receptor gene (long repeat allele) was reported in 1996 to explain about 10% of the genetic variance in novelty-seeking, the first gene-personality link, but replications were inconsistent; meta-analyses now suggest only subtle connections. The serotonin transporter (5-HTT) short variant contributes 3–4% of total variance (7–9% of genetic variance) in neuroticism/harm-avoidance; extrapolated, roughly 10–15 genes of similar weight might underlie neuroticism's heritability. The largest honest lesson: personality is polygenic with each gene contributing modestly, which is why no "personality test" based on genes exists.

**Gene × environment: the aggression finding.** In a Dutch family, a mutation abolishing MAO-A function produced males with severe aggression across generations; MAO-A knockout mice are aggressive; and, the pivotal human finding, a common low-activity MAO-A promoter variant interacts with childhood abuse: only abused children carrying the low-activity variant showed elevated later antisocial and violent behaviour. Aggressive temperaments also involve serotonin (5-HT1B knockout mice attack faster) and vasopressin-serotonin ratios.

**Affiliation biology.** Oxytocin, opioids, prolactin and vasopressin systems mediate attachment across mammalian bonds; the prairie-vole vasopressin V1a receptor gene story (transferring monogamous bonding patterns between species) shows how strongly single social-behaviour circuits can be genetically tuned. Socially deprived monkeys differ from mother-reared in nearly every measure of adult social function; deprivation in the first 6–9 months of life persists into adulthood. Poor or distorted affiliative behaviour is the single most predictive marker of personality disorder diagnosis.

**Stress physiology.** Borderline patients with impulsive suicidal behaviour show high basal cortisol with blunted response to challenge; a stress system that has lost adaptive range; amygdala sensitisation plus insufficient cingulate/prefrontal regulation is the current synthesis of BPD affective instability.

## 6. What happens in the mind: three short stories

**Splitting (the borderline lens).** The infant's world arrives unblended: wholly good breast, wholly bad breast. Maturity blends them into "frustrating but lovable person". In borderline organisation the blending never stabilises, so the beloved therapist of Tuesday is the persecutor of Friday, and the patient's own self-image splits with it. Splitting plus identity diffusion plus *intact reality testing* is Kernberg's borderline personality organisation; a level of psychological architecture that can underlie several personality disorders, not just the DSM borderline category. Idea: the problem is not losing touch with reality; it is organising people and self in all-or-nothing files.

**The control story (anankastic).** The anankastic person lacks an internal sense of security and tries to manufacture it by making the external world totally predictable: rules, lists, punctuality, hierarchy, hoarded objects against catastrophe. Novelty and spontaneity feel like threats; emotion is distrusted (especially anger); the strategy works well enough to run a career, until the unpredictable happens (illness, promotion, a free weekend) and the machinery jams into doubt, indecision, and depression. The disorder's engine is fear of one's own inner world, managed by controlling the outer one.

**The reward/brakes story (Cluster B's common engine).** Approach drives run hot, novelty-seeking (dopaminergic), attention and admiration (narcissistic and histrionic currencies), intense attachment (borderline), while the serotonergic brakes on impulse are weak (low CSF 5-HIAA in impulsive-aggressive patients). The result is the Cluster B signature: big feelings, quick actions, aftermath. Each type aims the same engine differently: at risk and stimulation (antisocial), at intimate others (borderline), at audiences (histrionic), at self-esteem maintenance (narcissistic).

## 7. Symptoms: the types one by one

### Cluster A

**Paranoid personality disorder.** Pervasive suspiciousness and mistrust: reads hidden demeaning meanings into neutral remarks; doubts the loyalty of friends; bears grudges persistently; reads innocent remarks as attacks on character and reacts with counterattack; pathologically jealous; reluctant to confide ("information will be used against me"). Appears rational, cold, unemotional, but with quick, rigid anger when threats are perceived; the "fanatic" subtype of Schneider adds crusading grievance. Presents via family, employers, courts; rarely voluntarily ("they have no wish to be cured; they believe they are right"). Under stress, brief quasi-psychotic episodes occur. Familial relationship with delusional disorder. Distinguish from delusional disorder (the paranoid personality's beliefs never reach fixed delusional conviction); from schizophrenia; from schizotypal (which adds perceptual oddness, unusual speech).

**Schizoid personality disorder.** Persistent detachment from social relationships and restricted emotional expression: chooses solitary activities, takes pleasure in few activities, lacks close friends, indifferent to praise or criticism, emotionally cold and detached. Little interest in sex or intimacy; can be attached (silently) to a few family members. Better occupational functioning than schizophrenia patients; apparent in early childhood. Key contrasts: schizotypal adds eccentricity and perceptual/cognitive distortions; avoidant (see below) *wants* relationships and suffers without them; the schizoid does not; anankastic constrains emotion to keep control, while the schizoid lacks the capacity for emotional display altogether.

**Schizotypal personality disorder.** Peculiarity and oddness in relationships, social detachment, cognitive-perceptual distortions: ideas of reference (not reaching delusional conviction), odd beliefs and magical thinking (sixth sense, telepathy, superstitions, rituals to ward off harm), unusual perceptual experiences (shadows becoming figures, sounds becoming calling voices), odd/digressive/vague speech, inappropriate or constricted affect, suspiciousness, odd appearance and mannerisms, social anxiety that *does not diminish with familiarity* (it is paranoid-based, unlike avoidant). Course: childhood solitariness, academic underachievement, bizarre fantasies; presents with comorbid depression, dysphoria, anxiety; under stress, transient psychotic minutes-to-hours episodes; in some, progression to brief psychotic disorder, schizophreniform or schizophrenia (schizotypal PD as premorbid state). Cultural caution (essential in India): mind-reading, evil eye, shamanic and possession beliefs that are normal in a subculture must not be labelled schizotypal. Treatment: low-dose antipsychotics for ideas of reference and perceptual symptoms; antidepressants for depressive states; long, patient confidence-building in psychotherapy.

### Cluster B

**Antisocial (dissocial) personality disorder.** Disregard for others' rights and safety without remorse; begins before age 15 (conduct disorder evidence required in DSM; ICD notes childhood conduct disorder supports the diagnosis; DSM requires age ≥18). May appear charming and understanding; history reveals recklessness unaffected by punishment, exploitativeness, manipulation, lying, glib cognitive style, easy hedonism interrupted by rage and cruelty, incapacity for guilt, refusal of authority. Suicide threats common; somatic preoccupation; deficient parenting. Biology: strong genetic contribution (twin/adoption studies); hyperactivity in childhood predicts adult antisociality; low CSF 5-HIAA and low platelet MAO with aggression; frontal inhibition deficits; parental deprivation, inconsistent care, family violence and severe physical abuse as developmental predictors; social disintegration produces *episodic* antisocial behaviour (a normal adaptation to an abnormal environment), but the early-onset pervasive pattern cannot be reduced to social determinants. Course: most pronounced in early adulthood, decreases with age; stable partnership and work motivation help; maturation may trade aggression for depression/hypochondriasis; HIV risk through substance use and promiscuity. Treatment: medication for symptomatic anxiety, rage, depression (SSRIs, lithium, carbamazepine, weaker effects than in BPD); methylphenidate only where ADHD coexists; **benzodiazepines contraindicated (behavioural disinhibition)**; psychotherapy efficacy low: therapeutic communities (Maxwell Jones tradition) offer the best-documented results.

**Borderline personality disorder.** Affective instability + impulsivity + unstable self-identity: frantic efforts to avoid abandonment; idealisation-devaluation relationships; identity diffusion with contradictory traits and chronic emptiness (intolerable, driving substance use and self-harm); impulsive self-aggression and suicide attempts; dysphoric, irritated mood mixing depression, anger, loneliness; black-and-white thinking; suggestibility; transient stress-related paranoid ideation or dissociative-like psychotic episodes (minutes-hours, following affective shifts in unstructured situations, differentiating from schizotypal's more persistent psychotic-like experiences); rejection sensitivity. History: term from Stern (1938) for the border between neuroses and psychoses; DSM-III separated it from schizotypal; Kernberg's borderline *organization* is broader (can underlie other severe PDs). Aetiology: family mood-disorder loading (not schizophrenia); antisocial PD and alcoholism in families; serotonin deficiency markers with impulsive aggression; HPA-axis dysfunction and amygdala sensitisation with cingulate/prefrontal under-regulation; childhood sexual/physical abuse and neglect strongly over-represented; attachment failures in early development. Comorbidity: depression (major, dysthymia, double depression), anxiety, somatisation, PTSD, alcohol; differentiation from **bipolar II**: bipolar mood swings run sadness/apathy ↔ euphoria, BPD reactivity runs sadness ↔ dysphoric anger within a day; from intermittent explosive disorder (lacks identity disturbance and affective instability). Course: severe dysfunction in work and relationships; **8–10% die by suicide**; every gesture must be taken seriously; the diagnosis becomes rare after 40 (maturation of neural structures and defences with age plus social learning). Treatment: symptom-targeted pharmacotherapy (SSRIs/venlafaxine for affective symptoms, impulsivity, suicidality; antipsychotics and anticonvulsants for cognitive-perceptual and impulsive symptoms; pragmatic sequential trials of 2–3 drugs; beware prescribed-drug abuse and non-compliance); psychotherapy is the core: DBT (Linehan) for emotional self-regulation and suicidal/impulsive behaviour; expressive psychoanalytic therapy focused on here-and-now transference and defence confrontation; supportive psychotherapy with psychoeducation for fragile patients, in practice the most used.

**Histrionic personality disorder.** Excessive emotionality and attention-seeking: uncomfortable when not the centre of attention; seductive/provocative interactions; rapidly shifting shallow emotions; appearance used to draw attention; impressionistic speech lacking detail; self-dramatisation and theatricality; suggestibility; relationships considered more intimate than they are. Cognitive style global and diffuse ("a life of illness": Kraepelin's description of symptoms in dramatic terms); sexualisation of non-sexual relationships, often with frigidity underneath; dependency disguised as romance. Prevalence 2–3% community; comorbidity with somatisation and conversion disorder, dissociative states, brief reactive psychoses. Two flavours (Akhtar): the *healthier* hysterical personality (integrated identity, repression-based, stable) versus the *sicker* histrionic (borderline organisation, splitting, promiscuity, erratic careers). Distinguish from mania (episodic), from borderline (more chaotic relationships and suicide attempts), from antisocial (shared promiscuity and shallowness, but histrionic has warmth and no calculated ruthless exploitation). Course: depressive episodes, suicide attempts, heavy medical-service use; may improve with maturation ("maturation of infantilism"). Treatment: antidepressants/anxiolytics for symptoms (with abuse-precautions); supportive therapy in crisis; psychotherapy confronting covert feelings with clear limits; demanding dependent behaviour must not be rewarded.

**Narcissistic personality disorder.** Grandiose self-importance, need for admiration, lack of empathy: fantasies of unlimited success/power/ideal love; belief in being special; entitlement; exploitation; envy and arrogant haughtiness. The engaging surface masks preoccupation with self-regard; successes yield no inner satisfaction: a "hungry, inferior real self" hides behind the compensatory grandiose self (often planted by high parental expectations and harsh criticism); pathological lying; mood swings and hypomanic-like exaltation when admired, vengeful rage and devaluation when the mirror fails; unable to fall in love (only fantasies of ideal love); trivialised sexuality; relationships as applauding crowds; **grandiosity is the best discriminator from borderline** (plus better impulse control, better social adjustment, fewer suicide attempts, less fragmentation risk). Kernberg: self-hatred rather than self-love at the root; *malignant narcissism* = narcissistic PD + antisocial behaviour + ego-syntonic sadism + paranoid orientation; the extreme end. Not an ICD-10 category ("other specific PDs"). Course: middle-age depression or defensive hypomania as "narcissistic supplies" dry up; hypochondriasis and anxiety complications. Treatment: anxiolytics/antidepressants for episodes; individual psychotherapy (analysis of idealising transference, interpretation of grandiosity, but supportive first, interpretations delayed until a confident attachment forms); serious countertransference hazards (devaluation, demands): the therapist must have worked through their own narcissism.

### Cluster C

**Avoidant (anxious) personality disorder.** Active social isolation *protecting an intense desire for connection*: avoids occupational interpersonal contact for fear of criticism; unwilling to get involved unless certain of being liked; restrained in intimacy (fear of shame/ridicule); preoccupied with criticism/rejection; inhibited in new situations; self-viewed as inept and inferior; reluctant to take risks that might embarrass. Extreme shyness, blushes and cries under scrutiny (then avoids further); fantasy as escape; open **in interview once rapport is established**, the clinical paradox: easier to talk to a doctor than to a party. Follows childhood stranger-fear and school shyness; worst in adolescence when relationships become demanding; remits or softens in older age. Distinguish from social phobia (arguably the same condition, impairment greater and onset earlier in avoidant PD); from schizoid (*avoidants suffer for their isolation and want relationships; schizoids accept isolation*); from dependent (avoidant fears criticism, dependent fears abandonment-of-care); from paranoid (avoidant fears being found inadequate; paranoid fears malicious intent). Treatment: anxiolytics, beta-blockers, MAOIs, antidepressants for the anxiety; assertiveness and self-esteem work, cognitive restructuring of self/other distortions, addressing dependency needs.

**Dependent personality disorder.** A pervasive need to be cared for: everyday decisions need excessive advice; others assume responsibility for major life areas; difficulty expressing disagreement (fear of losing support); cannot initiate projects alone; volunteers for unpleasant tasks to secure nurturance; helpless when alone; urgently seeks a new relationship when one ends; preoccupied with fears of abandonment. One person, partner or parent, may decide clothing, work, friendships; self-sacrificing to the point of tolerating verbal, physical or sexual abuse rather than lose the attachment; presents as helpless, unaggressive, pessimistic; can function well inside a protective relationship. Follows childhood separation anxiety or chronic illness-with-care. Distinguish from histrionic (dependent waits passively to be cared for; histrionic actively seduces/manipulates); from avoidant (no social shyness, fears loneliness not criticism); from borderline (borderline reacts to separation with emptiness and rage, demanding; the dependent becomes submissive and appeasing, hunting for the next carer). Cultural rule (crucial in India): passivity and deference normal in some societies, diagnose only deviation from the cultural average. Treatment: drugs only for depression/anxiety with losses; psychotherapy must *avoid fostering dependency on the therapist*, build self-confidence, autonomy, cognitive restructuring, social skills.

**Obsessive-compulsive (anankastic) personality disorder.** Exaggerated pervasive control: orderliness, details, rules, lists, schedules, punctuality; perfectionism that *interferes with task completion* (the report never finished, excessive correction); work to the exclusion of leisure; over-conscientious, inflexible morality; cannot discard worthless objects (hoards newspapers, broken appliances); miserly hoarding for future catastrophes; rigidity and stubbornness; cannot delegate ("no one does it as well as I"); indecisive without structured guidelines (doubt, data-gathering, delay); constricted affect: labels feelings, cannot display them; pedantic circumstantial history-taking (must finish the monologue before answering); indirect aggression (the small tip, the withheld favour); dominant-submissive dance with authority (excessive submission to a *respected* authority, obstructiveness with a disrespected one). Developmental relationship to OCD is **controversial**; most OCD patients do *not* have the personality disorder, and vice versa: the personality is ego-syntonic (the person endorses the traits), while OCD is ego-dystonic (fought against). Adolescent obsessive traits occasionally antedate schizophrenia ("pseudoneurotic schizophrenia"). Course: persistent; does well in detail-and-order jobs; vulnerable to unexpected change; **late-onset depression is a classic complication**; hypochondriasis when control is lost. Treatment: benzodiazepines for doubt/scruple tension; serotonergic antidepressants sometimes help; cognitive therapy targeting perfectionism, rigidity, intolerance of failure, the mainstay; psychodynamic work for repressed aggression, guilt, dependency needs.

### Beyond the ten

**Passive-aggressive (negativistic) personality disorder**: removed from DSM-IV main text (Appendix B as "negativistic PD", never in ICD): procrastination, stubborn resistance, "forgotten" obligations, sullen argumentativeness, feeling misunderstood and unappreciated, caustic complaints, martyrdom; best understood as a continuum: a defence mechanism that can be normal, a trait of many disorders, or a full disorder when pronounced and persistent. **Self-defeating (masochistic) personality**, also provisionally dropped: persistently seeking humiliation and failure, choosing abusive partners, undermining achievements, responding to success with depression.

## 8. Diagnosis: the discrimination table (the exam and clinic core)

| Pair | The discriminating question |
|---|---|
| Borderline vs bipolar II | Bipolar: sadness/apathy ↔ euphoria, episodes, decreased sleep need. BPD: minutes-to-hours reactivity, dysphoria + anger, triggered by interpersonal rejection, no true elation. |
| Borderline vs schizotypal psychotic-like features | BPD: brief, dissociative-like, follow affective shifts. Schizotypal: persistent odd beliefs/perceptions, odd speech. |
| Borderline vs antisocial | Borderline: affects, self-harm, capacity for intimacy (chaotic), some remorse. Antisocial: calculated exploitation, no remorse, no genuine attachment. |
| Avoidant vs schizoid | Both isolated. Avoidant: wants closeness, hurts without it, fears criticism. Schizoid: indifferent, no desire. |
| Avoidant vs dependent | Avoidant fears criticism/rejection. Dependent fears loss of care, and seeks it. |
| Dependent vs histrionic | Dependent: passive waiting for care. Histrionic: active seduction/manipulation for attention. |
| Dependent vs borderline (both fear abandonment) | Dependent: submissive, appeasing, hunts the next carer. Borderline: rage, emptiness, demanding. |
| Narcissistic vs histrionic | Narcissist wants admiration for superiority, lacks empathy. Histrionic wants attention, has empathy, clings. |
| Narcissistic vs borderline | Grandiosity, better impulse control, fewer suicide attempts. |
| Anankastic vs OCD (illness) | Ego-syntonic order/control vs ego-dystonic obsessions-compulsions with resistance. |
| Paranoid vs delusional disorder | Suspicious interpretations never reach fixed delusional conviction. |
| Paranoid vs avoidant withholding | Paranoid fears malice. Avoidant fears being found inadequate. |
| Schizotypal vs autism/communication disorders (children) | Language symptoms dominate and compensatory efforts in the developmental disorders; odd beliefs/perceptions dominate in schizotypal. |

**Assessment discipline:** every type diagnosis needs longitudinal, multi-source evidence; informant history is often decisive; defer definitive typing during acute depressive/psychotic states.

## 9. Management: one gesture per type (full programmes in the treatment note)

- **Paranoid:** treatment acceptance is the bottleneck, gain confidence, avoid confrontation of the suspicion system directly; treat comorbid depression/anxiety; low-dose antipsychotics if quasi-psychotic stress episodes appear.
- **Schizoid:** rarely seek help; low-dose antipsychotics sometimes useful; social skills training; respect the chosen distance while enlarging functioning.
- **Schizotypal:** low-dose antipsychotics for cognitive-perceptual symptoms; antidepressants for depressive presentations; long patience.
- **Antisocial:** treat comorbidity; SSRIs/lithium/anticonvulsants for aggression (modest); **benzodiazepines contraindicated**; therapeutic communities offer the best evidence; forensic coordination.
- **Borderline:** psychotherapy core (DBT for suicidality/impulsivity; structured dynamic therapy; supportive + psychoeducation in fragile patients); symptom-targeted pharmacotherapy; crisis-planning; treat comorbid depression.
- **Histrionic:** limit-setting without rewarding dependency; treat depressive/anxiety symptoms; guard against medication misuse.
- **Narcissistic:** supportive phase first, interpretations delayed; manage countertransference (devaluation); treat middle-age depressions.
- **Avoidant:** combined medication (anxiolytics/antidepressants/beta-blockers) with assertiveness training, cognitive restructuring, exposure-like social practice.
- **Dependent:** therapy that deliberately avoids recreating the dependency; autonomy-building, decision-making practice; treat losses.
- **Anankastic:** cognitive therapy of perfectionism and failure-intolerance; benzodiazepines for doubt-tension; watch for late-onset depression.

## 10. Indian practice

- **The bipolar-borderline conflation** is the highest-value discrimination in Indian OPDs: young women with rapid, reactive mood shifts and self-harm get over-diagnosed as "rapid-cycling bipolar" and over-treated with mood stabilisers. Apply the table above before escalating medication.
- **Cultural relativity is a diagnostic duty**: deference to elders, joint-family decision-making, arranged-marriage adjustments and religious observance can look "dependent" or "anankastic" to a checklist. The Oxford rule (diagnose only what is markedly beyond the culture's average) protects against pathologising normal Indian patterns; conversely, do not let cultural cover hide genuine, distressing disorder.
- **Somatic routes**: Cluster C distress presents through somatic complaints and faith-healing before it reaches psychiatry; Cluster B arrives through emergency self-harm and "family problem" labels.
- **Marriage and disclosure**: a personality-disorder label carries alliance consequences; give the family a working explanation ("a long-standing pattern of reacting, treatable over time") rather than a stigmatising verdict.
- **Services**: dedicated PD programmes are scarce; NIMHANS and a few centres run DBT-informed therapy; the realistic Indian pathway is structured general-psychiatry care with clear crisis plans, planned follow-up and family psychoeducation.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: The Friday therapist and Monday's villain.** A 22-year-old student in her third psychiatric consultation brings a notebook: cut wrists since 16, three overdoses, a best friend "perfect" until last month then "evil", an engagement broken and re-broken twice, chronic emptiness ("I don't know who I am"), two hours of paranoid feelings after her boyfriend ignored her messages at a party. Previous files: "bipolar affective disorder" on two prescriptions of valproate; no sustained elevation, no decreased sleep need. She is warm, quick to trust; then, at the end of the session, furious that the session ended "like everyone leaves". Management begins with re-formulation: emotionally unstable (borderline) PD, comorbid depressive episodes; valproate reassessed against actual symptoms; a written crisis card (which service to contact, which strategies first), scheduled (not crisis-driven) appointments, and referral into the hospital's DBT-informed group with family psychoeducation for the parents. Teaching points: idealisation-devaluation including of the treating clinician; the 8–10% suicide mortality makes every gesture serious; structured, predictable care is itself an intervention.

**Case 2: The perfect clerk who could not retire.** A 58-year-old bank officer is referred for "depression" after being bypassed for promotion; his wife reports he cannot sleep, checks the doors five times nightly, and has become "impossible; everything must be his way". Lifelong pattern: worked weekends, holidays unused in decades, subordinates given instruction lists no one could follow, unable to discard 20 years of bank statements, moralistic about colleagues' shortcuts, decisions deferred until "all the data was in", affection expressed as correction. No true obsessions or compulsions: he *endorses* his standards. Formulation: anankastic personality disorder with late-onset depression triggered by loss of control (the promotion, an approaching retirement). Treatment: antidepressant for the depressive episode; cognitive work on perfectionism and catastrophe-anticipation; explicit planning of a structured retirement (roles, routines), because unstructured time is the threat. Teaching points: the anankastic's decompensation arrives with unpredictable change; ego-syntonicity separates him from the OCD patient; treat the episode first, the style patiently.

## 13. FAQ

**"Is 'borderline' a slur?"** No: it began as a location (the border between neurosis and psychosis in 1938) and is now a defined, treatable condition. If the word feels loaded in your setting, ICD-10's "emotionally unstable personality disorder" is the same construct.

**"Does she have bipolar or borderline?"** The practical test: bipolar moods last days-to-weeks with true elation and sleep changes, cycling semi-independently of relationships; borderline moods flip within hours, from dysphoria to rage to emptiness, usually after rejection or abandonment fears. Both can coexist, which is why longitudinal history matters more than any single visit.

**"My brother has always been like this, can people really change?"** Yes, gradually and with the right structure: borderline symptoms decline substantially with age and treatment; even antisocial behaviour typically burns down after early adulthood. "Personality" is not carved in stone; follow-up studies are clearer on this than the textbooks imply.

**"Is schizoid the same as autism?"** No. Both can be solitary, but schizoid personality involves normal early development with a chosen, stable detachment in adulthood; autism is a neurodevelopmental condition with early social-communication differences and restricted interests.

**"Will an SSRI fix my personality?"** No drug rewrites a personality style. Medication treats *states within* the disorder (the depressive episodes, the rage spikes, the impulsive urges) and makes psychotherapy workable. Expect symptom relief, not a new character.

**"Is narcissism treatable?"** Harder than most, because the person rarely experiences their grandiosity as a problem until middle age, when supplies (admiration, looks, status) dry up and depression or hypomania breaks through. That crisis is often the treatable entry point.

**"Why does the doctor keep asking about my childhood?"** Because the risk architecture is built early: abuse and neglect raise borderline risk; harsh criticism and impossible expectations feed narcissistic and anankastic patterns; contradictory parenting feeds passive-aggression. The history explains the pattern; the treatment still happens now.

**"Is being orderly and careful a disorder?"** Only when it costs more than it buys; perfectionism that prevents finishing, control that prevents living, hoarding that fills the house, morals that forbid rest. Culture defines the average; disorder is the marked, impairing deviation.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **Cluster mnemonics**: A "Weird/Wild" (paranoid-schizoid-schizotypal); B "Wild" (antisocial-borderline-histrionic-narcissistic); C "Worried" (avoidant-dependent-obsessive-compulsive).
- **Age ≥18 + conduct disorder before 15**, the two gates of antisocial PD; ICD's "dissocial" adds low frustration tolerance and inability to profit from punishment.
- **The 8–10% suicide figure** for borderline; quote it, and the "take every gesture seriously" rule.
- **Kernberg triad**: identity diffusion + primitive defences (splitting, projective identification) + intact reality testing = borderline *organisation*, broader than the DSM *category*.
- **Genetics one-liners**: D4DR-novelty-seeking (10% of genetic variance, weak replications); 5-HTT short variant-neuroticism (3–4% variance); MAO-A × abuse, the gene-environment interaction; V1a receptor: the prairie-vole monogamy transfer; low CSF 5-HIAA: impulsive aggression.
- **Course rules**: antisocial declines with age; borderline/histrionic/avoidant improve with age; narcissistic and anankastic decompensate in middle/late life (depression, hypochondriasis).
- **Treatment traps**: benzodiazepines contraindicated in antisocial (disinhibition); histrionic: limit-setting, don't reward dependency; narcissistic: support first, interpret late; dependent: don't become the next person they can't leave.
- **Indian exam corner**: EUPD subtypes (impulsive/borderline); the bipolar-vs-borderline discrimination as a short-note favourite; cultural caution before labelling dependency/obsession.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**Q1.** A 23-year-old woman has intense, hours-long mood shifts from rage to emptiness triggered by her partner's unavailability, three episodes of wrist-cutting, and a series of relationships moving from idealisation to devaluation. There is no history of sustained elation or decreased sleep need. The most likely diagnosis is:
- (a) Bipolar II disorder
- (b) Emotionally unstable (borderline) personality disorder
- (c) Histrionic personality disorder
- (d) Intermittent explosive disorder

**Answer: (b).** Interpersonally triggered, rapid, dysphoric reactivity plus identity disturbance, self-harm and idealisation-devaluation define the borderline picture; bipolar requires sustained episodes with true elation.

**Q2.** Which single feature best separates avoidant personality disorder from schizoid personality disorder?
- (a) Avoidants dress oddly
- (b) Avoidants desire relationships and suffer from their absence
- (c) Schizoids have perceptual distortions
- (d) Avoidants have eccentric speech

**Answer: (b).** The avoidant isolates to protect a wanted connection; the schizoid is indifferent to it. Perceptual oddness belongs to schizotypal, not either.

**Q3.** The MAO-A finding most cited in personality disorder aetiology is:
- (a) Low-activity MAO-A variant alone predicts adult violence
- (b) Childhood abuse alone predicts adult violence
- (c) Abused children carrying the low-activity MAO-A variant show elevated later antisocial behaviour, a gene-environment interaction
- (d) MAO-A genotype fully determines aggressive temperament

**Answer: (c).** Neither gene nor environment alone: the risk emerges from their interaction, the model lesson of personality genetics.

**Q4.** A 26-year-old man with dissocial personality disorder becomes agitated during detoxification. Which drug class is specifically contraindicated?
- (a) SSRIs
- (b) Benzodiazepines
- (c) Antipsychotics
- (d) Anticonvulsants

**Answer: (b).** Benzodiazepines can cause behavioural disinhibition in antisocial personality disorder, a classic, exam-ready caution.

**Q5.** The best single clinical discriminator between narcissistic and borderline personality disorder is:
- (a) Suicide attempts
- (b) Grandiosity
- (c) Gender distribution
- (d) Impulsivity

**Answer: (b).** Grandiosity distinguishes narcissism; borderline additionally shows more suicide attempts, poorer impulse control, and greater risk of regressive fragmentation.

**Q6.** An obsessive-compulsive personality disorder patient differs from an obsessive-compulsive disorder patient chiefly because the personality-disorder patient:
- (a) Has true obsessions and compulsions
- (b) Endorses their standards and habits as correct (ego-syntonic)
- (c) Responds completely to SSRIs
- (d) Experiences symptoms only in adolescence

**Answer: (b).** Ego-syntonicity is the classic separator: the anankastic *wants* order and control; the OCD patient fights their symptoms.
