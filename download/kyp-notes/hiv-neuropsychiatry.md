# HIV-Associated Dementia & HAND — When the Virus Reaches the Brain

## 1. In one line + overview

**A dementia of young adults, caused by HIV itself, where the legs and the motivation slow down before the memory does.**

HIV can enter the brain early in infection, hidden inside infected immune cells. In some people (mainly those with advanced, untreated disease) this produces a characteristic pattern: mental slowing, forgetfulness, apathy, clumsiness and leg weakness that worsen over months. The Oxford chapter calls this HIV-associated dementia, the severe end of a spectrum that also includes milder cognitive impairment. Because the patients are often young, and because the early symptoms look like depression or "not coping", the diagnosis is frequently missed at first. This is also one of the few dementias where treating the cause, antiretroviral therapy (ART), can slow, halt, or partly reverse it. The psychiatrist's jobs are: spot it early, separate it from depression and from opportunistic brain infections, treat the behavioural and mood complications with extra pharmacological caution, and protect adherence to ART when memory itself is failing.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Describe how HIV enters and damages the brain (the "Trojan horse") without inventing mechanisms.
- Recognise the early triad (cognitive slowing, apathy, motor slowing) that distinguishes HIV-associated dementia from cortical dementias like Alzheimer's.
- State the WHO and American Academy of Neurology (AAN) diagnostic logic in plain words.
- List the differential diagnosis that matters most: depression, cerebral toxoplasmosis, primary CNS lymphoma, cryptococcal meningitis, CNS tuberculosis, CMV encephalitis, neurosyphilis.
- Explain why patients with AIDS are exquisitely sensitive to antipsychotic side effects, and name the classic herbal interaction (St John's Wort and protease inhibitors).
- Use methylphenidate (5–20 mg/day) for apathy and slowing where appropriate.
- Place the disorder in Indian practice: NACO ART centres, free treatment, stigma, and who actually makes the diagnosis.

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global (Oxford figures).** Before combination therapy, HIV-associated dementia was one of the most common causes of dementia in young adults. In a multicentre cohort, incidence fell from about 21 per 1,000 person-years (1990–92) to about 10.5 per 1,000 person-years (1996–98) after highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) was introduced, halving the risk. Post-mortem data then showed severe HIV encephalopathy largely disappearing while mild-to-moderate encephalopathy became more common, probably because people now survive longer with the virus in the brain. A further rise in incidence was suggested around 2003 as treatment failure accumulated in some cohorts.

**India.** India has one of the largest populations living with HIV in the world; NACO estimates have put it in the low millions (adult prevalence roughly 0.2%), concentrated in high-risk groups and in certain districts. The mental-health consequence is under-measured, but the practical point stands: most people with HIV in India are diagnosed late, present to general hospitals and district ART centres rather than psychiatry, and HIV-associated neurocognitive impairment is usually detected, if at all, by physicians and ART-centre counsellors rather than by psychiatrists. Any young adult presenting with cognitive decline plus motor slowing in an Indian setting deserves an HIV test as part of the workup, because the diagnosis is treatable and stigmatised patients rarely volunteer it.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

- **Viral:** active HIV infection of the CNS; viral load in blood and CSF.
- **Biological:** advanced immunosuppression; low CD4 T-lymphocyte count is a predictor of faster progression.
- **Cellular mechanism:** infected and activated macrophages and microglia release neurotoxins that injure and kill neurons (apoptosis).
- **Direct toxicity:** viral proteins (e.g. the envelope protein gp120) may also damage neurons directly.
- **Vulnerable regions:** frontal lobes, subcortical white matter, basal ganglia (post-mortem findings).
- **Behavioural/social:** history of intravenous drug use predicts faster progression.
- **Treatment history:** untreated or failing antiretroviral therapy.
- **Co-morbidity:** secondary infections and metabolic disturbances can aggravate the cognitive picture.
- **Indian context:** late diagnosis, interrupted ART due to stigma, migration and distance from ART centres, and self-medication with herbal products (which sets up drug interactions) all raise risk.

## 6. What happens in the brain: three short stories

**The Trojan horse.** HIV cannot simply walk into the brain. It hides inside macrophages (immune cells) that it has infected, and these cells carry it across the blood-brain barrier. Once inside, the virus infects glial cells and sets up a long, smouldering infection in precisely the regions that serve speed and drive; frontal lobes, white matter, basal ganglia.

**The innocent bystanders die.** It is not the virus eating neurons directly that does most of the damage. The infected and activated macrophages and microglia pump out neurotoxins, and neurons are injured and die by apoptosis. In this sense HIV-associated dementia is an inflammatory bystander disease of the brain: the immune battle kills the tissue it is trying to protect.

**The thermostat story (why ART changed everything).** Before 1996 the brain infection burned unchecked: people deteriorated in months. Antiretroviral therapy does not always cross into the brain well, but it lowers the systemic fire enough to reduce the incidence of dementia and stretch its course. People still live with the virus in the brain for decades, so milder impairment is now the commonest form, and survival after dementia diagnosis rose from about 5 months (1993–95) to about 38.5 months (1996–2000) in cohort data.

## 7. Symptoms

**Early cognitive symptoms (the "slowing" cluster).** Forgetfulness, loss of concentration, mental slowing; missing appointments, needing lists for ordinary duties; losing track of conversations or one's own train of thought; needing extra time to organise thoughts and finish daily tasks.

**Early behavioural symptoms (the "apathy" cluster).** Reduced spontaneity, blunted emotional responsiveness, social withdrawal; indifference to personal and professional responsibilities; falling work output; fatigue, malaise, loss of sexual drive. Depression, irritability, emotional lability, agitation and psychotic symptoms can also occur.

**Early motor symptoms (the "legs" cluster).** Loss of balance and coordination, clumsiness, leg weakness; the person drops things, trips more often, writes and eats less precisely, and walks more carefully.

**On examination (early stage).** Mental status testing may be near-normal, showing only slowed verbal/motor responses and difficulty recalling objects after 5 minutes or more. Neurological signs: postural tremor, brisk lower-limb reflexes (hyperreflexia), ataxia visible only on rapid turns or tandem gait, slowed rapid alternating movements, frontal release signs (snout reflex, palmar grasp), dysarthria, and interrupted smooth pursuit or slowed/inaccurate saccades on eye-movement testing.

**Late stage.** Global cognitive deterioration with severe psychomotor retardation; speech slow and monotonous with word-finding difficulty, possibly progressing to mutism; paraparesis leaves the person bed-bound, often indifferent to illness and surroundings; bladder and bowel incontinence; myoclonus and seizures may appear; painful paraesthesias from concurrent sensory neuropathy. Consciousness is usually preserved, except for hypersomnolence, which is one of several features that separates this from delirium.

**The modern spectrum (clinical update, post-Oxford).** Today's neurology uses the umbrella term HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder (HAND) with three tiers (asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment, mild neurocognitive disorder, and HIV-associated dementia) replacing the older "minor cognitive/motor disorder" of the WHO 1990 and AAN 1991 criteria. The Oxford chapter's clinical logic survives intact inside this modern scheme.

## 8. Diagnosis

**The WHO (1990) logic, in plain words.** The ICD-10 research criteria for dementia are met, with HIV-specific modifications: (a) memory decline may not be severe enough to impair daily activities; (b) motor decline is part of the syndrome and verified clinically/neuropsychologically; (c) symptoms last at least 1 month; (d) aphasia, agnosia and apraxia, the cortical flagship signs, are unusual. Plus: laboratory evidence of systemic HIV infection, and no evidence of another aetiology on history, examination, CSF and CT/MRI (to exclude active CNS opportunistic processes).

**The AAN (1991) logic, in plain words.** Laboratory evidence of HIV, plus acquired abnormality in at least two cognitive abilities (attention, processing speed, abstraction/reasoning, visuospatial skills, memory/learning, speech/language) for at least 1 month, plus at least one of: motor abnormality, or decline in motivation/emotional control/social behaviour; no clouding of consciousness while establishing the cognitive deficit; no other aetiology found. Both systems grade severity (mild/moderate/severe) by impairment in daily activities.

**Neuropsychological profile.** The worst scores are in fine motor control (finger tapping, grooved pegboard), rapid sequential problem solving (trail-making, digit symbol), visuospatial problem solving (block design), verbal fluency (spontaneity), and visual memory, while naming and vocabulary are largely preserved even in advanced disease. This subcortical signature helps separate it from Alzheimer's disease.

**Separating depression (pseudodementia) from dementia: the three warning signs.** (1) Intratest variability: failing easy items then answering harder ones correctly. (2) Mood-congruent complaint/performance mismatch: complaining of difficulty while performing near-perfectly. (3) "I don't know" or giving-up answers that turn correct with gentle urging. Remember: both can coexist in the same HIV-positive patient.

**Imaging.** Cerebral atrophy with widened cortical sulci (less often, enlarged ventricles) is the main finding; MRI often adds T2 hyperintensities in periventricular white matter and centrum semiovale (sometimes basal ganglia/thalamus), without mass effect. The same scan performs differential work: ring-enhancing lesions point to cerebral toxoplasmosis; contrast-enhancing mass lesions point to primary CNS lymphoma.

**CSF.** Typical findings: raised total protein, raised IgG fraction and index, possible mononuclear pleocytosis; HIV core antigen p24 can be detected (though also in neurologically normal subjects), and HIV RNA by PCR; immune-activation markers (neopterin, β2-microglobulin, quinolinic acid) and cytokines may be raised but are not specific. The decisive differential tests: India ink stain, cryptococcal antigen and fungal culture for cryptococcal meningitis; CSF also identifies CNS tuberculosis, CMV encephalitis and neurosyphilis.

**Differential diagnosis table.**

| Mimic | Distinguishing clue |
|---|---|
| Depressive pseudodementia | Variability, "I don't know" answers, performance better than complaint |
| Cerebral toxoplasmosis | Ring-enhancing lesions on CT/MRI; focal signs; fever |
| Primary CNS lymphoma | Contrast-enhancing mass lesion; may resemble toxoplasmosis, trial of anti-toxoplasma therapy or biopsy |
| Cryptococcal meningitis | Headache, meningism, raised CSF pressure; India ink/antigen positive |
| CNS tuberculosis / neurosyphilis / CMV encephalitis | CSF and serology-directed; consider in Indian practice early, given TB prevalence |
| Delirium | Fluctuating consciousness: in HIV dementia, consciousness is preserved |
| Substance-related cognitive impairment | History; intravenous drug use also predicts faster dementia progression |

## 9. Management

**Treat the driver: antiretroviral therapy.** The regimen should be started/optimised with the physician and monitored constantly: ART reduced dementia incidence and improved psychomotor speed in cohort studies, but the optimal regimen for the dementia itself has not been established. (Modern practice debates which drugs penetrate CSF best; the honest position is that a CNS-optimised regimen has not been proven superior, echoing the Oxford chapter's caution.) *India note: ART is free through NACO-programme centres; psychiatrists should reinforce, never duplicate or alter, the ART prescription, and coordinate with the ART-centre physician.*

**Treat aggravators.** Secondary infections and metabolic disturbances (common in advanced HIV) must be diagnosed and managed, as they worsen cognition.

**Apathy and slowing.** Methylphenidate 5–20 mg/day is useful for apathy and cognitive slowing with relatively mild side effects. Cholinesterase inhibitors such as donepezil have only anecdotal support here, unlike Alzheimer's disease.

**Psychotic symptoms / behavioural dyscontrol.** Standard strategies apply, but with two cautions from the Oxford chapter: AIDS patients given typical antipsychotics are particularly prone to extrapyramidal side effects and neuroleptic malignant syndrome; preliminary evidence suggests some atypical antipsychotics are tolerated even by patients who had to stop standard neuroleptics. Start low, go slow, review early. *India note: haloperidol is still the most available typical in Indian emergency settings, in AIDS patients it deserves extra justification and the shortest possible course.*

**Depression.** Tricyclics and SSRIs work about as well as in HIV-negative patients; SSRIs appear better tolerated, except in patients troubled by diarrhoea.

**Interactions.** Psychotropics and antiretrovirals can interact; the classic case is St John's Wort inducing metabolism of the protease inhibitor indinavir and dropping its levels to treatment-failure range. Ask every patient about herbal and Ayurvedic self-medication.

**Psychosocial management.** Maintain a structured daily schedule; titrate (adjust in steps) external stimuli; restrict to familiar environments; provide frequent orienting interactions with significant others; monitor personal and financial affairs early, while the person can still participate. Family psychoeducation is essential.

**Service questions.** Care makes increasing demands on health and volunteer/community systems; it remains uncertain whether specialised units or general psychiatric/medical services are best. Behavioural disturbance (poor impulse control, sexual acting-out) can pose risk to other patients and staff and needs explicit safety planning. Terminal-stage placement is often blocked by the lack of appropriate community options; an even harder problem in India, where hospice capacity is thin and family carers carry the load.

## 10. Indian practice

- **Where care happens.** Government ART centres (under NACO) provide free antiretrovirals and counselling; district hospitals manage complications; psychiatry is usually consulted late; for "behavioural" problems, depression, or when family report "he has become dull".
- **Who makes the diagnosis.** Often the ART-centre physician or a neurologist; the psychiatrist's role is the depression-vs-dementia separation, behavioural and mood management, and adherence support.
- **Stigma is a treatment issue.** Disclosure fears, employment loss, and marriage concerns delay presentation and interrupt ART, both of which feed directly into dementia risk. Counselling around disclosure is a clinical intervention here, not a courtesy.
- **Costs.** ART itself is free in the programme; out-of-pocket costs arise for imaging (MRI, contrast), CSF procedures and private consultation (vary widely by city; approx 2026). Methylphenidate in India is a Schedule X drug; prescription and dispensing are tightly controlled, which affects availability outside major centres.
- **The herbal question.** Patients commonly use herbal/Ayurvedic preparations; the St John's Wort–indinavir interaction is the textbook example of why this must be asked about by name.
- **Primary care role.** An MBBS doctor seeing a young adult with "slowing + imbalance + apathy" should include an HIV test in the workup; the diagnosis is stigmatised and rarely volunteered, but it is treatable.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: "He has become dull" (the missed window).** A 34-year-old migrant worker, diagnosed HIV-positive two years earlier at a district ART centre, stopped attending after four months ("no time, no bus money, people talk"). His wife brings him to a psychiatry OPD because he has stopped calling home, forgets conversations, walks carefully at night, and was reprimanded at work for slow output. She believes he is "in depression". Examination: postural tremor, brisk knee reflexes, slowed alternating finger movements; mini-mental testing shows slowed responses with 5-minute recall difficulty. Depressive pseudodementia signs are actively looked for, but his complaints understate his deficits, the opposite pattern. CD4 count is very low. Management: re-linkage to the ART centre (the psychiatrist phones the counsellor), workup for opportunistic infection (MRI + CSF), structured daily routine and orientation at home, and a family session explaining that the "dullness" is a brain illness of the HIV itself, not a character change. Teaching points: re-engagement with ART is the disease-modifying move; cognitive impairment and poor adherence form a vicious cycle; the wife's presence at the OPD is the single strongest prognostic asset available.

**Case 2: The night-fever trap (opportunistic mimic).** A 28-year-old man on ART for six years presents with two weeks of forgetfulness plus mild left-arm weakness; his mother reports evening fevers. MRI shows ring-enhancing lesions. The psychiatric question, "is the HIV dementia worsening?", is the wrong question: this is cerebral toxoplasmosis until proven otherwise, and the treating team starts anti-toxoplasma therapy rather than changing psychotropics. Teaching points: focal signs and fever should pull the clinician out of the dementia script; in India, TB of the CNS belongs in the same differential; the psychiatrist's contribution is refusing to medicalise a new focal neurological picture as "progression".

## 13. FAQ

**"Is my memory loss because of the virus, or because I am depressed?"** It can be either, and often both. The interview and testing patterns differ: depression complains loudly and performs variably; dementia under-complains and performs consistently badly. Both deserve treatment, and the answer usually clarifies within weeks of treating the depression and the HIV.

**"Can this get better?"** With antiretroviral therapy many patients stabilise or improve partly, especially the psychomotor speed. Full reversal of established dementia is uncommon. The earlier treatment starts, the better the outcome.

**"Is it contagious to care for him?"** No. HIV is transmitted through blood and sexual contact, not touching, feeding or sharing a room. Families should be told this explicitly, because fear-driven distance worsens care.

**"He refuses to take the ART tablets, he forgets as well."** This is a recognised bind: the illness damages the very system needed to treat it. Practical answers: supervised daily dosing tied to a fixed routine (meals, prayer time), pill organisers, a designated family "tablet monitor", and counselling at the ART centre rather than scolding.

**"The doctor gave risperidone for his aggression, is that safe?"** It can be used with caution in low doses and with early review. In advanced AIDS, typical antipsychotics carry high risks of stiffness and neuroleptic malignant syndrome; atypicals are generally better tolerated. Report new fever with stiffness urgently.

**"Can he take Ayurvedic medicines alongside ART?"** Some herbal products powerfully alter antiretroviral levels; St John's Wort is the documented example with indinavir. The safe rule: list everything he takes, and let the ART physician check the combination before continuing.

**"Will he end up bed-bound?"** The untreated disease could progress there, paraplegia, incontinence, mutism. On effective ART, many patients never approach that stage, and those who do progress usually do so slowly, over years.

**"Who will look after him at the end?"** This deserves early, honest planning: identify the main carer, brief the family doctor, arrange respite, and consider community/hospice options. In India the realistic answer is usually the family, with programme support, so supporting the family is part of treating the patient.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **High-yield tagline:** the dementia of *subcortical speed* (slow, apathetic, clumsy) not the *cortical forgetfulness* of Alzheimer's; naming and vocabulary are preserved.
- **The naming timeline (classic viva):** AIDS dementia complex (1986, Navia) → WHO "HIV-associated dementia" + minor cognitive/motor disorder (1990) → AAN nomenclature (1991) → modern three-tier HAND (post-Oxford; know it as an update).
- **The four WHO modifications to ICD-10 dementia:** memory decline may not hit daily function; motor decline counts; 1-month minimum; aphasia/agnosia/apraxia unusual.
- **Diagnosis needs three legs:** evidence of HIV, the cognitive-motor-behaviour decline, and *exclusion* of other causes; CSF and CT/MRI are not optional.
- **Mnemonic for the differential (T-C-N-T-S-M):** Toxo, Cryptococcus, lymphoma (N for "neoplasm"), Tuberculosis, Syphilis/CMV, Metabolic, the opportunistic crowd that fakes progression.
- **Drug traps:** typical antipsychotics → EPS/NMS in AIDS; St John's Wort → protease-inhibitor failure; SSRIs preferred over TCAs except in diarrhoea; methylphenidate 5–20 mg/day for apathy.
- **Indian exam corner:** free ART via NACO centres; CNS tuberculosis belongs early in the differential; HIV test in any young subcortical dementia; methylphenidate is Schedule X in India.
- **Numbers worth remembering:** incidence 21.1 → 10.5 per 1,000 person-years (pre- vs post-HAART); survival after diagnosis 5 → 38.5 months.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**Q1.** A 32-year-old man with advanced HIV presents with 2 months of forgetfulness, mental slowing, apathy and leg clumsiness. Consciousness is preserved; examination shows brisk knee reflexes and slowed rapid alternating movements. Which pattern best fits HIV-associated dementia?
- (a) Cortical: early aphasia, agnosia and apraxia with preserved processing speed
- (b) Subcortical: slowed processing, apathy and motor slowing with preserved naming and vocabulary
- (c) Amnestic-only: isolated 5-minute recall deficit with intact everything else
- (d) Fluctuating attention with visual hallucinations, worse at night

**Answer: (b).** The disorder is a subcortical dementia of speed and drive; naming/vocabulary are preserved even late, and consciousness is preserved, ruling out the cortical and delirium patterns.

**Q2.** During neuropsychological assessment of an HIV-positive patient reporting severe memory difficulty, the examiner notices she fails easy items but answers harder ones correctly, complains about tests she performs near-perfectly, and answers "I don't know" but gives the correct answer when gently urged. The most likely explanation is:
- (a) HIV-associated dementia
- (b) Depressive pseudodementia (with genuine coexistence still to be assessed)
- (c) Cerebral toxoplasmosis
- (d) Malingering, by definition

**Answer: (b).** These are the classic three pseudodementia signs from the Oxford chapter; the caveat is that depression and dementia can coexist in HIV, so improvement after depression treatment must be verified.

**Q3.** Which investigation pair is specifically required by the diagnostic criteria to exclude opportunistic processes before diagnosing HIV-associated dementia?
- (a) EEG and electromyography
- (b) Cerebrospinal fluid analysis and CT or MRI
- (c) Nerve conduction studies and audiometry
- (d) Serial mini-mental examinations only

**Answer: (b).** Both WHO and AAN logic require exclusion of other aetiology; the chapter names CSF analysis plus CT/MRI as the minimum exclusion set.

**Q4.** An AIDS patient develops new fever and pronounced muscle stiffness two days after a typical antipsychotic was started for agitation. The most important immediate concern is:
- (a) Simple akathisia, reassure and continue
- (b) Neuroleptic malignant syndrome: stop the drug and treat as a medical emergency
- (c) HIV dementia progression
- (d) Serotonin syndrome from an SSRI started two weeks earlier

**Answer: (b).** The Oxford chapter specifically flags the high risk of extrapyramidal effects and neuroleptic malignant syndrome with typical antipsychotics in AIDS patients.

**Q5.** A patient on a protease inhibitor self-medicates with a herbal antidepressant. Which classical interaction should the clinician know cold?
- (a) St John's Wort induces protease-inhibitor metabolism and can cause treatment failure
- (b) Ginkgo biloba increases protease-inhibitor levels to toxicity
- (c) Garlic potentiates all antiretrovirals equally
- (d) No documented herbal interactions exist

**Answer: (a).** The documented example is St John's Wort lowering indinavir concentrations into the failure range (Piscitelli 2000).

**Q6.** Which statement about methylphenidate in HIV-associated dementia is correct per the Oxford chapter?
- (a) It is first-line for the dementia itself, replacing ART
- (b) 5–20 mg/day is useful for apathy and cognitive slowing, with relatively mild side effects
- (c) It is contraindicated with all antiretrovirals
- (d) Evidence equals that for donepezil in Alzheimer's disease

**Answer: (b).** Methylphenidate addresses apathy/psychomotor slowing; cholinesterase inhibitors have only anecdotal support in this condition.
