# Neurotransmitters & Signalling — How Brain Cells Talk, and Where Psychiatry Intervenes

## 1. In one line + overview

**Chemical neurotransmission is the shared language of the brain (dozens of transmitter families, hundreds of receptor proteins, cascades of second messengers) and nearly every drug in psychiatry works by whispering into this conversation.**

The Oxford chapter tells this story from first principles. Sherrington named the synapse in the 19th century; Loewi identified the first transmitter (acetylcholine) in 1921; today we count tens to hundreds of signalling molecules, amines, amino acids, neuropeptides, purines, trophic factors, cytokines, lipids, even gases. The chapter's teaching architecture: how a transmitter is made, released, received, and switched off; the two great receptor families (fast ion channels vs slow G-protein-coupled receptors); the retrograde messengers that talk backwards across the synapse; and the signalling cascades that reach the genome; the current best explanation for why antidepressants take weeks rather than minutes to work. Every concept lands on a drug you already prescribe: fluoxetine blocks SERT, methylphenidate blocks DAT, reserpine blocks VMAT, diazepam opens GABA-A, clozapine blocks D2 and 5-HT2A, lithium dampens the inositol cycle.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:

- Trace the life cycle of a small-molecule transmitter: synthesis → vesicular storage → release → reception → reuptake → metabolism.
- Name the plasma-membrane and vesicular transporters, and match each to the drugs that act on it (DAT/NET/SERT; GAT-1 and tiagabine; VMAT and reserpine).
- Contrast ligand-gated ion channels with G-protein-coupled receptors structurally and functionally, with psychiatric examples of each.
- Explain retrograde signalling (nitric oxide, endocannabinoids) and why CB1 is a drug target.
- Describe second messenger cascades (cAMP, IP3/DAG, cGMP), their regulation (PDEs, phosphatases), and the lithium-inositol and GSK3β stories.
- Outline the CREB → BDNF → plasticity/neurogenesis hypothesis of delayed antidepressant action, as a hypothesis, honestly flagged.
- Connect the chapter's India-relevant pharmacology: reserpine from *Rauwolfia serpentina*.

## 4. The core ideas

### 4.1 What counts as a neurotransmitter

The classical criteria: the molecule is localised in neurones, released from terminals (and often soma and dendrites) on depolarisation, and exerts its effects through receptors on the target cell. Three updates have rewritten this tidy definition. First, the old split between fast "neurotransmitters" and slow "neuromodulators" is abandoned; the same molecule can act fast or slow depending on *which receptor* it meets. Second, tens or hundreds of molecules qualify: the three classic classes (amines, amino acids, neuropeptides) plus purines (adenosine, ATP), neurotrophic factors (BDNF, NGF), cytokines and chemokines, endocannabinoids (anandamide), and gases (nitric oxide, carbon monoxide). Third, some signals travel **backwards**: synthesised in the *postsynaptic* neurone, diffusing back to act on the *presynaptic* terminal (retrograde messengers).

### 4.2 The life and death of a small-molecule transmitter

Amines and amino acids are made **at the nerve terminal** by a few enzymatic steps, pumped into small synaptic vesicles by proton-coupled **vesicular transporters**, and released when an action potential arrives. After release, the transmitter crosses the cleft and binds postsynaptic receptors; it also spills onto **presynaptic autoreceptors**, providing negative feedback. Termination is by **reuptake**: sodium-coupled plasma-membrane transporters (DAT for dopamine, NET for noradrenaline, SERT for 5-HT) pull the transmitter back, keeping extracellular levels low and allowing reuse, and delivering it to catabolic enzymes such as monoamine oxidase. This is where psychopharmacology lives: amphetamines and cocaine act at DAT/NET/SERT; most antidepressants block reuptake at SERT/NET; the molecular mechanism of tricyclic binding has been revealed structurally.

**GABA** has four transporters (GAT-1/2/3, BGT-1); **tiagabine**'s anticonvulsant effect comes from GAT-1 blockade. **Glycine** has GLYT1 (glial, forebrain) and GLYT2 (neuronal, hindbrain/spinal cord), and glycine is also a positive co-modulator at the NMDA receptor, so blocking GLYT1 might enhance NMDA signalling without direct agonism and its excitotoxicity; this is the logic testing glycine-transport blockers as **antipsychotics** (the schizophrenia–low-glutamate link). **Glutamate** clearance runs on EAAT1–5 (EAAT1/2 mostly glial); blocking them increases excitotoxicity, while enhancing their expression is neuroprotective. **Vesicular transporters**: VMAT2 fills the vesicles of dopamine, noradrenaline, 5-HT and histamine neurones (VMAT1 in neuroendocrine cells); **reserpine blocks VMAT, depleting amines: its tranquillising action follows directly**. VGLUT1–3 fill glutamate vesicles (VGLUT3 sits in amine neurones, glutamate as co-transmitter); VGAT fills GABA vesicles and, surprisingly, also glycine's (no separate vesicular glycine transporter exists).

### 4.3 Neuropeptides: the slow, non-recycled majority

Peptides (3–100 amino acids) are built the slow way: gene → mRNA → precursor polypeptide in the cell body, cut by **prohormone convertases** into active fragments, packaged into large dense-core vesicles, and shipped by axonal transport. One precursor can yield a whole family: **POMC** is processed to ACTH in the anterior pituitary but to α-MSH and β-endorphin in the intermediate lobe; proenkephalin yields multiple met-enkephalins plus leu-enkephalin; prodynorphin yields dynorphin: three opioid genes producing at least **18 endogenous opioid peptides**. The tachykinin gene yields five peptides (including Substance P) by alternative splicing. Peptides are released preferentially during **burst/high-frequency firing**, are *not* recycled (extracellular peptidases terminate them; replenishment waits for axonal transport), and almost always travel **co-localised with a classic transmitter**: GABA/dynorphin in movement pathways, CCK/dopamine in reward, glutamate/Substance P in pain, 5-HT/galanin in the raphe (the basis for galanin ligands as experimental antidepressants). The ACE-inhibitor precedent (prohormone convertase inhibition in hypertension) keeps peptide-drug dreams alive.

### 4.4 Receptors: the two great families

**Ligand-gated ion channels (ionotropic)**: multi-subunit pores (4–5 subunits, each with 4 transmembrane domains) that open to let ions through in milliseconds. Members: AMPA, NMDA and kainate receptors (glutamate), GABA-A (chloride), nicotinic acetylcholine, 5-HT3, P2X (ATP). Glutamate receptors carry allosteric opportunities, **AMPAkines** (procognitive enhancers), the NMDA receptor's Mg²⁺ voltage-dependent block, and its glycine/polyamine modulatory sites as safer enhancement routes. AMPA receptor subunit composition controls Ca²⁺ permeability, tuning **long-term potentiation (LTP)**, the neurophysiological correlate of learning and memory. GABA-A receptors have ≥18 possible subunits, but only ~10 natural assemblies; their allosteric sites bind benzodiazepines, anaesthetics, ethanol and endogenous steroids. Point-mutated mice cracked the benzodiazepine code: **sedation via α1-containing receptors, anxiolysis via α2/α3**, the design brief for future selective benzos; α5-containing receptors may carry alcohol's effects.

**G-protein-coupled receptors (metabotropic, GPCRs)**: single proteins with 7 transmembrane domains, signalling in hundreds of milliseconds to seconds through heterotrimeric **G-proteins** (α, β, γ subunits): **Gs** activates adenylate cyclase (↑cAMP), **Gi/o** inhibits it, **Gq** activates phospholipase C (IP3 + DAG), **G0** couples to Ca²⁺/K⁺ channels. The diversity is staggering; **5-HT has 14 receptor subtypes across 7 families** (13 GPCRs + the 5-HT3 channel); dopamine has D1–D5; glutamate adds mGluR1–8; noradrenaline has α1, α2, β families. Two clinical gems: **α2-adrenoceptors open K⁺ channels on noradrenaline neurones**, reducing noradrenergic firing, which contributes to clonidine's anxiolytic-sedative effects; and **5-HT2A receptors close K⁺ channels on cortical neurones**, increasing their activity, plausibly the psychotomimetic mechanism of LSD.

**GPCR regulation** (how receptors switch off): GRKs phosphorylate the activated receptor, β-arrestins bind, the signal is cut and the receptor internalises. GPCR-interacting proteins (GIPs) traffic receptors: the p11 protein carries 5-HT1B receptors to the membrane; **p11 is reduced in postmortem brain of people who died by suicide**, and p11-knockout mice show a depressive-like phenotype. Small G-proteins (Ras, Rho, Rab) act as molecular switches for vesicle traffic, cytoskeleton, and the MAP-kinase cascades on which trophic factors converge.

**Trojan exceptions**: neurotrophic factors signal through receptor tyrosine kinases (TrkA for NGF, TrkB for BDNF), a third receptor family; steroid hormones skip surface receptors entirely, crossing the membrane to act as transcription factors.

### 4.5 Retrograde messengers: the backwards synapse

**Nitric oxide (NO)**: glutamate activates NMDA receptors on the postsynaptic neurone → calcium-calmodulin activates neuronal NO synthase → NO diffuses *backwards* across the synapse → activates presynaptic guanylate cyclase → cGMP → enhanced transmitter release. NO participates in cognition and synaptic plasticity and can be neuroprotective, but excess NO is neurotoxic, and delivering it to the CNS without peripheral chaos has defeated drug development so far.

**Endocannabinoids**: anandamide and 2-AG are lipid messengers synthesised **on demand** in the postsynaptic neurone, diffuse backwards, and activate **presynaptic CB1 receptors**, suppressing transmitter release. The chapter's memorable line: endocannabinoids are to THC what opioid peptides are to morphine, the brain's own version of the drug. CB1 carries THC's analgesia, appetite stimulation and euphoria; CB2 dominates the immune system. CB1's powerful control of synaptic transmission with limited peripheral distribution makes it a prime target, analgesics and anorectic agents among the possibilities.

### 4.6 Second messengers and the road to the genome

Cascades: GPCR → G-protein → effector enzyme (adenylate cyclase → **cAMP**; phospholipase C → **IP3 + DAG**; guanylate cyclase → **cGMP**) → protein kinases → phosphorylated target proteins → physiological change, opposed by phosphatases and cleared by phosphodiesterases (11 PDE types; caffeine's effects partly reflect PDE inhibition at high doses; the PDE4 inhibitor **rolipram** showed antidepressant promise but intolerable peripheral effects; subtype-selective PDE4 inhibition remains a live drug-design route).

**Lithium's two molecular stories**: it inhibits **inositol-1-monophosphatase**, depleting brain inositol (which crosses the blood-brain barrier poorly) and dampening IP3/DAG signalling, the classic inositol-depletion hypothesis, still unproven; and it inhibits, as valproate also does, **glycogen synthase kinase 3β**, an enzyme with trophic and signalling reach, a focus for lithium-mimetic drug development.

**The delayed-antidepressant cascade (the chapter's synthesis)**: antidepressants raise synaptic amine levels within *hours*, yet clinical benefit takes *weeks*. The proposed bridge: elevated amines → GPCR → cAMP → protein kinase A → **CREB** (a gene transcription factor) → **BDNF expression** → trophic actions (synaptic remodelling, possibly neurogenesis) repairing connectivity damaged by stress and environment. The theory drives current pharmacological strategies, and the chapter flags its honest limits: exactly which molecules translate signalling change into symptom relief remains unknown.

## 5. Why it matters clinically

- **Every drug rationale becomes readable.** SSRI = SERT blockade; methylphenidate/amphetamine = DAT/NET; cocaine = triple transporter blockade; tiagabine = GAT-1; reserpine = VMAT; benzodiazepines = GABA-A allosteric sites; clozapine = D2 + 5-HT2A (among others); clonidine = α2 agonism; lithium = inositol-cycle and GSK3β effects. Mechanism-based prescribing beats recipe-based prescribing.
- **Side effects become predictable from receptors.** Sedation vs anxiolysis of benzodiazepines map onto GABA-A α-subunit composition; LSD's psychotomimetic effect and atypical antipsychotic logic both run through 5-HT2A; clonidine's sedation is α2 physiology.
- **Delay is biology, not failure.** The CREB-BDNF cascade explains why antidepressants need weeks, protecting patients and prescribers from premature abandonment of treatment.
- **Receptor diversity is the future.** Fourteen 5-HT receptors, 5 dopamine receptors, glutamate's NMDA modulatory sites, CB1, galanin receptors, PDE subtypes, p11, each a designed target for subtler drugs than the blunt first generation; and each a candidate gene and biomarker for psychiatric genetic research (the chapter's closing point).
- **Cautions the chapter itself teaches.** The lithium-inositol hypothesis is unproven; NO-based therapy has defeated delivery; neuropeptide drug development is still more promise than product; the antidepressant-cascade knowledge is "far from complete". Teaching uncertainty is teaching science.

## 6. India lens

- **Reserpine: India's gift to neuropsychopharmacology.** Reserpine comes from *Rauwolfia serpentina*, the Indian snakeroot (sarpagandha), used for centuries in Indian medicine for psychosis and hypertension. Its VMAT-blocking, amine-depleting action is the chapter's cleanest one-molecule demonstration that transmitter depletion changes mental state, and it remains a superb teaching case for Indian students (historical use, mechanism, and why it left psychiatric practice: depression and hypotension as dose-limiting effects).
- **Pharmacology teaching at the bedside.** Indian medical students meet DAT, SERT and GABA-A before they meet psychiatry; this note supplies the connective tissue. In postgraduate exams, "mechanism of action" questions on antidepressants, antipsychotics and lithium are answered directly from this chapter's cascade logic.
- **Cost reality (approx 2026).** First-generation mechanism-based drugs (imipramine, reserpine's successors, lithium, diazepam) remain the cheapest options in Indian pharmacies; the note's mechanism map lets a rational prescriber use inexpensive drugs intelligently rather than reach for costly newcomers by default.
- **Research direction.** India's growing pharmacogenomic and biomarker programmes (the chapter's "huge diversity of neurotransmitter-related proteins is a large resource for genetic-risk and biomarker studies") give the note's closing thought local relevance: transporter and receptor gene variants (SERT promoter variants are studied in Indian cohorts) are the bridge from this basic science to personalised psychiatry.

**FAQ**
*"Is depression just a chemical imbalance?"*: No, and this chapter is the honest answer to the slogan: amines are one chapter of a many-chaptered story. Signalling reaches genes, trophic factors and plasticity; stress and environment shape the same machinery. The amine hypothesis explains the drugs we have, not the whole illness.
*"Why did my antidepressant take three weeks to work if it raises serotonin in hours?"*: The fast event is amine elevation; the therapeutic event is likely downstream, gene programmes (CREB, BDNF), remodelled synapses, possibly new neurones. Cascades take time.
*"How can a gas be a neurotransmitter?"*: Nitric oxide isn't stored or vesicle-packed; it's made on demand, diffuses freely (including backwards), and is destroyed as fast as it's made. It breaks every classical rule, which is why it was hard to accept.
*"Why doesn't the brain just make more receptors when a drug blocks them?"*: It often does the opposite: GRKs and β-arrestins desensitise and internalise overstimulated receptors, part of tolerance and adaptation, and a live target for drug design.
*"Is cannabis acting on a system the brain already uses?"*: Yes: THC mimics anandamide at CB1 receptors; the brain's own retrograde signal. That is why its effects (appetite, analgesia, euphoria) feel physiological rather than foreign, and why the system is a target for designed medicines.

**Self-test MCQs**

**Q1.** Amphetamines and many antidepressants act at which molecular sites?
- (a) Postsynaptic G-protein-coupled receptors only
- (b) Plasma-membrane transporters (DAT, NET, SERT)
- (c) Vesicular monoamine transporters
- (d) Mitochondrial monoamine oxidase

**Answer: (b).** DAT, NET and SERT are the plasma-membrane reuptake transporters where stimulants and antidepressants act; VMAT (option c) is reserpine's target, and MAO (option d) is the enzyme inhibited by MAO inhibitors.

**Q2.** Studies with point-mutated mice revealed that diazepam's sedative effect is mediated by which GABA-A receptor population?
- (a) α1-containing receptors
- (b) α2/α3-containing receptors
- (c) α5-containing receptors
- (d) δ-subunit-only receptors

**Answer: (a).** α1 carries sedation; α2/α3 carries anxiolysis, the basis for designing behaviourally selective benzodiazepines. α5 is implicated in alcohol's effects.

**Q3.** A lipid messenger synthesised on demand in the postsynaptic neurone that suppresses presynaptic transmitter release via CB1 receptors is:
- (a) Nitric oxide
- (b) Anandamide
- (c) Substance P
- (d) Brain-derived neurotrophic factor

**Answer: (b).** Anandamide is the retrograde endocannabinoid ("to THC what opiate peptides are to morphine"); NO is the gaseous retrograde messenger acting via cGMP.

**Q4.** The proposed mechanism bridging the hours-scale rise in synaptic amines to the weeks-scale antidepressant effect involves:
- (a) Immediate ion-channel blockade
- (b) GPCR cascades activating CREB and increasing BDNF expression, with trophic remodelling
- (c) Permanent depletion of monoamines
- (d) Acute inhibition of glutamate release

**Answer: (b).** The cascade (amine ↑ → cAMP → PKA → CREB → BDNF → plasticity/neurogenesis) is the chapter's leading theory, flagged as incomplete, not proven.

**Q5.** Reserpine, historically derived from the Indian snakeroot *Rauwolfia serpentina*, produces its amine-depleting effect by blocking:
- (a) Dopamine D2 receptors
- (b) The synaptic vesicular monoamine transporter (VMAT)
- (c) The serotonin transporter SERT
- (d) Monoamine oxidase

**Answer: (b).** VMAT blockade empties vesicular stores of dopamine, noradrenaline and 5-HT, the tranquillising (and eventually depressogenic) consequence.

**Q6.** 5-HT2A receptors closing K⁺ channels on cortical neurones, thereby increasing their activity, is the proposed mechanism underlying:
- (a) Clonidine's sedation
- (b) The psychotomimetic effects of LSD
- (c) Tiagabine's anticonvulsant action
- (d) Benzodiazepine anxiolysis

**Answer: (b).** The 5-HT2A-K⁺-channel story explains LSD; clonidine's sedation is the mirror-image α2-adrenoceptor opening of K⁺ channels on noradrenaline neurones.
