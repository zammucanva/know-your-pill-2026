# Acute & Transient Psychotic Disorders (ATPD)

## 1. In one line + overview

**ATPD = a storm of psychosis that erupts within days to two weeks of a major stress, and clears completely, usually within one to three months, with full return to the previous personality.**

Picture a mind that has functioned well for decades, then shatters dramatically within a fortnight after a trigger: a wedding collapses, a daughter elopes, a man loses his job and his house in the same month. Suspicion, voices, confusion, bizarre behaviour and wild mood swings appear together, terrifying the family. The defining features are the speed of onset, the presence of an identifiable stressor in most cases, the "polymorphic" mixed picture (symptoms changing from hour to hour, paranoid in the morning, grandiose by evening), and above all the **full recovery**. This is the psychosis of the Indian OPD, the emergency night call, the family conference at 2 a.m., and getting the diagnosis right matters enormously, because it decides whether a young person is told "six months of medicine and a full life" or "lifelong schizophrenia treatment".

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Define ATPD using its three time anchors (onset ≤ 2 weeks, full remission, 3-month ceiling)
- Recognise polymorphic symptoms as the signature of the group
- Separate ATPD from the first episode of schizophrenia (the most consequential distinction)
- Explain the concept of "bouffée délirante" and its ICD-11 successor categories
- Use brief psychotic disorder (DSM-5) confidently at the bedside
- State the treatment rule: treat the episode hard, then reassess before committing to long-term labels
- Counsel families that full recovery is expected, without over-promising
- Describe what follow-up looks like and what a relapse means

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** ATPD is diagnosed in roughly 1–4 per 1,000 people per year in ICD-using countries, and it is far commoner in WHO studies of developing countries than in Western samples, the classic WHO determinants-of-outcome study found acute-onset psychoses with full recovery concentrated in India, Nigeria and Sri Lanka. Women outnumber men roughly 2:1. Peak onset is 20–35 years, later than the schizophrenia peak. A substantial minority relapse (recurrence estimates range from about 30–50% over follow-up), but progression to schizophrenia occurs in a minority; the transition risk is one of the strongest arguments for follow-up, not "treat and forget".

**India.** Indian centres report ATPD as a large slice of first-admission psychosis, several times the rates seen in UK or US samples. Explanations are debated: true population differences, cultural shaping of how distress emerges (somatic + dissociative + paranoid mixtures), tighter family networks that bring patients to care within days (making "acute onset" recordable), and possibly lower background rates of the chronic schizophrenia-spectrum diathesis. Postpartum (puerperal) psychosis, the prototypical acute polymorphic psychosis, affects roughly 1–2 per 1,000 deliveries and recurs in a meaningful share of subsequent pregnancies.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**Biological.**
- No single lesion; dopaminergic stress-response mechanisms are presumed, similar to but more labile than schizophrenia.
- Sensitivity to sleep loss: the common "engine" of the acute state.
- Postpartum: abrupt hormonal withdrawal plus immune activation plus sleep deprivation, a perfect storm after delivery.
- Substance triggers: amphetamine, cocaine, high-potency cannabis, steroids, some antimalarials and cold remedies.

**Genetic.**
- Family loading is lower than in schizophrenia; many patients have no family history at all, which itself supports the diagnosis.

**Psychological.**
- Acute catastrophic stress is the classic trigger: sudden loss, humiliation, terror, disaster exposure.
- Premorbid traits: some patients show mild histrionic or paranoid traits; maladaptive coping under shock.

**Social / cultural.**
- Migration, displacement, refugee status.
- Socioeconomic collapse (debt, crop failure, job loss), a recurring Indian pattern.
- Religious festivals, mass trance episodes and possession states sit at the cultural boundary of this category (see Indian practice).

**Indian context.**
- Marriage-related catastrophes (broken engagements, dowry harassment, discovered infertility) are among the most common triggers in Indian series.
- "Possession" presentations (voice change, speaking as a deity or deceased ancestor, resolve within the cultural frame in days) overlap heavily with ATPD, and the boundary is negotiated locally.

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: the pressure cooker.** The brain regulates arousal, sleep and threat-perception as a single circuit. Under sudden, extreme stress, the circuit jams at maximum: sleep collapses, threat-detection locks on, and dream-like, unfiltered thinking spills into waking hours. That is why the symptoms change shape hour to hour; the system is not settled into one fixed delusion, it is boiling. Treatment (medicine + sleep + safety) turns the heat off; the pot cools; contents return to normal.

**Story 2: the postpartum switchboard.** After delivery, hormones drop off a cliff within 72 hours, immune signals surge, and night-time feeding destroys sleep. In a small minority of women, this slams the same stress switch: within two weeks of delivery, an acute polymorphic psychosis erupts. It is an emergency (the risk of self-harm or harm to the baby is real) but treated, it usually clears fully within weeks. Each subsequent delivery re-runs the same wiring with a predictable relapse risk.

**Story 3: sleep as the master key.** In all these storms, sustained sleeplessness both signals and drives the psychosis. Clinicians watch for it on the way in and celebrate it on the way out: the first full night's sleep is often the visible turning point of recovery. Families can be taught this; stop the coffee, stop the arguments, darken the room, give the prescribed medicine on time.

## 7. Symptoms

**The polymorphic core (the classic picture):**
- Delusions that shift: persecutory in the morning, grandiose or religious by evening
- Hallucinations (voices, visions, bodily sensations) appearing briefly then disappearing
- Confusion and disorientation, misidentifying familiar people
- Rapid mood swings: ecstatic, terrified, tearful, raging within hours
- Overwhelming anxiety and inner restlessness
- Behaviour: wandering, shouting, undressing, singing, praying loudly, refusing food, self-harm gestures
- Speech that rushes, stalls, or mixes languages unpredictably

**Onset patterns (ICD-11/ICD-10 successors worth knowing):**
1. **Acute polymorphic, with symptoms of schizophrenia**: the boiling picture settles into stable schizophrenia-like delusions for most of the episode.
2. **Acute polymorphic, without schizophrenic symptoms**: pure storm, shifting throughout.
3. **Acute schizophrenia-like disorder**: stable, non-polymorphic psychotic symptoms with abrupt onset and rapid resolution.
4. **Other acute delusional disorders**: e.g., the intense, self-limited delusion that flares around a specific interpersonal catastrophe.

**DSM-5's "brief psychotic disorder"** is the American cousin: psychotic symptoms 1 day to 1 month, then full return to baseline. With a marked stressor, without a stressor, or with postpartum onset are the three specifiers.

**What you do NOT expect in classic ATPD:** primary negative symptoms, gradual thought disorder as the dominant picture, years of prodromal social decline, those point toward schizophrenia.

## 8. Diagnosis

**The logic in five sentences.** Psychosis begins abruptly (under two weeks from clearly well to fully ill). The picture is polymorphic and/or stress-linked. There is no obvious medical or substance cause. The person recovers completely back to their old self. And all of this stays within the time limits, symptoms fully settle within one month (DSM brief psychotic disorder) or up to three months in the ICD tradition, after which you must rethink the label.

**Workup is mandatory, not optional; one of these is hiding in a notable share of "first ATPDs":**
- Blood: glucose, TFT, LFT, renal, electrolytes, calcium, B12, syphilis/HIV where plausible
- Urine drug screen; ask specifically about steroid tablets, cannabis, methamphetamine, "slimming" or "study" tablets
- In women of childbearing age: pregnancy test; puerperal psychosis and pregnancy-related psychoses are on the differential
- CT/MRI brain if any focal sign, head injury, fever, first seizure, or atypical features
- Malaria and typhoid in India with fever; autoimmune encephalitis (anti-NMDA) in any young patient with a mixed psychotic-movement-seizure picture that does not settle

**Instruments:** document psychosis severity (PANSS or BPRS, named, not reproduced) at intake and weekly; the trajectory of scores is diagnostic gold in ATPD.

### Differential table

| Looks like ATPD | Clue pointing away from ATPD | Clue pointing toward ATPD |
|---|---|---|
| First-episode schizophrenia | Slow prodrome, negative symptoms, stable (not shifting) delusions | Abrupt onset, polymorphic storm, full recovery |
| Schizophreniform disorder | Symptom stability > 1 month without clearing | Resolution within the 1-month DSM window |
| Mania | Sustained euphoria, goal-directed energy, past episodes | Mixed chaos, terror, confusion, rapid cycling within days |
| Substance-induced psychosis | Urine positive / history of use, symptoms persisting in withdrawal | Clean screen, drug-free for months |
| Delirium | Fluctuating consciousness with medical cause; worse at night but cognition-focused | Onset linked to psychosocial catastrophe; orientation intact at peak moments |
| Autoimmune encephalitis | Movement oddities, seizures, autonomic instability, CSF/MRI changes | Normal neuro exam, classic stressor, textbook polymorphic picture |
| Possession/trance states | Confined to ritual contexts, family accepts, no functional collapse | Distress and dysfunction beyond the ritual frame |
| Puerperal psychosis | Onset unrelated to delivery | Onset within 2–4 weeks postpartum |

## 9. Management

**Immediate phase (days 0–14): calm, safe, slept.**
- Hospitalise if: risk to self/others, no reliable home supervision, medical workup incomplete, or puerperal (with baby separated until judged safe).
- An antipsychotic at proper antipsychotic dose (e.g., olanzapine 10–20 mg, risperidone 3–6 mg, haloperidol 5–10 mg with anticholinergic, or IM/short-acting forms in agitation); under-dosing is the classic error; it prolongs the storm.
- Benzodiazepine bridging for sleep and agitation (lorazepam 2–4 mg/day divided); sleep is the master key.
- Treat the trigger context: family meeting, temporary removal from the stressor, crisis social support.

**Consolidation phase (weeks 2–8):** continue the antipsychotic that worked. Expect cognitive softness, mild apathy and anxiety while the mind rebuilds; reassure families explicitly that this is recovery, not a new illness.

**Maintenance phase, the honest conversation at 8–12 weeks:**
- Fully well, single episode, strong stressor, no family history of schizophrenia → taper off medicine over 4–8 weeks with a written relapse drill.
- Recurrent ATPD or postpartum pattern → keep medicine 6–12 months, re-review.
- Any unrecovered symptom persisting beyond the window → re-diagnose (schizophreniform/schizophrenia/bipolar) and adjust.

**Puerperal psychosis:** treat as an emergency; combination of antipsychotic plus mood stabiliser or ECT when severe; prophylaxis (starting an antipsychotic within 48 hours of the next delivery) in women with previous episodes; plan this DURING pregnancy, not after.

*India note: olanzapine 10 mg ≈ ₹120–250/month, risperidone 4 mg ≈ ₹80–200/month, lorazepam ≈ ₹30–80/month (approx 2026). Admissions in district hospitals are low-cost but crowded; a single attendant sleeping on the ward floor is both realistic and useful. ECT availability concentrates in medical college hospitals. Postpartum women in rural areas are frequently labelled "ghost affliction" and treated by faith healers for days before reaching care; the puerperal-psychosis clock matters more than the route in.*

**Psychotherapy:** after the acute phase, processing the stressor, rebuilding self-confidence, sleep hygiene, family psychoeducation with the "full recovery expected, watch for relapse signs" script.

## 10. Indian practice

- **The label is common here; use it confidently.** Indian psychiatrists diagnose ATPD often because the pattern genuinely concentrates here. Do not reflexively "upstage" to schizophrenia; the WHO data backs the better outlook.
- **Stressor categories to ask about by name:** broken marriage/engagement, dowry conflict, exam failure, crop failure and debt, sudden bereavement, workplace humiliation, and dowry-related harassment. Families often hide the trigger from the record; privacy-aware phrasing ("was there a shock or a loss in the family before this began?") works better than a checklist tone.
- **Possession presentations.** A household may have already consulted a healer and may continue to do so. Where symptoms stay within ritual bounds and resolve, cultural framing may be enough; where there is distress, starvation, self-harm or months of dysfunction, steer to medical care. Never ridicule the family's beliefs: ally with them.
- **Ward realities.** District and medical-college hospitals manage most cases; family provides nursing, food and supervision. Suggest one designated decision-maker (usually the patient's parent or spouse's senior) to avoid the crossfire of a large joint family issuing conflicting instructions.
- **Postpartum pathway.** Anganwadi workers and ASHAs see the mother at home; train-friendly messaging ("new mother talking strangely, not sleeping, within a month of delivery = emergency, bring her the same day") catches puerperal psychosis earlier than any clinic can.
- **Medico-legal note.** During the episode, decision-making capacity is impaired; admission and treatment follow the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 provisions for supported admission in emergencies with review mechanisms (see the Schizophrenia note's section for the framework).

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: the broken engagement.** A 27-year-old teacher in Indore became acutely disturbed eight days after her engagement was dissolved amid public recrimination. She stopped sleeping, guarded the door, said men in a white car were following her, spoke to her deceased grandmother, and by evening declared she had "divine sight". Her mother brought her to the emergency ward on day 3 of the illness. Urine drug screen negative; pregnancy test negative; physical exam and basic labs normal. Olanzapine 15 mg nocte plus lorazepam for two nights; she slept 11 hours and awoke oriented, embarrassed, partially insight-able. By week 3, only mild anxiety remained; by week 8 she was back at school. Medicine was tapered over six weeks with a written relapse drill (early insomnia, "cars following me" thoughts, appetite loss). At one year she remained well. Teaching points: (1) polymorphic storm + abrupt onset + stressor = ATPD until proven otherwise; (2) the first long sleep often marks the turn; (3) full-dose antipsychotics shorten the episode; (4) a written, specific relapse drill beats a generic "come back if unwell".

**Case 2: the tenth-day mother.** A 24-year-old first mother in a village near Nagpur delivered a healthy boy at home. From day 8 she slept under two hours a night, on day 10 she was found speaking in a male voice, refusing to hold the baby, saying the child was "not hers but sent to test her", and twice tried to walk to the river at night. The ASHA worker flagged it; the family first took her to a temple. On day 13 she reached the district hospital: she was awake, terrified, misidentifying her husband, with rapid mood shifts and fleeting persecutory ideas. Diagnosis: puerperal psychosis, acute polymorphic type. She was admitted with the baby kept with relatives and brought for supervised feeding once she stabilised; olanzapine plus lorazepam, ECT was available if needed but not required. Full remission by week 5. Plan recorded for future pregnancies: prophylactic antipsychinic within 48 hours of the next delivery, plus a pre-conception counselling visit. Teaching points: (1) onset within 2 weeks postpartum is the signature; (2) safety of the baby comes before bonding rhetoric; (3) prophylaxis planning happens during the NEXT pregnancy's booking visit, write it in the antenatal card.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way patients ask it

**"The doctors say this is not schizophrenia. What is it then?"** It is a short, sharp psychotic illness, your mind's circuit breaker tripped after a shock. It looks dramatic, it is treatable, and the expectation is a complete return to who you were, usually within weeks to a couple of months.

**"Is my daughter going mad permanently?"** Everything about the picture (sudden start after a stress, changing symptoms, her previous normal personality) points to a full recovery, and most people with this condition do recover completely. The follow-up matters: a small group relapses or later shows a different pattern, which is why we keep reviews even when everything is fine.

**"Why did the doctor stop the medicine after three months when my neighbour takes it for life?"** Your neighbour most likely has a different, ongoing condition. In this illness, once you have been fully well for a stretch, controlled tapering is standard, with a clear plan for restarting at the first warning signs.

**"Did the shock really cause this?"** A severe shock is usually the trigger that lights the match, but the match and the tinder vary between people. Some people survive worse and stay well; a few break after lighter stresses. Blaming the family (or the patient's "weakness") helps nobody.

**"Was it the ghost, or the nerves?"** Families ask this directly. Honest answer: we treat the brain's reaction, whatever language you use for the trigger. If prayer brings the family peace and the patient sleeps, both can coexist with the medicine.

**"She is fine now but keeps asking about the things she said and did. How do we respond?"** Shame after the episode is almost universal. Answer plainly: "your brain was ill and it said things that were not you; we do not hold a fever's delirium against a person." If the shame deepens rather than fades, bring it to review; post-psychotic depression is real.

**"Will it come back when she marries/has a baby?"** A future major stress or a delivery can re-trigger it in some people. That is not a reason to avoid life; it is a reason for a plan, early warning signs, a contact number, and for pregnancy, preventive medicine that we start the moment the baby is born.

**"Can she write her exams next month?"** If cognition and sleep have normalised, gently graded return, a mock exam at home first, is better than either forcing or shielding. Absence for a full year is sometimes necessary, but premature cave-dwelling is its own injury.

**"Do we need a CT scan / all these blood tests if she already looks better?"** The bloods, urine test and any scans are to make sure the storm was not the surface of something medical, thyroid, infection, drugs, brain inflammation. Finding nothing is the good result, and it is not wasted money.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**Time anchors to memorise:**
- Onset to full psychosis: **≤ 2 weeks**
- DSM-5 brief psychotic disorder duration: **1 day–1 month**, full return
- ICD total course ceiling: **~3 months**, then re-diagnose

**Mnemonic for workup in any "ATPD": "PSYCH-CHECK"**: Pregnancy test, Substance screen (incl. steroids), Y thyroid (TFT), Check sugars, Head imaging if red flag, Encephalitis markers if odd, Cultures if fever, Kidney/liver/electrolytes.

**High-yield facts:**
- Female preponderance ~2:1; age 20–35; low family loading vs schizophrenia.
- Commoner in developing countries per WHO determinants-of-outcome study.
- Puerperal psychosis = prototypical acute polymorphic psychosis; recurrence risk in later pregnancies is meaningful; plan prophylaxis antenatally.
- Polymorphic = symptoms change type within hours/days; stable delusion for weeks points elsewhere.
- Transition to schizophrenia occurs in a minority; the number quoted in exams is often around 15–20% from brief-psychosis cohorts, but varies by study and setting.

**Classic traps:**
1. Diagnosing schizophrenia at first contact because the family "looks like a schizophrenia family"; follow the time rules.
2. Missing steroid-/stimulant-induced psychosis because "she never touched drugs".
3. Missing pregnancy in the "acute psychosis in a young woman".
4. Treating with sub-antipsychotic doses "because it is only brief", this prolongs episodes.
5. Forgetting anti-NMDA encephalitis in a young woman with psychosis plus orofacial movements or autonomic swings.

**Indian exam corner:** previous viva questions include "differentiate ATPD from schizophrenia (five points)", "classify ICD acute psychoses", "discuss puerperal psychosis management with reference to the Mental Healthcare Act", and "cultural presentation of possession states, when is it illness?". The WHO cross-cultural finding is a favourite theory question.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. A 26-year-old man becomes fully psychotic over 5 days after a catastrophic workplace accident, with shifting delusions and mood swings. He is completely back to normal by day 20. Best DSM-5 label:**
A. Schizophrenia
B. Brief psychotic disorder
C. Schizophreniform disorder
D. Delusional disorder
**Answer: B.** Duration 1 day–1 month with full return to premorbid function = brief psychotic disorder.

**2. The "polymorphic" in ICD-11 ATPD refers to:**
A. Multiple personalities
B. Symptom type changing within hours to days
C. Symptoms present in multiple family members
D. Both psychotic and seizure symptoms
**Answer: B.** Rapidly shifting delusions, mood, and hallucination types, the boiling-pot picture.

**3. Puerperal psychosis classically begins:**
A. Within 48 hours of conception
B. In the third trimester
C. Within the first 2 weeks postpartum
D. After weaning
**Answer: C.** Abrupt onset in the first two weeks after delivery; it is a psychiatric emergency.

**4. A 22-year-old woman with "ATPD" is not settling after 4 weeks of full-dose treatment; new orofacial movements and seizures appear. Most urgent consideration:**
A. Raise the antipsychotic
B. Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis workup
C. Start lithium
D. Family therapy
**Answer: B.** Refractory course plus movement/seizure/autonomic signs should trigger autoimmune encephalitis investigation (CSF antibodies, MRI, EEG).

**5. After a single, fully resolved stressor-linked ATPD with no family history, the standard plan is:**
A. Lifelong antipsychotic
B. 6–12 months antipsychotic minimum, always
C. Taper off medication with a written relapse drill
D. Antidepressant monotherapy
**Answer: C.** Controlled withdrawal with explicit relapse education; longer cover is for recurrence, postpartum pattern, or incomplete recovery.

**6. The WHO determinants-of-outcome study found acute-onset psychoses with full recovery to be:**
A. Equally distributed worldwide
B. Concentrated in developed countries
C. More common in developing countries such as India and Nigeria
D. A Western diagnostic fiction
**Answer: C.** The cross-cultural concentration of acute, stress-linked, fully remitting psychoses is one of psychiatry's most replicated geography findings.
