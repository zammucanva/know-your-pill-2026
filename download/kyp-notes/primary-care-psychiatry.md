# Psychiatry in Primary Care

## 1. In one line + overview

**The great majority of mental illness in India will never meet a psychiatrist; it will meet a primary-care clinician for five minutes with a complaint of gas, burning, weakness or sleeplessness, or it will meet nobody at all; primary-care psychiatry is the discipline of making that five minutes count: recognise the common disorders, treat them safely with first-line tools, hold the dangerous ones, and know the referral line.**

The numbers frame everything. The National Mental Health Survey found roughly one in seven adult Indians living with a diagnosable mental disorder, and the overwhelming majority of them (for common depression and anxiety, in the range of 80 to 95 per cent) receiving no care at all; India's psychiatrists number some 0.3–0.75 per 100,000 people (registered active practitioners at the lower end of that range), a ratio arithmetic no realistic hiring plan can fix this generation. Yet the conditions that fill this gap are exactly the ones a trained MBBS prescriber can treat well: depression, the anxiety disorders, alcohol and tobacco problems, insomnia, somatic symptom presentations, first-detected psychosis awaiting referral, dementia's behavioural crises. The evidence from every continent, including Indian trials, is consistent: when primary care is trained, supported by a supervising specialist, and equipped with a short list of safe medicines, outcomes for common mental disorders approach specialist outcomes at a fraction of the cost. This note is the field kit: what to detect, what to treat, what to refer, how to do it in minutes, and how India's programmes (DMHP, Tele-MANAS, mhGAP training) are built to help the clinician at the frontline do exactly this.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- State the treatment-gap arithmetic for India and its clinical consequence: the primary-care clinician is the mental-health system
- Detect depression and anxiety through the somatic front door with brief screening (two-question screens; named instruments), and avoid the two classic detection errors (missing masked depression; over-diagnosing "stress")
- Prescribe first-line antidepressant and anxiolytic-free management safely: SSRI choice, dose, duration, review rhythm, and the specific cautions (adolescents, pregnancy, elderly, interactions)
- Deliver the four brief interventions that fit a consultation: behavioural activation, sleep instruction, worry-time, alcohol brief intervention
- Apply the referral red-flag list: psychosis, mania, high suicide risk, complex withdrawal, perinatal, childhood, treatment resistance, diagnostic doubt
- Describe the Indian support architecture: DMHP's district team and essential-drug list, Tele-MANAS 14416 and tele-psychiatry (e-Sanjeevani), mhGAP-IG training, ASHA/ANM roles, the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 duties
- Handle the somatic symptom presentation without endless investigation or dismissal
- Document and follow up with the rhythm that makes brief care effective: the 2–4 week review, the 6–9 month continuation rule, the relapse-drill handover

## 4. The core ideas

### Idea 1, The arithmetic: the primary-care clinician IS the mental-health system

Specialist-to-population ratios in India range around one psychiatrist per 100,000-plus people, with clinical psychologists and psychiatric social workers even scarcer, concentrated in metros and medical colleges. The National Mental Health Survey (2015–16) put common mental disorders near 10% of adults with an 80–92% treatment gap, alcohol use disorders and severe mental illness similarly vast in their gaps, and every subsequent District Mental Health Programme expansion has faced the same denominator. The conclusion is not despair but design: the World Health Organization's mhGAP programme exists precisely because the global answer to this arithmetic is task-shared primary care. Indian trials at the primary level, lay counsellors in Goa (the MANAS trials and colleagues), depression care in Karnataka and Assam clinics, the NMHS-linked programme evaluations, demonstrate the same direction: brief training, a supervising specialist, and safe drug supply produce measurable recovery at population scale. Every clinician who treats one detected case well is the programme.

### Idea 2, Detection: reading the somatic front door

The Indian primary-care psychiatric patient rarely opens with mood. The presenting sentences are "gas problem, saab", "burning all over", "no strength", "sleep is not coming", "palpitations; get an ECG", "my head becomes blank". The detection discipline has three moves:

1. **The door-question**: for any repeated, multiple, or unexplained physical complaint with a normal examination (especially with sleep change, appetite change, fatigue or recent loss) ask the mood-and-interest pair in plain speech: "In the last two weeks, have you been feeling low, sad, or hopeless most days?" and "Have you lost interest or pleasure in things you usually enjoy?" Two yeses is a depression signal meriting fuller check (screening and severity instruments like the PHQ-9 are named and available; the PHQ family is public-domain; use them as the quantified tier). Sensitivity in Indian validation studies is strong; the screen's main enemy is that nobody asks.
2. **The company it keeps**: early-morning waking, weight change, "everything feels like a burden", tearfulness in the consultation itself, slowed answers; the examination of mood is the consultation's own behaviour.
3. **The error-pairs to avoid**: (a) masking missed, treating the "acidity" for a year while the depression compounds; (b) the "stress/weakness" label applied to everything, the diagnosis of tension anaesthetises further inquiry; (c) missed mania in the irritable energetic patient who "has stopped complaining and become confident"; (d) alcohol read as habit, never illness.

Anxiety, insomnia and their somatic fleet are detected with the same history discipline; the Panic note, GAD note and Insomnia note carry the full method; the primary-care version: persistent worry-plus-body-symptoms, and the three-question sleep map (time to bed, time to sleep, time awake).

### Idea 3, The treatment kit: what to prescribe, and what not to

**Pharmacology, short list, long discipline:**

- **First-line antidepressant: an SSRI**, sertraline or escitalopram as the standard Indian choices (fluoxetine for the lethargic; paroxetine generally avoided for its withdrawal and interaction profile). Start at a standard low dose, titrate to an adequate dose within a week or two if tolerated. The two laws: **adequate dose and adequate duration**; sub-therapeutic dosing "for mildness" and discontinuation at first improvement are the twin failures of primary-care prescribing. Review at 2–4 weeks for early response, 6–8 weeks for full judgment, and **continue 6–9 months (minimum) after remission**; for second or third episodes, the maintenance arithmetic lengthens (see Depressive Disorders note).
- **Anxiolytic discipline**: benzodiazepines only as a short bridge (1–2 weeks) in distressing anxiety or insomnia while the SSRI builds; the long-term benzo prescription is the single commonest iatrogenic pathway running through Indian primary care (see the Benzodiazepine Misuse note). Non-addicting alternatives for sleep: low-dose sedating antidepressants where suitable (trazodone, mirtazapine; amitriptyline in low dose with its TCA cautions); ramelteon/melatonin as the non-sedating chronobiotic tier (availability varies).
- **The alcohol and tobacco package**: brief intervention (the five-minute structured conversation: feedback, responsibility, advice, menu, empathy, follow-up, the FRAMES logic in plain delivery) for hazardous drinking; nicotine patch/varenicline/bupropion where available (see the Nicotine Dependence note); thiamine in the malnourished drinker.
- **Psychosis and bipolar first-contact management**: the primary-care task is recognition, safe initiation or continuation of a single antipsychotic (risperidone up to 3–4 mg or olanzapine up to 10 mg as the classic frontline doses while awaiting specialist review), avoidance of multiple-agent cocktails, depot continuation logistics, and linkage, not long-distance complex polypharmacy.
- **Elderly cautions at the frontline**: SSRI hyponatraemia and falls, TCA avoidance, anticholinergic load, delirium recognition before any new sedative.

**The four brief interventions that fit minutes** (delivered as instructions, not referrals):

1. **Behavioural activation** ("three small scheduled activities per week, two pleasant one necessary, written on the calendar, done badly rather than perfectly") the best evidence-per-minute of any depression treatment in primary care.
2. **The sleep instruction**, stimulus control in three sentences: bed for sleep only; same rise-time daily; 20-minute wake rule with a boring chair (the Insomnia note's full CBT-I belongs to the counsellor tier).
3. **Worry-time and the uncertainty note**: a daily 20-minute appointment with the worry itself, written, contained (the GAD note's package compressed).
4. **The alcohol brief conversation**: honest units-and-consequences feedback plus one clear recommendation and a follow-up date.

**Psychoeducation, the cheapest item on the list**: the illness-is-treatable, medication-needs-time-and-continuity, relapse-early-warning talk, given to patient and one family member in the same consultation, the Indian primary-care version of family intervention's first rung.

### Idea 4, The referral line: what to send, what to hold

Hold in primary care: mild-to-moderate depression and anxiety, insomnia, adjustment states, stable maintained psychosis and bipolar (with specialist back-up and a supply line), dementia's routine follow-up, nicotine and uncomplicated alcohol problems, somatic symptom presentations (the chronicity tier).

**Send to the specialist tier** (DMHP district psychiatrist, Tele-MANAS escalation, medical college), the red-flag list:

- **Suicide risk beyond brief**: expressed intent, plan, means, or a history of attempt; all get immediate assessment, safety-planning and means discussion, not a routine appointment (see the Suicide note; Tele-MANAS card 14416 for the interim).
- **First-episode or suspected psychosis**; any new-onset mania.
- **Depression with psychotic features, catatonia, refusal of food and drink**; think ECT early, not late.
- **Treatment non-response** after two adequate trials; diagnostic uncertainty.
- **Pregnancy, postpartum and perinatal psychiatric presentations** (specialist-led balancing).
- **Children and adolescents** (assessment pathway via child services where they exist; DMHP psychologist otherwise).
- **Complex or polysubstance withdrawal, delirium tremens risk, opioid dependence needing agonist treatment** (de-addiction centre).
- **Dementia with unexplained rapid decline** (reversible-cause workup) or unmanageable BPSD (see Dementia Management).
- **Severe self-harm, eating disorder with physical compromise, severe OCD**, the specialist-tier conditions detected in primary care (the respective notes carry thresholds).

The referral craft in India: give the family one named route with a date, not a letter; "Tele-MANAS now on speakerphone" beats an opd slip that may never be redeemed; the DMHP psychiatrist's visiting day and the district hospital's clinic day are the district's mental-health calendar and belong on your wall.

### Idea 5, The collaborative architecture: nobody is expected to do this alone

The model that moves primary-care outcomes to specialist-level is **collaborative care** (the Cochrane-backed, decade-replicated design): a care manager who follows patients on a registry and does the psychosocial work; a supervising psychiatrist who reviews the caseload and guides treatment changes without seeing every patient; and stepped escalation for non-responders. India's translation of this machinery:

- **The DMHP district team**: the programme's psychologist, psychiatric social worker and visiting psychiatrist attached to the district hospital, extending training and clinic days to blocks; the collaborative tier as infrastructure (programme quality varies by district; the clinician's local network map of who actually answers is worth more than the scheme brochure).
- **Tele-MANAS (14416)**: since 2022 the national tele-mental-health service, trained counsellors first, with psychiatrist and clinical-psychology escalation tiers, in over twenty languages; the primary-care clinician's uses: interim risk handling, advice, family guidance, and the patient's own helpline to carry home.
- **e-Sanjeevani and state tele-clinics**: video consults (including specialist) from the PHC itself; prescription logistics through the PHC pharmacy.
- **mhGAP-IG (Intervention Guide)**: WHO's non-specialist decision aid, disorder flow-charts, priority conditions, "do/do not" cards; the training curriculum DMHP uses; the single document that best operationalises this entire note.
- **ASHA and ANM/MPW tier**: household identification of psychosis, disability and perinatal depression in the field; medication accompany-and-observe; they will bring the missed cases to you.
- **Essential drug supply**: the PHC list at its best carries sertraline (or escitalopram), amitriptyline, risperidone, buprenorphine (in designated centres), thiamine, and now sometimes the depots; the realistic frontliner stocks the gap privately, a week's bridge supply with the district referral note costs less than one abandoned round-trip.

**The Mental Healthcare Act 2017 duties that touch the frontline**: no discrimination in care provision; informed consent standards for admission and treatment; the prohibition on chaining and the duty to report inhumane treatment; attempt-to-suicide decriminalised (Section 115): the casualty ward's duty is treatment, not police. The law's humane architecture is, at the block level, mostly delivered by clinicians who know it exists.

### Idea 6, The somatic presentation: the highest-volume, highest-art primary-care skill

A large fraction of Indian primary-care consultations are medically unexplained or only partly explained symptoms, the gas, the burning, the multiple migrating pains with normal reports. The evidence-based middle path (neither endless investigation nor dismissal; both of which actively worsen outcomes in the somatic literature):

1. **Take the symptom seriously and examine properly, once**; establish the negative findings explicitly.
2. **Name the condition in functional, non-pejorative terms**: "the nerves' sensitivity has been turned up; the pain is real, the machine is sound"; connect openly to the tension-sleep-mood axis (the Somatic Symptom tier lives in the missing Parts 6–7 of the source book; this note writes the primary-care frame from general evidence).
3. **Schedule regular brief reviews** rather than on-demand with new tests; containment of the testing loop is the treatment's spine.
4. **Treat the depression or anxiety riding with it** if present, and the insomnia always.
5. **Screen for the red flags** that do warrant fresh workup (weight loss, night symptoms, neurological signs, age-appropriate cancer screening) and then honestly say they are not there.

The Goa/Vellore trials' demonstration (that reattribution-style brief interventions and antidepressant treatment for the somatic-plus-depression cohort reduce both symptom counts and consultation rates) is the proof that this art is teachable and worth learning.

## 5. Why it matters clinically

- **The gap closes at the five-minute level or not at all**: no realistic specialist hiring plan touches the denominator; every detected-and-treated case in primary care is the programme working.
- **The two-question screen is the highest-yield thirty seconds in Indian medicine**: applied to the recurring "gas and weakness" queue, it converts consultation-years into detected depression.
- **Prescribing laws, not habits**: adequate dose, adequate duration, scheduled review, benzo discipline; four habits that would prevent most of the treatment failures and iatrogenic pathways the specialist tier later inherits.
- **The referral letter is a system, not a paper**: one named route with a date, a phone bridge (Tele-MANAS on speaker), and a bridge-supply of medication where supply is uncertain.
- **Your prescribing sends public signals**: the clinician who says "this is an illness like blood pressure, treatable, not a weakness" at the PHC counter does anti-stigma work at population scale; the one who says "stop thinking, take rest" legislates the opposite.
- **Perinatal depression is the highest-leverage detection in the system**: mother-and-child outcomes ride on it; every ANM-contact, every immunisation clinic is a screen-and-treat opportunity (the Depressive Disorders and Paediatric Mood notes carry the protocol).

## 6. India lens

- **The pathway runs through everything else first**: the average Indian family's help-seeking chain passes the temple, the tantric or the faith-healer, the local RMP, the pharmacist (the country's true first prescriber, alprazolam over the counter where enforcement sleeps), the general physician, and only then, after months and expenditures, the mental-health system. Primary-care psychiatry's Indian version therefore includes three diplomatic tasks: respect the faith-healing stage without deferring diagnosis to it (the WHO's classic finding: Indian faith-healers refer psychosis eventually; your alliance determines when); deprescribe the pharmacist's benzo habitually; and convert the RMP next door from competitor to referral ally.
- **The programme reality**: the District Mental Health Programme (India's flagship since the 1990s, now nominally covering 700-plus districts) delivers training days, a district team, clinic days and free medicines where it functions; its honest weaknesses (post vacancies, drug stock-outs, supervision gaps) are the daily weather of district practice. The clinician's work-around discipline: know the actual humans (which psychiatrist answers, which counsellor is present), use Tele-MANAS for the gaps, and document caseloads that make the vacancy visible.
- **Costs at the counter (approx 2026, generic, vary by state and scheme)**: sertraline 50–100 mg a month, roughly ₹40–150; escitalopram 10 mg, ₹60–200; amitriptyline (the eternal PHC stock), ₹15–40 monthly; risperidone 2–4 mg, ₹60–180; buprenorphine-naloxone through designated centres, near-free to subsidised; counselling at government/DMHP tier free; private brief-counselling ₹300–800 per session in towns, more in metros. Under PM-JAY and state schemes the inpatient and some day-care tiers are covered; the outpatient drug cost remains the family's usual burden; one reason continuation rates follow the cheapest generic, and one reason prescribing the affordable brand in writing is itself a mental-health intervention.
- **Language, idioms and the local lexicon**: the frontal presentation is local ("tension," "nervous weakness," "gas," "chakkar," "mann nahi lagta") the clinician who can say the patient's idiom before translating it to the chart detects earlier and prescribes with more authority.
- **The cadre of the future is being trained now**: the BSc/MSc community-psychology and counselling cadres, the Tele-MANAS counsellor workforce, the ASHA perinatal screening pilots; India is mid-experiment in task-sharing at a scale no country has run; the primary-care clinician who learns to supervise and deploy this tier multiplies several-fold. The resistance to guard against: the reflex to treat psychological hands as lesser hands, the trials (Goa lay-counselling for depression; community workers for psychosis and dementia disability) already settled the outcome question in the cadres' favour.
- **The exam corner**: Indian postgraduate and FMG questions on primary-care psychiatry are anchored on the NMHS treatment-gap numbers, the DMHP/Tele-MANAS architecture, the mhGAP priority conditions, two-question screening, and the first-line/second-line antidepressant logic with safe-prescribing cautions (the viva favourite: "Sketch your district's mental-health plan with the available human resources") this note is the answer sheet.

**FAQs (in patients' and families' words):**
- *"Doctor, why are you giving a stomach-patient a mood tablet?"*: Because the body's reports show the machine is sound, and the wiring that carries the complaints has been sensitised, this tablet turns the sensitivity down; the gas is real, and it will go the same way it came, through the nerves.
- *"Is this medicine addictive, saab?"*: This one, no: antidepressants are not habit-forming; they need time (2–4 weeks) and they need to be continued months after you feel well, then stopped gradually; that is not addiction, that is completing the course, like a bone set and then exercised.
- *"You gave the same tablet my neighbour takes, she is a mental case."*: She has the same chemistry: this medicine is for an illness, not an identity; so is blood-pressure medicine, and half the village is on it without a village whisper.
- *"Can I not just take the sleeping tablet? It works in one night."*: For a night or two, in crisis, yes; the tablet that fixes sleep permanently teaches the body to sleep without any tablet, and the nightly one quietly takes over the job. Two weeks, then we take it back.
- *"We have no psychiatrist within a hundred kilometres."*: We have three roads: the district team's clinic day, Tele-MANAS on your phone now, and me; for the common ones, this is enough; for the serious ones, these roads run.
- *"The temple healer said it is a spirit and gave this thread."*: Keep the thread; it harms nothing and may hold you steady while we work. But the voice and the fears are an illness of the brain's chemistry, the thread and the tablet together, then we watch together which one the illness respects.
- *"How long must he take this risperidone?"*: Long enough that the illness learns it cannot return; the first episode needs at least a year of full recovery before any taper discussion, and that decision belongs with the specialist visit we have booked; missing doses between now and then is the one thing that resets the clock.
- *"He attempted once, should I inform the police?"*: No: the law since 2017 says this is a health matter, not a crime; our job is treatment and keeping the means away, and the helpline number on this card, 14416, is staffed day and night for exactly this.

**Self-test MCQs:**

**1. The recurring "gas and burning" patient, third visit, all reports normal. The single highest-yield next step in an Indian PHC:**
A. Upper GI endoscopy: complete the set
B. The two-question mood-and-interest screen, taken in the patient's own idiom
C. A trial of a proton-pump inhibitor at double dose
D. Referral to the medical college's gastroenterology OPD
**Answer: B.** The unexplained-repeat queue is where the treatment gap hides; thirty seconds of mood questions outperforms the next investigation on every measure.

**2. Correct SSRI discipline in primary care for a first-episode moderate depression, detected and started this week:**
A. Half the standard dose "for mildness," stop at first improvement
B. Adequate dose, review at 2–4 weeks, judge at 6–8 weeks, continue at least 6–9 months after remission, taper slowly
C. Add alprazolone nightly for the duration
D. Switch SSRI at week 3 if not fully recovered
**Answer: B.** Under-dosing and early stopping are the twin failures; switching before 6–8 weeks wastes both drugs and confidence.

**3. The red flag that changes the plan immediately rather than scheduling a routine referral:**
A. First panic attack in a 30-year-old
B. A stable maintained-psychosis patient for routine refill
C. Expressed suicidal intent with a plan and access to means
D. Mild OCD with washing rituals
**Answer: C.** Risk means now: safety-plan, means discussion, Tele-MANAS (14416) involvement and an emergency psychiatric assessment; the other three are routine-pathway conditions.

**4. The collaborative-care engine's three moving parts, as India translates them:**
A. Psychiatrist on-site daily; psychologist for all; free universal transport
B. Care manager/registry (DMHP worker or counsellor), supervising specialist caseload review (district psychiatrist/tele-consult), stepped escalation (Tele-MANAS/medical-college referral)
C. ASHAs prescribing antidepressants independently
D. Pharmacists dispensing SSRIs without prescription nationwide
**Answer: B.** The Cochrane-backed architecture, translated: follow-up hands, supervising eyes, escalation routes.

**5. The somatic presentation's evidence-backed middle path rejects both:**
A. Antidepressants and psychotherapy
B. The endless-testing reflex (fear-driven investigation loops) and the dismissal reflex ("nothing is wrong with you")
C. Specialist referral and primary care
D. Sleep inquiry and family involvement
**Answer: B.** Both classic errors worsen outcomes in the somatic literature; the middle path is named-function explanation, scheduled brief reviews, red-flag screening, and treatment of the comorbid axis.

**6. The Mental Healthcare Act 2017's instruction to the casualty officer receiving a person after a suicide attempt:**
A. File a police report before treatment
B. Presume severe stress; the attempt is decriminalised (s.115); the duty is treatment, protection of dignity and mental-health assessment
C. Refuse admission without a magistrate's order
D. Treat only after the family signs a no-liability bond
**Answer: B.** The law changed the casualty ward's reflexes; the officer who knows it treats first and protects the person's rights in every step that follows.
