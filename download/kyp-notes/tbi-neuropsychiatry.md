# Neuropsychiatry of traumatic brain injury

## 1. In one line + overview

**Tagline:** A knock to the head can leave behind an invisible triad (**slowed thinking, changed mood and released temper**) that starts months after the accident, lasts years, and is almost never connected to the crash by the family, the employer or the doctor.

**Overview, in plain words:** India runs one of the world's largest road-traffic injury burdens, and survivors of significant head injury commonly walk out of surgical care "recovered", and then change. The wife says he is not the same man: irritable over nothing, sad without reason, forgetful of instructions, disinhibited at functions, sleeping badly, seizing occasionally. That is post-traumatic brain injury neuropsychiatry: **cognitive, emotional and behavioural sequelae that follow the injury on a timeline from weeks to years**. Three honest truths organise the whole subject. First, **severity predicts only loosely**: a "mild" injury with a bad knock can produce more disability than a severe one. Second, **most symptoms are treatable**; depression, anxiety, sleep, seizures and behavioural overload respond to structured care and careful medicines. Third, **the label is therapy**; connecting the change to the injury converts "he became a bad person" into "his brain was hurt", which rescues marriages, jobs and the patient's self-respect. The psychiatric assessment of any head-injury survivor is therefore as much historical detective work as examination: what hit, how long was memory lost, what has changed since, and what we can rebuild.

## 2. Learning objectives

1. Grade TBI severity using loss-of-consciousness and post-traumatic amnesia, and explain why grading predicts outcome only loosely.
2. Map the post-TBI timeline of neuropsychiatric syndromes: post-concussion syndrome, mood disorders, cognitive impairment, behavioural change, psychosis (rare), dementia risk.
3. Explain the difference between post-traumatic amnesia and ordinary forgetfulness, and why PTA duration matters more than the LOC number.
4. Recognise post-traumatic epilepsy and post-traumatic stress disorder riding together after road accidents.
5. Assemble the assessment: collateral behavioural history, frontal-weighted testing, medicine review, compensation/legal documentation.
6. Manage it: small-dose pharmacology, structure, family education, graded return to work and driving.
7. Address Indian realities: helmet law, compensation claims, litigation distress, employer communication.

## 4. Epidemiology

### Global
- TBI is a leading cause of disability in young adults (peak incidence 15–35, male-predominant); road traffic, falls, sports and assault dominate causes.
- Roughly **10–15% of mild TBI patients** report persistent post-concussion symptoms beyond a year; moderate–severe TBI survivors almost all carry residual cognitive-behavioural deficits.
- Depression prevalence post-TBI is high (about a quarter to a half over follow-up); anxiety disorders and PTSD commonly co-travel with accident trauma; suicide risk is raised.
- Single severe TBI roughly **doubles later dementia risk**; repeated mild impacts (the boxing pathway) carry a distinct degenerative risk (chronic traumatic encephalopathy, a tau pathology; name known mainly from sport).

### India
- Road-traffic injuries are among the top causes of death and disability in the young; two-wheeler riders dominate casualties, helmet use is inconsistent, and traumatic brain injury admissions fill neurosurgical services in every city.
- Follow-up is the Indian failure point: surgical review ends, no neuropsychiatric service exists in most districts, and families absorb the sequelae unlabelled.
- Compensation claims (Motor Vehicles Act tribunals, ESIS, insurance) commonly demand psychiatric documentation; clinical work and medico-legal work arrive together, and the doctor must keep both honest.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

- **Injury mechanics:** acceleration-deceleration shears fibres (diffuse axonal injury), coup-contrecoup contusions bruise the frontal poles and temporal poles, precisely the offices of self-control and memory integration.
- **Biology that predicts poorer outcome:** longer post-traumatic amnesia; deeper coma; early seizures; intracranial bleed; older age at injury; pre-existing learning or psychiatric history; alcohol misuse at the time of injury.
- **Psychological amplifiers:** the accident itself was terrifying (PTSD co-travels); ongoing pain, tinnitus and vertigo maintain arousal; blame, litigation and job loss maintain depression.
- **Social/environmental:** return to unmodified work too early; families who interpret disinhibition as disrespect; no compensation, so treatment competes with wage-earning.
- **Indian context:** two-wheeler and pedestrian vulnerability, night-road injuries with delayed rescue, alcohol-involved crashes, and the near-absence of post-acute neuro-rehabilitation in the public sector.

## 6. What happens in the body/brain

**Story 1: the shaken wiring.** The brain floats in fluid; a sudden stop throws it against the skull's inner ridges and twists it on its own stem. Deep white-matter fibres shear: the brain's long-distance cabling. The injured offices are predictably the **frontal poles** (the manager of manners and plans) and **temporal poles** (the filing room of new memories). That geography is the whole neuropsychiatric phenomenology: disinhibition plus forgetfulness plus slow thinking, in a person whose storage of old knowledge remains.

**Story 2: the overloaded CPU.** After injury, the brain's processing budget shrinks. Ordinary tasks (a noisy shop, a family argument, a work deadline) now cost more than the budget allows, so the system overheats: headache, fatigue, irritability, the urgent need to lie down in a dark room. Families see "laziness"; the clinic sees **fatigability**: the symptom that most shapes return-to-work plans.

**Story 3: the mind re-lives the crash.** For many, the traumatic event itself is not over: it replays intrusively, sleep is broken by nightmares, roads are avoided, hypervigilance keeps the body alarm ringing. PTSD and post-concussion syndrome share symptoms so heavily (sleeplessness, irritability, concentration failure, jumpiness) that the only honest clinical move is to assess both in every accident survivor and treat what you find.

## 7. Symptoms

**Cognitive:**
- Processing speed slowed; attention flickers in noise; multitasking collapses (the "overloaded CPU").
- New-learning difficulty (temporal filing bruised); appointments missed, instructions lost after the second step.
- Executive failure: poor planning, poor judgement with money, difficulty adapting when routines change.

**Emotional:**
- Depression: often biological and reactive at once (loss of job, status, self-image); anhedonia, morning heaviness, hopelessness; **suicide risk genuinely elevated: ask directly**.
- Anxiety, panic in crowds, travel re-experiencing; full PTSD symptom set (intrusions, avoidance, hyperarousal, negative mood) in a substantial minority.
- Irritability and low frustration tolerance: the commonest family complaint; temper explosions disproportionate to triggers.

**Behavioural (frontal-type release):**
- Disinhibition: tactless jokes, overfamiliarity, impulsivity with money or driving; neglect of hygiene; childish or rigid humour.
- Apathy in a subgroup; perseveration (same story, same task, same argument on repeat).
- Sleep-wake reversal, day somnolence; occasionally a state of post-traumatic confusion or wandering.

**Neurological flags to catch in psychiatry clinic:**
- Post-traumatic epilepsy: any odd episodic behaviour (staring, lip-smacking, nocturnal tongue-bite, lost minutes), investigate, and check antiepileptic adherence and interactions.
- Chronic subdural haematoma (elderly, alcohol users, on anticoagulants) presenting as *subacute* personality change or drowsiness weeks after a "trivial" fall; a missed surgical emergency mimicking psychiatric decline.
- Post-traumatic headache, vertigo, tinnitus: pain maintenance amplifies every other symptom.
- **Hypopituitarism** after moderate-severe TBI: fatigue, low mood, sexual dysfunction, weight change, lethargy that mimics depression and does not respond to SSRIs; the under-remembered treatable cause; screen hormone axes in unexplained fatigue after TBI.

## 8. Diagnosis

### The assessment structure
- **The injury interview, in numbers:** duration of loss of consciousness; **duration of post-traumatic amnesia** (the gap between injury and continuous memory, the strongest single predictor; hours worse than minutes, days worse than hours); imaging findings; seizures; time off work.
- **The change inventory (collateral, in private):** temper, humour, patience, money, hygiene, sleep, driving, affection, work quality, before vs after, with a timeline.
- **Frontal-weighted bedside testing:** as in the FTD note, proverb interpretation, go/no-go, alternating sequences, fluency under time; memory-weighted screens under-sell TBI exactly as they under-sell FTD.
- **Mood and PTSD screening** in every accident survivor, the two most treatable riders.
- **Review the drug list:** antiepileptics (sedation, cognitive blunting, especially older agents), pain opioids, benzodiazepines given "for nerves", muscle relaxants.
- **Rule-outs for subacute decline:** chronic subdural (image), hypopituitarism (endocrine screen), hypothyroid, anaemia, alcohol relapse.
- **Documentation for compensation** should be factual and functional: impairments, capacities, prognosis, care needs, written for the tribunal as a clinical document, not an advocacy pamphlet; honesty protects the patient better than inflation.

### Differentiation notes
| Against | Clue |
|---------|------|
| Pre-existing personality disorder | Collateral says the pattern predates the crash |
| Depression alone | Temporal link to injury, disinhibition and fatigue pattern out of proportion |
| PTSD alone | Cognitive slowing and disinhibition are not PTSD core |
| Postictal states | Episodic pattern, tongue-bite history, EEG |
| Chronic subdural | Weeks-delayed drowsiness, headache, focal signs; elderly/anticoagulated |

## 9. Management

### (a) Relabel, then rehabilitate
Explain the shaken-wiring story to the family in the first consultation. The relabelling converts blame ("he has become difficult") into treatment alliance. Then:
- **Structure and pacing:** fixed routines; one instruction at a time; graded sensory load (quiet rooms, short shifts); scheduled rest: fatigue management is the core of cognitive rehabilitation.
- **Graded return to work/school** with written accommodations: reduced hours, written instructions, no multitasking initially, supervision for safety-critical tasks; the employer letter that states "neurological recovery, not misconduct".
- **Driving:** a formal decision, not a family one, seizures, slowed reaction, impulsivity and vision each disqualify until cleared; document.
- **Family programme:** one page on disinhibition ("his brakes are injured, not his values"), anger drill (leave-stay-return), single-speaker rule.
- Formal neuropsychological rehabilitation where available (metro centres), attention retraining, memory strategy building; scarce in the public sector, so teach the family the core techniques themselves.

### (b) Pharmacology: small doses, slow titration, one change at a time
- **Depression:** SSRIs (sertraline, escitalopram) first; the injured brain amplifies side effects: start half-standard doses, titrate slowly; mirtazapine where sleep and appetite need help (watch next-day grogginess).
- **Irritability/explosive anger:** SSRI trial first; then cautious mood-stabiliser option (valproate-type with pregnancy counselling; also useful if epilepsy coexists, one drug, two jobs).
- **Sleep:** sleep hygiene first; melatonin for rhythm; mirtazapine if depression co-travels; avoid long-term benzodiazepines (they tax cognition and balance).
- **Cognition:** no stimulant is licensed for TBI cognition; treat the treatable (sleep, mood, pain, hormones) before concluding the deficit is fixed.
- **Post-traumatic epilepsy:** with neurology; prefer seizure control with the least cognitively sedating agent compatible with the individual.
- Disinhibition severe and dangerous: small-dose quetiapine with fixed review, same caution logic as the dementia notes.

*India note: sertraline and valproate generics are inexpensive (approx ₹50–150/month, 2026). The expensive items are imaging and medico-legal time; the scarcest is rehabilitation; the family-as-therapist teaching programme is the realistic Indian delivery channel.*

### (c) Medico-legal and social work
- MACT/insurance documentation at defined intervals (functional picture, care needs, prognosis); ESIS/workmen's-compensation coordination for employed patients.
- Guard against the two failure modes: symptom inflation under litigation pressure (treat clinical truth, document uncertainty honestly) and symptom dismissal ("compensation neurosis"); most post-TBI suffering is genuine even when money is at stake; assess, don't editorialise.

## 10. Indian practice

- **The pathway today:** neurosurgery → home → (maybe) physiotherapy for limbs → nothing for cognition/behaviour. The family physician and the occasional psychiatrist are the whole service; this note's family programme is the practical substitute for missing rehab infrastructure.
- **Helmet counselling is psychiatry too:** every head-injured two-wheeler survivor who returns to riding without a helmet is a repeat customer; the conversation belongs in the consultation, especially for the young and the drinking.
- **Alcohol junction:** intoxication at the time of injury predicts both worse outcomes and post-injury misuse escalation; screen and address both sides.
- **Employer letters** prevent terminations for "attitude" and unlock accommodation; write them routinely.
- **Caregiving:** spouses carry irritability and disinhibition without understanding; a single psychoeducation session measurably reduces household conflict, cheap and effective.
- **Primary care role:** the three questions (temper since? memory since? sleep since?) at every post-injury follow-up; plus subdural vigilance in the elderly faller on anticoagulants.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: the manager who failed the comeback.** A 34-year-old software manager sustained a "mild" TBI in a car crash: LOC minutes, PTA around 12 hours, CT normal. Two months later, back at full hours, he began shouting in stand-ups, missing deadlines, sleeping badly, avoiding the crash route. His employer warned him; the family wondered "if he had become arrogant". The correct package: relabelling session with wife and HR, graded return (half-days, written tasks), sertraline for the depressive-anxious load, sleep protocol, driving suspended pending assessment. Twelve weeks later he managed full hours with two accommodations; two years later, close to baseline.

*Teaching points: "mild" by LOC can disable by PTA; PTSD-depression-fatigue is the common post-concussion engine; graded return with documentation outperformed any prescription; the family's relabelling rescued the job.*

**Case 2: the sweet grandfather who turned rude.** A 72-year-old on warfarin, three weeks after a "trivial" bathroom slip, became drowsy by day, rude at dinner and unsteady. The family asked for "a psychiatric check for dementia". CT: chronic subdural haematoma. After neurosurgical drainage, he returned to his gentle baseline within days.

*Teaching points: weeks-delayed change after any fall in the elderly, especially anticoagulated, is a surgical emergency impersonating psychiatry; the strongest psychiatric skill here was ordering the scan.*

## 13. FAQ

**"The scan is normal, then why is he different?"**
Wiring injuries (shearing of fibres) often do not show on routine scans; the office manager is hurt even when the building photographs look fine. The functioning (temper, patience, memory) is the real scan.

**"It has been a year. Is this as good as it gets?"**
Much of the visible "slowness" is often the treatable load on top: depression, broken sleep, pain, medicines. Treat those before deciding the floor. And recovery continues for years; the brain keeps adapting if the load is managed.

**"He fights over nothing since the accident. Is he just using it as an excuse?"**
His brakes are injured. Irritability after head injury is as physical as a limp, and it responds to structure and sometimes medicines; blame worsens it, always.

**"Can he drive again?"**
A clinical decision with a checklist: seizures, reaction speed, impulsivity, vision, medicines. Some return in weeks, some must not; the family should never decide this alone.

**"He has nightmares about the accident and avoids that road."**
That is post-traumatic stress, and it travels with head injury constantly. It has specific, effective treatment; ask for it rather than waiting; it also lifts concentration and sleep.

**"The compensation case is pending and he seems worse whenever we go to court."**
Stress genuinely flares the symptoms; the case and the brain feed each other. Keep treating clinically, and keep the documentation factual; honest records help the claim more than exaggeration does.

**"Do head injuries cause dementia later?"**
One severe injury raises the risk meaningfully; repeated mild ones (boxing-type) carry a distinct risk. What one can do: protect from further knocks, treat vascular risks, and monitor; worry is optional, vigilance is not.

**"What are the chances of seizures?"**
Raised after significant injuries, highest in the first two years. Any odd episodic event (staring spells, lost minutes, tongue-bite) must be reported for EEG and treatment decisions.

## 14. Exam & student lens

- **Mnemonics:** the outcome question as **"LOC lies; PTA tells"**; post-TBI syndrome cluster as **"Head, Heart, Hindrance"** (headache/dizziness; mood/PTSD; cognition/behaviour).
- **Highest-yield facts:** PTA duration > LOC as outcome predictor; frontal/temporal pole vulnerability; depression incidence; post-traumatic epilepsy window; chronic subdural in the elderly anticoagulated; hypopituitarism as the treatable mimic.
- **Classic traps:** (1) labelling disinhibition "personality worsening" without a timeline; (2) missing PTSD riding under "post-concussion"; (3) benzodiazepines for anxiety in a cognitive-impairment patient; (4) missing chronic subdural; (5) writing "compensation neurosis" instead of assessing.
- **Viva favourites:** diffuse axonal injury; post-traumatic amnesia grading; second-impact and repeat-injury concept; CTE vs single-injury dementia risk; return-to-play/return-to-work protocols; driving fitness.
- **Indian exam corner:** two-wheeler helmet legislation and counselling; MACT documentation duties; the district-level service gap and family-as-therapist solution; alcohol at both ends of TBI.
- **Rehearse one line:** "After head injury, treat the mood, the sleep, the pain and the load; then see how much brain remains to rehabilitate."

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. The best single early predictor of cognitive outcome after TBI is:**
A. Duration of loss of consciousness
B. Duration of post-traumatic amnesia
C. Skull fracture visible
D. Duration of hospital stay

**Answer: B.** PTA outperforms LOC in predicting outcome across the classic literature.

**2. Three weeks after a fall, an anticoagulated 70-year-old becomes drowsy and disinhibited. First suspicion:**
A. Post-concussion syndrome
B. Chronic subdural haematoma: order urgent imaging
C. Early dementia
D. Depression

**Answer: B.** Weeks-delayed decline in an anticoagulated elderly faller is a neurosurgical emergency impersonating psychiatry.

**3. The mood-PTSD-cognition triad after a road accident is best addressed first by:**
A. Long-term benzodiazepines
B. Screening and treating depression/PTSD with SSRI plus structured pacing
C. Antipsychotics
D. No treatment until compensation concludes

**Answer: B.** SSRIs plus sleep, pacing and graded return; benzodiazepines harm cognition and balance.

**4. Post-TBI "treatment-resistant" fatigue and depression with weight change should prompt:**
A. Doubling the SSRI
B. Endocrine screening (hypopituitarism)
C. ECT
D. A second antipsychotic

**Answer: B.** Post-traumatic hypopituitarism mimics depression and responds to hormone replacement.

**5. Disinhibition after frontal-pole contusion is best first managed with:**
A. Environmental structure and family psychoeducation
B. Immediate high-dose antipsychotic
C. Punishment-based behaviour chart
D. Restraints

**Answer: A.** Structure and education are the first line; drugs enter only for danger or distress that structure cannot hold.

**6. Regarding driving after significant TBI:**
A. The family should decide
B. It is a documented clinical decision covering seizures, reaction, impulsivity and vision
C. All patients can drive after discharge
D. Only patients with seizures need review

**Answer: B.** Driving fitness is a clinical, documented decision, not a family compromise.
