# Neuroendocrinology in Psychiatry — Hormones as Brain Messengers and Markers

## 1. In one line + overview

**The same molecules are hormones in blood and neurotransmitters in brain, and by reading the hypothalamic-pituitary axes (thyroid, adrenal, growth hormone, gonadal, prolactin) psychiatry found its most robust biological findings: HPA-axis hyperactivity and the CRF hypothesis of depression, the thyroid-depression relationship, and the endocrine fingerprint of early adversity.**

The Oxford chapter (Nemeroff & Neigh) begins with the field's founding discovery: neurones can function as true endocrine tissue (the magnocellular vasopressin-oxytocin system of the paraventricular hypothalamus, the posterior-pituitary story, and the hypothalamic releasing-factor system controlling the anterior pituitary), and its immediate dissolution: **the artificial endocrine/neuronal/neuroendocrine demarcations lost their heuristic value**, because the *same substance* acts as a hormone at one site and a neurotransmitter at another (adrenaline in adrenal medulla vs CNS; **CRF as hypothalamic hormone and extrahypothalamic neurotransmitter**). Neuroendocrinology in psychiatry comprises: neural regulation of hormonal secretion; the neurotransmitter regulation of that; hormone effects on the CNS; axis alterations in psychiatric disorders; and the behavioural consequences of endocrinopathies. Its classical research strategy, the **neuroendocrine window**: measuring basal and stimulated pituitary and end-organ hormones in plasma as an index of brain monoamine activity (with its severe limitation honestly stated: the monoamine circuits regulating hormone secretion may not resemble the pathophysiological circuits; especially the dopamine problem, where tuberoinfundibular prolactin regulation is unrelated to mesolimbic schizophrenia circuitry). Then the axis-by-axis psychiatry: **thyroid** (the four grades of hypothyroidism with the depression links, blunted TSH response to TRH in a quarter of depressives, the T3-acceleration and augmentation findings, grade-4 autoimmune thyroiditis in comorbid depression-anxiety); **adrenal** (the CRF hypothesis: HPA hyperactivity as "the most important finding in all of biological psychiatry": cortisol and CSF-CRF hypersecretion, adrenocortical and pituitary enlargement, CRF-1 receptor downregulation, the dexamethasone suppression and combined Dex-CRF tests with trait-marker implications, CRF-antagonist development, hippocampal glucocorticoid damage, early-abuse HPA persistence and the CRF1-polymorphism vulnerability findings); **growth hormone** (the clonidine-blunted GH response: the most consistent affective-disorders finding, trait-persistent, suicide-attempt robustness); **gonadal** (the under-researched axis: oestrogen's possible antidepressant contributions, the leuprolide-chemical-ovariectomy treatment of PMS, testosterone in hypogonadal depressed men); and **prolactin** (the dopamine-inhibited axis; the blunted prolactin response to serotonergic probes in depression and borderline/cluster-B personality, a 5-HT1A story).

## 4. The core ideas

### 4.1 The founding principle and the window strategy

**Neurones as endocrine tissue**: the 1950s controversy (neurohistologists documenting Gomori-positive, granule-containing neurones that looked like endocrine cells) resolved into certainty: magnocellular paraventricular neurones synthesise **vasopressin and oxytocin**, transport them down axons to the posterior pituitary, and release them physiologically (fluid balance; milk-letdown); the hypothalamic releasing and release-inhibiting factors act on anterior-pituitary trophic cells. The demarcations then dissolved: **pleiotropy**, one substance, multiple roles (adrenaline: adrenal hormone and CNS transmitter; CRF: hypothalamic hypophysiotrophic hormone and extrahypothalamic neurotransmitter; TRH likewise with heterogeneous brain distribution and direct brain effects). Neuroendocrinology/psychoneuroendocrinology thus encompasses: neural regulation of peripheral, pituitary and hypothalamic secretion; the neurotransmitter regulation thereof; the CNS effects of every axis's hormones; axis alterations in psychiatric disorders; and the behavioural consequences of endocrinopathies (the discipline institutionalised in the journal and Society of Psychoneuroendocrinology).

**The neuroendocrine window** (1970s-80s strategy, still occasional): before functional imaging, plasma hormones were the accessible brain, basal and stimulated pituitary/end-organ secretion read as an index of monoamine-circuit activity, in the monoamine-theory era. **The assumption**: the monoamine neurones regulating endocrine secretion are disordered (or not) to the same extent as the pathophysiological circuits, plausible for the widely-projecting raphe-serotonin and locus-coeruleus-noradrenaline systems, **implausible for dopamine**: the tuberoinfundibular system (arcuate/periventricular perikarya → median eminence, regulating prolactin) has no reason to co-vary with the mesolimbicocortical pathway (ventral tegmental perikarya → accumbens, amygdala, cortex) implicated in schizophrenia, so **the prolactin-dopamine window tells nothing about limbic dopamine**. The strategy's contribution, despite its failure as a monoamine-assay: the CRF hypothesis of depression, the HPT findings, and the research direction toward PET-ligand measurement of peptide receptors in living brain.

### 4.2 The generic axis architecture

The hypothalamus synthesises release and release-inhibiting peptide factors (transcription of the prohormone DNA → translation in endoplasmic reticulum → processing during axonal transport → packaging in terminal vesicles → release at the median eminence into the primary plexus of the hypothalamo-hypophyseal portal vessels → humoral transport to the adenohypophyseal sinusoids → specific membrane receptors on trophic-hormone cells → release or inhibition of the trophic hormone → end-organ secretion → feedback at pituitary and brain). The exemplar: **GnRH** (a decapeptide) → LH and FSH → oestrogen/progesterone (women), testosterone (men); the exogenous intravenous GnRH stimulation test as a sensitive HPG-axis probe (influenced by GnRH secretion, gonadotrophin secretion, and gonadal-steroid feedback at pituitary and brain).

### 4.3 The axes in psychiatric disorder

**Thyroid (HPT)**. A century's knowledge: adult hypothyroidism's profound CNS disturbance, cognitive impairment and depression. The modern focus: subtle axis alterations in depression. The **four grades of hypothyroidism**: grade 1 (classic primary, raised TSH, low thyroid hormones, exaggerated TRH response); grade 2 (normal hormones, raised basal TSH, exaggerated response); grade 3 (normal basal everything, exaggerated TRH response, detectable only by stimulation test); grade 4 (all tests normal, antithyroid antibodies, symptomless autoimmune thyroiditis), untreated patients progressing from grade 4 to grade 1. **The depression findings**: an inordinately high rate of HPT dysfunction in major depression, with comorbid depression-and-anxiety patients especially showing grade-4 autoimmunity; schizophrenia and anxiety disorders showing normal axes. **Blunted TSH response to TRH in ~25% of depressives** (replicated for 25+ years since Prange and Kastin), mechanism obscure, best accounted by chronic TRH hypersecretion with pituitary TRH-receptor downregulation (with elevated CSF TRH in drug-free depressives as supporting evidence). **TRH as extrahypothalamic transmitter** with reported but unconfirmed intrathecal antidepressant effects. **The treatment literature**: T3 (25–50 μg) both accelerates antidepressant onset and converts non-responders (confirmed in STAR*D); T4 (100–300 μg) supplementation as an augmentation strategy; born of the finding that hypothyroid patients (and animals) respond poorly to antidepressants. Post-ablation patients do better on T3-plus-T4 than T4 alone for mood and cognition (Bunevicius).

**Adrenal (HPA): the CRF story.** CRF chemically identified in 1981 (Vale) after the 1955 crude discovery, opening comprehensive HPA assessment and the scrutiny of the peptide that **coordinates the endocrine, immune, autonomic and behavioural effects of stress**. The central claim: **HPA-axis hyperactivity in a significant subgroup of major depression is the most important finding in all of biological psychiatry**, its magnitude correlating with depression severity; thousands of reports since the Board/Bunney/Hamburg and Carroll/Sachar/Stokes/Besser observations applying Cushing's-diagnostic tests (urinary free cortisol, CSF cortisol, the **dexamethasone suppression test**) to depressives. The anatomy of the finding: cortisol hypersecretion with ACTH hypersecretion (and its co-secreted pro-opiomelanocortin products) → trophic **adrenocortical and pituitary enlargement** (CT and MRI); **CSF-CRF elevation** and postmortem CRF-mRNA hyperexpression, with **CRF-1 receptor downregulation** (binding and mRNA studies, including in suicide victims); the intravenous-CRF test showing **blunted ACTH response** (downregulated corticotrophs and/or cortisol feedback); everything normalising on recovery, and **persistent HPA abnormality (dexamethasone non-suppression or CSF-CRF elevation) predicting poor antidepressant response**. **The combined dexamethasone-CRF test** (Holsboer: dexamethasone one day, standardised CRF stimulation the next) (markedly greater sensitivity, detecting axis alteration in **never-symptomatic first-degree relatives**) raising the trait/vulnerability question. The preclinical wing: intracerebral CRF produces a depressive-anxiety syndrome in animals (decreased libido, appetite and weight loss, sleep disturbance, neophobia), hence **CRF-1 receptor antagonists as a putative antidepressant class** (activity in every preclinical screen; open-trial antidepressant effects; CRF-2 with urocortin ligands as the second receptor story). The **consequences of chronic cortisol**: hippocampal neuronal loss (the glucocorticoid feedback site: MRI-documented) potentially explaining elderly depressives' increasing adrenocortical activity. **The early-adult-life findings**: persistent HPA-axis and extrahypothalamic-CRF hyperactivity after early untoward life events (child abuse and neglect, in rats, primates and humans of both sexes), posited to underlie the abuse-depression vulnerability association, with **CRF-1-receptor SNPs conferring vulnerability or resistance** to depression after abuse, and CRF-antagonist early intervention as the prophylactic speculation. Other disorders: HPA hyperactivity common when depression complicates multiple sclerosis, Alzheimer's, multi-infarct dementia and Huntington's; little evidence in schizophrenia; **PTSD**: elevated CSF-CRF with *normal or reduced* adrenocortical activity (the dissociated pattern); **Alzheimer's disease**: cortical CRF-neurone degeneration (with reciprocal receptor upregulation), temporally preceding the cholinergic involvement.

**Growth hormone (somatotrophic).** The most consistent finding in affective-disorders research: **the blunted GH response to provocative stimuli, particularly clonidine (α2-agonist)**, also to apomorphine, desipramine and levodopa; persisting after recovery in some studies (a **trait marker of depression vulnerability**) and **particularly robust in recent suicide attempters**; basal secretion possibly showing a reduced nocturnal rise. The axis's uniquenesses: dual hypothalamic regulation (somatostatin/GIH inhibitory and GHRH stimulatory, the only axis with unequivocally two physiological hypophysiotrophic hormones); **no single target gland** (somatomedin C from liver; direct bone and muscle effects); somatostatin's wide extrahypothalamic distribution as a CNS transmitter with **marked reduction in Alzheimer's** and elevation in Huntington's basal ganglia; blunted GH-to-GHRH in depressives (smaller database); the postmortem research gap acknowledged. Schizophrenia GH findings confounded by antipsychotic treatment.

**Gonadal (HPG).** The rationale for expecting hypoactivity (the female prevalence of depression, postpartum rates, depression's lost libido), yet a remarkably small database: no basal gonadotrophin differences; small GnRH-test studies (blunted or normal); the field demanding menopausal-status, cycle-phase, contraceptive and baseline-steroid controls. The remarkable exceptions: **leuprolide (GnRH agonist) in premenstrual syndrome**, a chemical ovariectomy via GnRH-receptor downregulation (with bone-density and cardiovascular caveats; add-back oestrogen-progesterone reducing efficacy); oestrogen's possible antidepressant contribution in perimenopausal and postpartum depression; the fluoxetine-response hint in oestrogen-replaced postmenopausal women; **testosterone's antidepressant effect in hypogonadal depressed men**.

**Prolactin.** The unique axis: **a non-peptide release-inhibiting factor (dopamine)**; a prolactin-releasing factor long sought but unisolated (with TRH a candidate physiological PRF). Basal prolactin unaltered in depression mostly (while the TSH-to-TRH blunts, the prolactin-to-TRH does not); the large provocative-test database: **blunted prolactin responses to serotonergic probes** (l-tryptophan, 5-HTP, fenfluramines, clomipramine, direct agonists) in depression **and in cluster-B and borderline personality disorder**, mediated by altered **5-HT1A receptor responsiveness**.

### 4.4 The synthesis and the systemic implications

The window strategy failed at its original monoamine-assay purpose but succeeded beyond it: the CRF hypothesis of depression (multidisciplinary support, novel-therapeutic direction); the mechanism linking early trauma to adult depression; the replicated HPA and HPT findings (most depressives showing alteration in one of the two); the GH-clonidine and prolactin-serotonin bluntings. The research agenda: PET ligands for glucocorticoid, CRF and peptide receptors; target-hormone receptor measurement in patient brain. And the closing clinical reframing: **depression as a systemic disease**, increased coronary artery disease, stroke, perhaps cancer; reduced bone density (hip fracture risk); raised inflammatory measures: possibly *mediated by the endocrine alterations themselves*, the neuroendocrine findings becoming internal-medicine findings, and the impetus for studying psychiatric neuroendocrinology being general-medicine impetus.

## 5. Why it matters clinically

- **The endocrine workup of depression is not optional**: thyroid function (including the antibody-grade subtleties) belongs in the assessment of major depression; the poor-antidepressant-response of subtle hypothyroidism is an actionable finding.
- **Thyroid augmentation works**: T3 acceleration and augmentation (STAR*D-confirmed) is one of the best-validated antidepressant strategies, an endocrine intervention entering routine algorithm steps.
- **The dexamethasone story's residue**: the DST's diagnostic failure taught test-honesty, while the persistent-abnormality finding retains prognostic value (poor-response prediction), and the combined Dex-CRF test carries the vulnerability-marker future.
- **The early-adversity mechanism**: HPA/CRF persistence explains *how* childhood abuse becomes adult depression, the biological bridge the trauma notes needed; the CRF1-polymorphism work explains *why not every abused child*.
- **PTSD's dissociated pattern** (high CRF, low-normal cortisol) is the axis-explanation of trauma's distinctive physiology; the contrast with depression being the differential lesson.
- **India**: thyroid disease is common, autoimmune thyroiditis under-detected, and iodine-deficiency regions persist; the depression-thyroid screen is an Indian clinical duty more than most; and glucocorticoid-availability realities (dexamethasone cheap, CRF unavailable) define the testable layer.

## 6. India lens

- **The thyroid-depression Indian layer**: goitre-belt history, iodisation programmes, and high autoimmune-thyroid prevalence make the HPT screen obligatory in Indian depression workups: TSH, and where available anti-TPO antibodies (the grade-4 lesson: the antibody-positive, functionally normal patient still merits follow-up and treatment-attention).
- **T3 augmentation Indian-style**: T3 (liothyronine) is inexpensive and available in India; the augmentation strategy (with its dose-logic and monitoring) is a practical Indian option, taught explicitly in the STAR*D context.
- **The cortisol-availability gradient**: dexamethasone is cheap; midnight-cortisol assays and CRF-stimulation tests are confined to specialist centres; the Indian application of the HPA literature is primarily clinical-reasoning (the systemic-risk teaching, the early-adversity formulation) rather than test-based.
- **The early-adversity mechanism lands hard in Indian practice**: child abuse, neglect and early loss are prevalent and under-addressed; the CRF-persistence model gives clinicians a biological, non-blaming formulation for abuse survivors' depression vulnerability ("the stress system recalibrated in childhood"); powerful in psychoeducation and in court reports.
- **PTSD's pattern and Indian trauma loads**: disaster, conflict and displacement populations carry high PTSD burdens, the high-CRF/low-cortisol pattern explains the physiological distinctness of trauma from melancholia, informing why antidepressant-response differs.
- **The systemic-disease teaching in Indian internal medicine**: depression-post-MI and post-stroke care in India iscardiology-first; the HPA-mediated risk framing gives psychiatry a seat in that consultation (the depression-as-systemic-disease argument).

**FAQ**
*"How can a hormone also be a neurotransmitter?"*: Same molecule, different job by location: adrenaline from the adrenal medulla is a hormone; the identical molecule released in CNS synapses is a transmitter; CRF and TRH likewise: the body reuses its chemistry, which is why endocrine measurements can reflect brain events (and why the boundaries dissolved).
*"Why does my depressed patient need thyroid tests?"*: Because subtle thyroid failure is over-represented in depression (down to the antibody-only grade), and hypothyroid patients respond poorly to antidepressants, the screen and the augmentation (T3 speeding and rescuing response) are the practical dividends.
*"What is the dexamethasone test's status?"*: As a diagnostic separator it failed (too insensitive); as research machinery it produced the field's biggest finding (HPA hyperactivity in depression); and persistent non-suppression after recovery retains prognostic value, predicting harder-to-treat illness. The combined dexamethasone-CRF test is the modern sensitive version.
*"How does childhood abuse raise adult depression risk biologically?"*: Through a recalibrated stress system: early adversity leaves HPA-axis and brain-CRF activity persistently elevated (shown in rats, monkeys and humans), sensitising the person to later depression; with CRF-1-receptor gene variants determining who is most vulnerable. The mechanism is real, physical, and treatable-in-principle.
*"Is PTSD's stress system the same as depression's?"*: No: PTSD shows high brain CRF with *normal or reduced* cortisol, a dissociated pattern opposite to melancholia's hypercortisolism; the difference matters for treatment expectations.
*"Why does the doctor mention my bones and heart when treating depression?"*: Because HPA-driven cortisol excess links depression to systemic disease, reduced bone density (fracture risk), coronary disease and stroke risk, inflammation, the endocrine findings turning depression into an internal-medicine risk state, and its treatment into prevention.

**Self-test MCQs**

**Q1.** The principle that the same substance functions as a hormone at one site and a neurotransmitter at another is best illustrated by:
- (a) Adrenaline in the adrenal medulla vs the CNS, and CRF as hypothalamic hormone vs extrahypothalamic transmitter
- (b) Insulin in the pancreas only
- (c) Digestive enzymes
- (d) Haemoglobin

**Answer: (a).** Pleiotropy: the founding reason endocrine/neuronal demarcations lost heuristic value, and the logical basis of the neuroendocrine window.

**Q2.** The major limitation of the neuroendocrine window strategy for schizophrenia research:
- (a) Plasma is unavailable
- (b) The tuberoinfundibular dopamine system regulating prolactin is physiologically unrelated to the mesolimbicocortical dopamine pathway implicated in schizophrenia
- (c) Prolactin is not measurable
- (d) There is no dopamine in the brain

**Answer: (b).** The circuit-dissociation caveat: plasma prolactin-dopamine coupling says nothing about limbic dopamine (unlike the plausible serotonin/noradrenaline windows with their widely-projecting cell groups).

**Q3.** Blunted TSH response to TRH, found in approximately 25% of patients with major depression, is attributed most plausibly to:
- (a) Excess thyroid hormone
- (b) Chronic TRH hypersecretion with pituitary TRH-receptor downregulation (supported by elevated CSF TRH)
- (c) Pituitary surgery
- (d) lodine excess

**Answer: (b).** The downregulation account, one of the field's durable, replicated findings.

**Q4.** The "most important finding in all of biological psychiatry" per the chapter is:
- (a) The 5-HT2 receptor blockade rule
- (b) HPA-axis hyperactivity in a significant subgroup of patients with major depression
- (c) The dexamethasone suppression test's diagnostic validity
- (d) Ventricular enlargement

**Answer: (b).** Cortisol and CRF hypersecretion, pituitary/adrenal enlargement, receptor downregulation, recovery-normalisation, prognosis-persistence, the HPA story in one claim.

**Q5.** The combined dexamethasone-CRF test's claim to fame is:
- (a) It replaced thyroid function testing
- (b) Greater sensitivity than either test alone, detecting HPA alteration even in never-symptomatic first-degree relatives of depressives, raising the trait-vulnerability question
- (c) It cures depression
- (d) It measures prolactin

**Answer: (b).** Holsboer's innovation: the endocrine marker edging toward a vulnerability test.

**Q6.** The most consistent neuroendocrine finding in affective disorders research is:
- (a) Elevated prolactin at baseline
- (b) The blunted growth-hormone response to clonidine (persisting after recovery; robust in recent suicide attempters)
- (c) Raised oestrogen
- (d) Reduced FSH

**Answer: (b).** The α2-adrenergic-probe blunting: the field's reliability champion, with its trait-marker and suicide links.

**Q7.** In PTSD, the neuroendocrine pattern is:
- (a) Identical to melancholic depression (high cortisol, high CRF)
- (b) Elevated CSF-CRF with normal or reduced adrenocortical activity, a dissociated pattern
- (c) Low CRF and high cortisol
- (d) No measurable abnormality

**Answer: (b).** The trauma-physiology contrast with melancholia, one axis, two signatures.
