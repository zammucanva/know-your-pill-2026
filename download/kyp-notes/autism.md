# Autism Spectrum Disorder

## 1. In one line + overview

**Autism is a difference in how the brain develops its social-communication wiring and its handling of sensation, attention and routine (present from early childhood, lifelong, highly variable in profile) and the best outcomes come not from searching for a cure but from early, intense support of communication, understanding of sensory needs, structured teaching, and a family that learns the child's language instead of only demanding the child learn theirs.**

The parents' first worry is usually one sentence: "he doesn't talk" or "she doesn't respond to her name" or "he plays alone, lines up cars, and screams if the routine changes." The doctor's first job is to take the sentence seriously; a toddler not pointing, not bringing toys to show, not responding to name by 12–18 months is a red-flag constellation, not a "boys talk late" phase. Autism affects the DOUBLE antenna: the channel used for sharing attention and feeling (pointing, showing, eye contact in service of communication, to-and-fro conversation) and the channel that runs interests, sensation and predictability (intense focused interests, repetitive play, sensory over/under-reaction, distress at change). It is called a SPECTRUM not because severity slides neatly from mild to severe, but because profiles vary wildly, a non-speaking child with a strong desire to connect and a fluent-speaking professor who cannot read social subtext are both autistic. Around a third to half of autistic children have intellectual disability as well; many others are average or above; seizures, ADHD, anxiety, sleep and gut problems ride along frequently. There is no blood test; diagnosis is clinical, and EARLY, because the years from 1 to 4 are the brain's most buildable window, and intervention in them changes trajectories.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Recognise the early red flags of autism in a toddler (18-month checkpoints: name, point, show, pretend)
- Explain the two-domain DSM-5/ICD-11 structure (social communication + restricted/repetitive) with the sensory clause
- Describe the four brain stories in plain words: the prediction engine, the sensory gain dial, the attention tunnel, the connectivity pattern
- Run a diagnostic workup: developmental history, autism-specific observation (ADOS-lineage tools, named), developmental/IQ testing, hearing, and comorbidity sweep
- Separate autism from its mimics: primary language disorder, hearing impairment, intellectual disability, social anxiety, and the quiet girl who "masks"
- Deliver the management grid: early intensive naturalistic-behavioural intervention, communication systems (including AAC/PECS when speech is delayed), occupational/sensory work, parent-mediated programmes, education placement decisions, pharmacotherapy ONLY for defined comorbid symptoms
- Handle Indian realities: late presentation, scarce professionals, the parent-mediated route (ComDEAL/WHO CST), therapy costs, RPwD 2016 and the National Trust framework
- Answer the hard family questions honestly: vaccines, screens, "will he talk", cures claimed by WhatsApp, marriage and adulthood

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Autism's measured prevalence has risen steadily; current CDC surveillance in the US reports about 1 in 36 eight-year-olds; most high-quality surveys elsewhere land between 1 and 2 per 100. The rise is largely explained by broadened criteria, better detection (including girls and children without intellectual disability), and awareness, not by a true epidemic of a new brain condition. Boys are identified 3–4 times more often than girls; part of this is real biology, part is girls' quieter, "masking" presentations being missed. Mean age of diagnosis in well-resourced systems is ~3–4 years, but parents typically notice concerns by 18–24 months, a two-year gap of wasted buildable time that early-red-flag vigilance closes. Onset is early-childhood, though higher-support presentations are noticed in infancy while fluent-speaking presentations may be identified only at school age or later.

**India.** The landmark community study of over 4,000 households (Srinivasan, NIMHANS and colleagues, published 2021) estimated roughly 1 in 100 children, a million-plus Indian children, heavily under-identified. The typical Indian trajectory: concern noticed at 2–3 years, often dismissed as "he will talk late, his uncle also did"; presentation to a professional only when the child is 4–5, usually for "speech delay"; diagnosis by a developmental paediatrician/psychiatrist at centres concentrated in metros; then the desert: special educators, speech therapists and occupational therapists in short supply outside cities, private therapy priced per session. Yet India also has workable rails: the National Trust Act framework, RPwD 2016 entitlements, parent-training programmes validated in Indian conditions (the NIMHANS Communication DEall, ComDEAL, model, and WHO's Caregiver Skills Training delivered in Indian districts), and mature parent organisations (Action for Autism, Forum for Autism, Parent Support Groups regionally) that function as service-navigators in a way no clinic can match.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**Genetic (the dominant story)**
- Heritability estimates 60–90% from twin studies; autism is among the most heritable neurodevelopmental conditions
- Architecture: many common gene variants of small effect PLUS rare de novo mutations; sibling recurrence risk is much higher than population base (~10–20% in younger siblings of autistic children)
- The "broader autism phenotype", social-communication quirks in parents/engineering/analytic family lines, the biology expressed mildly
- Associated syndromes and medical genetics: fragile X, tuberous sclerosis, Rett (the regression-at-6–18-months girl with hand-wringing movements; a specific entity, ICD-11 separate), chromosome 15 duplications, genetic referral with karyotype/fragile-X/CGH microarray for selected cases

**Neurodevelopmental / biological**
- Advanced parental age, prematurity, very low birth weight, prenatal exposures (valproate is the clearest medication signal) raise risk modestly
- NO evidence for vaccines (the claim is dead; one retracted fraudulent paper started it, and every large study since, including millions-strong cohorts, is negative); NO evidence for "screen time causing autism"; NO evidence for cold "refrigerator" parenting (a discarded psychoanalytic slander)

**Environmental / social (as modifiers, not causes)**
- Nothing a typical Indian family did or did not do CAUSES autism; worth stating explicitly, because blame theories are the first thing to kill
- Deprivation-driven social difficulties (institutionalised children) can MIMIC autism and improve with care; the reason the developmental history matters

**Indian context**
- The local myth set (vaccines, "the mother worked", "too much mobile", "God's punishment") does damage twice: delays help-seeking AND loads the mother with guilt; address it in the first session
- Two-child-family dynamics: the "you spoilt him" accusation from elders; the hidden second-born at higher recurrence risk

## 6. What happens in the brain

**Story 1: the prediction engine.** The brain runs on prediction: it builds models of what happens next and flags surprises. In autism the engine leans on prediction less and on raw detail more, the world arrives less filtered, less pre-formatted. Social life is the least predictable stream of all: faces change, tone shifts, rules are implicit. So the child does the rational thing, anchors to the predictable: the same route, the same plate, the same YouTube clip, trains' timetables, ceiling fans. Insistence on sameness is not stubbornness; it is self-built scaffolding against a world that feels like static. Therapy does not remove the scaffolding need, it builds prediction INSIDE the social stream (routines, visual schedules, previewing changes) and then stretches it.

**Story 2: the sensory gain dial.** Each brain has volume dials for sound, touch, light, smell. In autism the dials are set differently, often UP: the temple loudspeaker, the mixer grinder, new clothes' seams, a relative's perfume can be genuinely painful or demand-collapsing. Some dials sit low: the child who doesn't react to a name may be under-registering, not ignoring; the spinning, flapping and toe-walking (called "stimming", self-regulatory movement) are the nervous system tuning itself. The practical medicine: sensory profiling (an occupational-therapy job) before behavioural labelling; many "behaviour problems" are sensory events with an obvious trigger once you audit them.

**Story 3: the attention tunnel.** Typical attention is a broad floodlight shared across people and things. Autistic attention more often runs as a narrow, deep beam, "monotropism". This is the engine of BOTH halves of the stereotype: the intense interests (dinosaurs, train routes, vehicle logos, dates) AND the missed social bids (a person's wave does not enter the beam). The beam is also the teaching handle: enter the tunnel (the child's interest) and build language, counting, and reciprocity INSIDE it, the principle that distinguishes naturalistic developmental-behavioural intervention from cold drills.

**Story 4: the connectivity pattern.** Early brain overgrowth with atypical long-range connectivity and locally dense wiring; the technical summary of what imaging shows. Its clinical translation: parts of the brain that need to coordinate (face-processing + language + motor-imitation, across distant regions) synchronise differently. Imitation-based learning, the human baby's default channel, runs better when slow, explicit and rewarded than when fast and implicit. That is why the child CAN learn faces and feelings (taught as skills, like typing) a fact that keeps early intervention honest and hopeful.

## 7. Symptoms

**The social-communication domain (patient-language: the "sharing channel"):**
- Infancy/toddlerhood: name response weak or absent; not pointing to show or request; not bringing toys to display; no shared gaze-checking; limited back-and-forth babble; does not follow a point; preference for objects over faces
- Pretend play absent or delayed (feeding a doll, "driving" a block) while object play may be rich but repetitive (wheels spinning, lining up, sorting)
- Language: delayed, echolalia (repeating heard words/scripts, often WITH meaning: "want biscuit" memorised as one word), pronoun reversal ("you want water" meaning "I"), unusual prosody (flat, sing-song, pedantic)
- Conversation at older ages: one-sided lectures on the interest; poor reciprocal questioning; missing sarcasm, teasing, lies; taking idioms literally ("keep an eye on the bag")
- Eye contact: reduced, avoided, or used on the child's OWN terms; reduced ESPECIALLY when listening hard (it is a processing trade-off, not always a deficit)

**The restricted/repetitive domain (the "predictability channel"):**
- Repetitive motor movements: hand-flapping, finger-flicking, rocking, spinning, toe-walking
- Repetitive object use: lining up, sorting, opening-closing, spinning wheels
- Insistence on sameness: the same route to school, the same cup, distress at furniture moved, at new clothes, at haircut; rituals around food (texture/colour-based selectivity: a nutritional issue in itself)
- Intense restricted interests: vehicles, trains, maps, celestial bodies, animal facts, video clips (the interest is often a talent-in-waiting)
- Sensory responses: hands over ears at mixer/blender/festivals; smell-sniffing; staring at spinning objects; pain reactions that seem too little or too much; food textures refused

**Other frequent travellers (the comorbidity layer):**
- Intellectual disability (roughly a third to half of diagnosed children); determines support needs
- ADHD (very common; adds inattention/hyperactivity on top of autism-specific profiles)
- Anxiety, especially the demand-heavy school years; "demand avoidance" presentations
- Sleep problems (settling, night-waking): chronic and under-treated
- Epilepsy (~10–20% across the lifespan, with two peaks, early childhood and adolescence; a regression in adolescence warrants EEG thinking)
- Gastrointestinal: constipation (often feeding-selectivity related), reflux; both worsen behaviour silently
- Meltdowns and shutdowns: NOT tantrums; the system-overload outcome (sensory + demand + change); look different (no audience-seeking, often with fear, exhaustion after)
- Regression (loss of words/skills at 18–24 months) occurs in a subgroup, always warrants workup (hearing, seizures, the Landau–Kleffner thought, and syndromes)

**The missed girl (clinical alert for Indian clinics):** girls more often mask, imitating peers, suppressing stims, copying social scripts, holding it together at school and collapsing at home ("the Dr Jekyll/Mr Hyde report"), then presenting at 10–14 with anxiety, eating difficulty, or exhaustion. Ask about the home-collapse pattern; ask the grandmother.

## 8. Diagnosis

**The logic (DSM-5/ICD-11 paraphrased):** (a) persistent differences in social communication and social interaction across multiple contexts, social-emotional reciprocity, nonverbal communicative behaviours, and developing/maintaining relationships; (b) restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour/interests/activities: at least two of stereotyped movement/speech, insistence on sameness, intense restricted interests, sensory differences; (c) present from early developmental period (may be masked until demands exceed capacity); (d) causing significant impairment; (e) not better explained by intellectual disability (which is judged INDEPENDENTLY; autism and ID coexist, one does not exclude the other).

**The workup:**
- **Developmental history with the red-flag timeline**: response to name, pointing, showing, pretend play, joint attention episodes; regression specifically probed; family history (the broader phenotype)
- **Structured observation**: ADOS-2 lineage (named; the gold-standard play/conversation-eliciting tool, with ADOS-derived calibrated severity), plus interview schedules like ADI-R/3Di (named) in specialist centres; DSM-5/ICD-11 levels (1/2/3 support-needed) assigned for services
- **Developmental/IQ testing** (Mullen/Bayley-lineage for toddlers; WISC-lineage for older; literacy-adjusted for Indian children): profile, not just a number
- **Language assessment** by a speech-language pathologist (receptive vs expressive gap; crucial: the child who UNDERSTANDS more than he produces is on a different track than the child with global comprehension loss)
- **Hearing test**: mandatory before any "speech delay" verdict
- **Medical genetics**: karyotype + fragile X ± CGH microarray where syndromic clues, regression, or family recurrence; metabolic screen selectively (the skin-smell-hair-organomegaly sweep)
- **EEG where**: regression, staring spells, or epilepsy; **brain MRI only for focal signs/regression with seizure thinking**
- **The comorbidity audit**: sleep, feeding/GI, ADHD screen, anxiety, the child's own pain/illness reporting style (illness in autistic children presents as BEHAVIOUR CHANGE; the mother of an autistic child saying "he is flapping much more today" is reporting a medical symptom until proven otherwise)

### Differential table

| Looks like autism | The distinguishing feature |
|---|---|
| Hearing impairment | No pointing/showing but STRONG non-verbal reciprocity (gaze, mime, social babbling); grows fast after aids; test hearing first, always |
| Primary developmental language disorder | Social reciprocity intact: eye contact, showing, pretend play, joint attention all present despite poor speech |
| Intellectual disability (without autism) | Social interest commensurate to mental age; the child ENGAGES warmly at their level; repetitive behaviours less fixed, interests not over-focused |
| Social anxiety / selective mutism | History of typical infancy (pointed, showed, pretended); mute in school but speaks at home; interest in peers visible |
| Attachment/deprivation history (institutionalised child) | Recovers social reciprocity with sustained caregiving trajectory |
| ADHD | Impulsivity, poor attention everywhere, but pointing/showing/reciprocity age-typical (comorbidity is the norm, though) |
| The masked girl (late-diagnosed) | Anxiety/eating/school-burnout presentations with home-collapse and social-exhaustion history, probe the earlier years |

## 9. Management

**The frame: autism is supported, not cured.** Everything with the word "cure", "recovery", or "detox" attached is commerce (see FAQ). What changes trajectories is a coordinated, early, long-run programme.

**1: Early intensive intervention (the core evidence).**
- Naturalistic Developmental Behavioural Interventions (NDBI), the modern fusion: applied-behaviour-analysis techniques delivered inside PLAY and the CHILD'S interests (the Early Start Denver Model is the archetype), 15–25 structured hours weekly in some form (professional + parent-delivered combined), measured targets, developmental sequence
- ABA-lineage structured teaching for specific skills and behaviour supports; TEACCH-style visual structure for classrooms
- The Indian delivery reality: professional density is thin, so parent-mediated programmes carry the dose: NIMHANS ComDEAL (Communication Development in Autism through Elevation of parent-learning) and WHO's Caregiver Skills Training (delivered in Indian districts, tele-version available) are the evidence-backed routes: parents trained to run naturalistic sessions through daily routines, with periodic professional review
- *India note: metro therapy costs (approx 2026): speech therapy ₹400–1,000/session, occupational therapy ₹400–1,000, autism-specific early intervention/ABT ₹500–2,000; three to five sessions a week is a middle-class-breaking ₹6,000–30,000/month. The honest counsel: parent-training plus ONE professional anchor session weekly, done faithfully at home, outperforms haphazard daily commercial sessions; and free/subsidised channels exist (district early-intervention centres under RBSK, government hospital child-guidance clinics, NGO programmes like Action for Autism).*

**2: Communication systems (speech is not the only door).**
- Speech-language therapy for pre-verbal foundations: imitation, joint attention, requesting
- AAC when speech is delayed/non-speaking: PECS (picture-exchange), choice-boards, and speech-generating devices/apps; giving a communication channel REDUCES meltdowns (the behaviour IS communication; remove the communication vacuum and the screaming loses its job)
- Total-communication (speech + gesture + pictures + text together); typing/keyboards for older minimally-speaking children: literacy taught explicitly (reading can outpace speech)
- Respect stims as regulation, not symptoms to extinguish first

**3: Sensory and occupational work.**
- Occupational therapy: sensory profile, graded exposure, daily-living skills (dressing, eating, toileting), motor coordination
- Environmental engineering at home: predictable spaces, warning before blender/vacuum, clothes labels cut, food texture mapping, cheap, powerful

**4: Structure and visual supports (the sameness used as medicine).**
- Visual timetables (picture sequence of the day), first-then boards, timers for transitions, previewing any change ("tomorrow we go by auto, not car; here is the auto picture")
- Consistency across home/school; social stories (short illustrated scripts for events: haircuts, festivals, weddings, write the child's own)

**5: Education.**
- Inclusion with support where possible (RPwD entitlement: every child has the right to neighbourhood schooling; a shadow/special educator where needed), special school when support needs exceed the inclusive classroom's capacity, decided by FUNCTION, not stigma or ambition
- Teaching adaptations: seat in the low-traffic corner, instructions short/literal/visual, warning before change, the child's interest as the curriculum doorway
- NIOS/open schooling for board years; vocational streams from 14+ onward

**6: Pharmacotherapy (symptom-targeted only, no drug treats autism itself).**
- Irritability/aggression/meltdown severity: risperidone and aripiprazole are the two best-evidenced (weight, sedation, prolactin monitoring)
- ADHD: methylphenidate/atomoxetine (usable, titrate carefully), atomoxetine often better tolerated
- Sleep: behavioural programme first; melatonin has the best evidence when needed
- Epilepsy: standard antiepileptics, neurologist-led
- SSRIs for anxiety: careful, low-start (activation risk)
- *India note (approx 2026): risperidone ₹30–150/month, aripiprazole ₹150–600, melatonin OTC-adjacent ₹200–500, methylphenidate ₹200–600/month; see ADHD note for stimulant rules.*

**7: Adolescence and adulthood (the Indian long-run).**
- Puberty preparation with visual material; privacy and self-care skills; safety/consent teaching in concrete scripts; vulnerability protection (autistic girls and boys are abuse targets; teach body rules explicitly)
- Mental health watch: anxiety and depression spike in adolescents, especially the fluent-speaking, aware-of-difference profile; also catatonia-like slowing in some adolescents (keep it in mind with a big regression/change)
- Vocational training around the interest (data entry, testing, libraries, bakery, tailoring, mechanics, art); Indian autism-employment initiatives exist (Action for Autism's employment work, SAP-style neurodiversity hiring pilots) but are metro-concentrated
- Legal planning: RPwD certification; National Trust guardianship (limited guardianship under the 2016 amendment logic) for adults needing decision support; the sibling conversation (the "after us" question); do it early, in the open, with a written plan

*India note: the government spine: RPwD 2016 (education, employment, certification, benchmark disability benefits where applicable), National Trust Act (guardianship, schemes like Niramaya health insurance for persons with autism/ID/cerebral palsy, a genuine ₹1-lakh-cover scheme), RBSK early-intervention centres (District Early Intervention Centres) as the free entry point. Parent organisations (Action for Autism Delhi, Forum for Autism Mumbai, regional support groups, the Autism Society of India) are the real navigation system; refer families on day one.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **The two lost years.** The average Indian child loses the critical window between "parent notices at 18 months" and "specialist confirms at 4 years", swallowed by reassurance ("boys speak late", "his father also spoke at 4") and watchful waiting. Every paediatrician and GP who checks the three 18-month flags (name response, pointing to show, pretend play) recovers those years for dozens of children per career. Refer on the flag, not after the wait.
- **The first-session script.** Kill the guilt myths explicitly (vaccines, mobile, working mother, karma); 90 seconds of this work prevents a year of maternal depression and family blame-war. Then give the honest frame: "his brain develops differently; he learns differently; we now teach the way he learns, early and consistently, most children make big gains; it is lifelong, not curable, and absolutely worth investing in." Then refer to a parent organisation THE SAME WEEK.
- **The therapy economics conversation.** Families sell land for ₹25,000/month therapy stacks. Give them the dose logic (parent-delivered + one anchor professional), the free channels (RBSK/DEIC, government clinics, NGO training), and permission to fire any "cure"-promising centre. A family with money left and a parent trained beats a family bankrupted by sessions.
- **The school negotiation.** Indian schools range from refusing admission ("we don't take such children") to excellent resource rooms. Know the RPwD education-rights language, carry the certificate, and expect the shadow-educator compromise. The interview question that reveals a school's real attitude: "what happens when he flaps his hands in your assembly?"
- **The joint family and the marriage market.** Grandparents need one job: no comparisons with the cousin's child. For the "will he marry" question from parents of a 4-year-old: park it; the functional-adulthood trajectory matters first (toileting, communication, safety, work); marriage is a downstream question answered by that trajectory. Late-diagnosed fluent-speaking adults are increasingly marrying, working and self-advocating in India.
- **The grandmother's observation is data.** In multi-generation households, the elder who says "this one is different from his brother at this age" is usually the most accurate historian in the room; interview her, not just the mother's notes.
- **Festivals and meltdowns.** Diwali/firecracker season, wedding bands, temple crowds; teach anticipatory sensory planning (ear defenders bought in advance, a retreat room identified at the venue, shorter visits). An Indian family that learns this engineering keeps the child IN family and religious life instead of excluded from it, culturally massive, clinically simple.

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: "speech delay" that wasn't the whole story.** A 26-month-old boy in Indore was brought by both parents with "he doesn't talk; his cousin spoke full sentences at two." History: babbling was there, words absent; he did not respond to his name even in a quiet room; never pointed at a plane or dog; brought no toys to show; instead he opened and closed the fridge door repetitively, lined up steel tumblers, and screamed for 30 minutes when the family's sofa was reupholstered. Grandmother: "he is different from his brother; he stays in his own world." Video-call consultations had twice yielded "wait till three." Examination: child avoided gaze not absolutely but under demand, tracked spinning fan blades in preference to a singing face, tolerated hearing testing (normal), had no dysmorphism; Mullen-scale testing: non-verbal reasoning near-age, communication domains both 12-month-level; ADOS-2 Module 1 scores in autism range; no regression. Diagnosis: autism spectrum disorder, level 2 social-communication, with language at early single-word level, cognition a relative strength. Management: hearing confirmed normal (closing the loop), parents enrolled in a parent-mediated ComDEAL-style programme (weekly training at a child-guidance clinic 40 minutes away, daily home sessions built around his door/lid interest, requesting games with jars and lids first), PECS began with two pictures (biscuit, ball) in week 3, first intentional picture-handover by week 6 with a drop in screaming episodes (from daily to twice weekly); ot referral for the sensory profile (mixer-grinder warning ritual, cut labels, firm-pressure games); visual daily-schedule board hung at home; grandmother given ONE defined job (the daily 20-minute "joint attention play" slot, she chose the singing game); vaccination completed per schedule without discussion: the parents' WhatsApp fears addressed in the first session with the evidence in one paragraph. At 12 months follow-up: 40 spoken words, two-word combinations emerging, pointing at buses from the window, "showing" his father a leaf once (the family sent the video). Teaching points: (1) the 18-month three-flag check in the parents' history gave the diagnosis away in four questions; (2) PECS reduced screaming by giving communication a channel; behaviour was the language; (3) the interest (lids and doors) became the curriculum; (4) the grandmother was converted from blamer to therapist with one defined role; (5) "wait till three" cost this child a year.

**Case 2: the girl who held it together at school.** A 13-year-old girl in a Pune convent school was brought for "sudden stubbornness and crying spells", refusing school for three weeks, headaches, marked food selectivity, washing her hands "until they feel right", and a 4 a.m.-waking pattern. School report: "a model student, slightly quiet, top marks in science." Home report (mother, weeping): "she is a different child at home, screaming, flapping her hands when excited, scripts entire Attenborough episodes, and has eaten the same six foods since she was small." History, revisited deliberately: sat late, walked late, no pointing (mother had to guess needs), intense imaginary worlds and one friendship at a time that collapsed when the friend "broke the rules", lifelong label at home, "sensitive and adamant", plus a mother who admitted she "reads people slowly and finds assemblies unbearable, always has" (the broader phenotype in plain sight). Assessment: fluent language with pedantic prosody, ADOS-2 Module 4 in autism range; cognition above average; PHQ-9 elevated; no eating disorder pathology (selectivity texture-based, long-standing, a sensory signature, not body-image). Diagnosis: autism (previously unrecognised, the masking profile) with secondary anxiety and depression, avoidant-restrictive food intake (sensory type). Management: naming the diagnosis to HER first (the relief scene; "there is a name and a whole internet of girls like me") framed as explanation, not defect; anxiety treated with low-dose SSRI plus CBT adapted to her literal style (written rules, explicit social scripts); school accommodation letter (assembly-break permission, lunch-table alternative, a "quiet pass"); the food work referred for slow sensory-ladder desensitisation, no coercion; mother-child co-education about masking costs and energy accounting (a "battery day" system for social-load planning); father counselled separately from the "she is being dramatic" frame. Six months: school attendance full, flapping permitted at home ("it is how she is happy, we stopped fighting it"), the friendship question under her own active study (she read the social-communication literature herself and requested a social-coaching group). Teaching points: (1) the Dr Jekyll/Mr Hyde home-school report IS the masking presentation, always ask for the home version; (2) the mother's own trait profile is family history that the family is living inside; (3) diagnosis at 13 was therapy by itself; identity relief precedes skill work; (4) "sudden stubbornness" in an autistic adolescent is usually anxiety wearing a costume.

## 12. Active recall prompts

- Recite the 18-month three-flag check (name response, pointing to show, pretend play) and what a fail on any one obligates you to do.
- State the two DSM-5 domains plus the sensory clause, and how severity levels 1–3 map to support, not to "mild/defect".
- Why is "he will talk late" a dangerous sentence at 24 months, and what distinguishes autism from pure language delay on the floor?
- Explain the prediction-engine story to a parent whose child melts down when the sofa is moved, and give two interventions that follow from it.
- Give the evidence hierarchy of intervention: NDBI/parent-mediated/AAC/OT, and the role of medication (which two drugs, for which symptom).
- Name the Indian service spine (RBSK/DEIC, RPwD, National Trust, Niramaya, parent organisations) and one honest sentence about therapy economics.
- List five comorbidities that change management (epilepsy, ADHD, sleep, GI, anxiety) and the "illness presents as behaviour change" rule.
- What is the masking presentation in girls, and which single interview manoeuvre detects it?

## 13. FAQ: asked the way parents ask it

**"Did the vaccines do this?"** No. This question has been studied more than almost any question in medicine, very large studies across countries, including over a million children, find no link. The one paper that claimed it was fraudulent and retracted, its author struck off. Vaccines prevented diseases that used to kill and disable children; they did not cause this. The timing coincidence (the MMR age overlaps with when autism becomes visible) explains why the myth felt true.

**"Was it because I gave him the mobile too early?"** No. Screens do not cause autism. (Screens also do not TREAT it, and heavy screen time can steal practice hours and delay language in ANY child, so we limit them, but as schedule-building, not as guilt.)

**"Will he ever speak?"** Most autistic children develop useful speech, and those who do not can still communicate: pictures, apps, typing. Our job is to open a communication channel whatever form it takes. The strongest predictors of later speech are early joint-attention engagement and non-verbal problem-solving; both of which we work on from day one, before words.

**"Some centre says they can cure autism with diet/detox/stem cells/hyperbaric."** No treatment cures autism. A child who improves did so through development and teaching, not the diet, and the improvement gets sold back to you as the diet's miracle. Meanwhile a gluten-casein-free trial shows weak and inconsistent evidence, and unmonitored diets can worsen the already-selective eating. The same money buys a year of parent-delivered intervention with better-documented gains.

**"Is it because I worked / didn't give enough time?"** No. This is a neurodevelopmental difference with strong genetic roots. Mothers who worked did not cause this, mothers who stayed home did not prevent it, and the relatives implying otherwise are wrong. You are now the most important resource this child has; guilt will waste your energy.

**"Special school or normal school?"** The child's support needs decide, not our ambition or shame. A child who can learn in a classroom with adaptations belongs in the inclusive school with support (which is also his legal right); a child who needs a low-sensory, high-structure, individually-paced setting learns MORE in the special school. Placement is reviewed yearly; it is a tool, not a verdict.

**"Why does he flap his hands? Should we stop it?"** Flapping and rocking are how his nervous system regulates itself; they usually mean excitement or coping, not illness. Stopping them by force removes the coping without removing the need. We intervene on stims only when they injure the child or block learning, and even then by REPLACING, not just suppressing.

**"What about a second child? Will it also have autism?"** The chance is higher than the base rate, roughly 10–20% for a younger sibling versus ~1% for others. It is a real elevation but NOT a certainty; most siblings develop typically. A pregnancy-decision is bigger than this number; genetic counselling can individualise it.

**"She talks so well and got top marks. How is she autistic?"** Fluent speech and good marks coexist with autism; the difference is in the SHARING channel (to-and-fro conversation, reading subtext, flexibility) and the sensory/predictability needs. Many such girls are identified only when the masking collapses in adolescence. Their diagnosis is not a mistake; it is late.

**"What will happen to him when we are gone?"** That question is why we plan: functional skills (communication, safety, daily living), the National Trust guardianship route, the sibling conversation early, and a written long-term plan, while also building whatever independent living his trajectory allows. Ask us for the planning session now, not the reassurance version; the plan is what lets you sleep.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**The two-domain answer for theory:** social-communication differences + restricted/repetitive patterns (incl. sensory), present from early development, causing impairment, with specifiers: severity levels 1/2/3 (per-domain), intellectual disability status, language level, associated medical/genetic conditions (e.g., epilepsy). ICD-11 splits the same architecture with explicit qualifiers.

**Mnemonic, "RISE":** Reciprocity (shared attention/feeling), Interaction, Sameness, and Excess/deficit of Sensation, the four symptom pillars; or for the toddler red flags: "Name, Point, Show, Pretend" (all by 18 months, fail one, refer).

**High-yield facts:**
- Prevalence ~1 in 36 (US surveillance) / ~1 in 100 (Indian community study 2021); boys:girls ~3–4:1 with detection bias
- Earliest deviating milestone: JOINT ATTENTION (pointing/showing/gaze-sharing), before speech
- Echolalia is often MEANINGFUL language in construction; pronoun reversal is classic
- Twin heritability 60–90%; sibling recurrence ~10–20%; no vaccine link (the question will be asked in viva)
- Epilepsy ~10–20% with an ADOLESCENT second peak; regression in adolescence → think EEG, also catatonia
- Only two drugs with solid evidence for irritability in autism: risperidone, aripiprazole (no drug treats autism core)
- NDBI/early intensive + parent-mediated programmes (WHO CST, NIMHANS ComDEAL) = the delivery evidence
- Meltdown ≠ tantrum: overload, not audience
- Indian legal spine: RPwD 2016 + National Trust (guardianship, Niramaya insurance) + RBSK/DEIC early-intervention entry

**Classic traps:**
1. "Speech delay" verdict without hearing test and joint-attention history at 24 months
2. Diagnosing intellectual disability and stopping (autism + ID coexist; one does not exclude the other; each judged separately)
3. Missing the masked girl (school-fine, home-collapse) until the adolescent anxiety crisis
4. Treating meltdowns as naughtiness with escalating punishment (which deepens the spiral)
5. Banning stims as step one (regulation removed before replacement exists)
6. "Wait till three": the two lost years; refer on the 18-month flags
7. Forgetting that illness presents as behaviour change (constipation, caries, ear infection, reflux behind "sudden aggression")

**Indian exam corner:** expected patterns ("an 18-month-old not responding to name, no pointing, repetitive toy play) outline assessment and management"; "early red flags of autism"; "distinguish autism from language delay and intellectual disability"; "role of parent-mediated intervention in LAMI settings". Indian-context marks: 1-in-100 prevalence study, ComDEAL/WHO CST, RPwD/National Trust provisions, the RBSK/DEIC entry point, therapy-economics counselling.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. The earliest developmental milestone that typically deviates in autism:**
A. Walking
B. Joint attention (pointing/showing/shared gaze)
C. Single words
D. Bowel control
**Answer: B.** Joint attention deviates before speech: the 18-month "Name, Point, Show, Pretend" check.

**2. An 18-month-old fails all four red flags. The correct action:**
A. Reassure and review at age 3
B. Refer for autism-specific assessment now, with hearing testing
C. Advise speech therapy only
D. Wait for regression to occur
**Answer: B.** The flags justify immediate referral: "wait till three" wastes the buildable window.

**3. The two DSM-5 domains plus the clause most often forgotten:**
A. Social communication + restricted/repetitive; sensory differences clause
B. IQ + adaptive function
C. Language delay + motor stereotypies
D. Anxiety + attachment
**Answer: A.** The sensory clause, and sensory triggers behind much "behaviour".

**4. Regarding vaccines and autism:**
A. MMR shows a small consistent link
B. No link in any large study; the original claim was fraudulent and retracted
C. Only thimerosal is implicated
D. The link is proven in Indian cohorts only
**Answer: B.** The settled science; be ready to state it plainly in clinic and viva.

**5. The two best-evidenced drugs for severe irritability/aggression in autism:**
A. Fluoxetine and lithium
B. Risperidone and aripiprazole
C. Haloperidol and diazepam
D. Atomoxetine and clonidine
**Answer: B.** Symptom-targeted pharmacotherapy, no drug treats the core condition.

**6. A previously fluent autistic 14-year-old shows new staring spells and academic decline. Priority:**
A. Assume adolescent anxiety; increase counselling
B. Arrange EEG/neurology evaluation: epilepsy's second peak
C. Start an SSRI immediately
D. Reduce school hours only
**Answer: B.** Epilepsy peaks again in adolescence; new symptoms get medical workup before behavioural framing.

**7. The Indian parent-mediated early-intervention programme developed at NIMHANS:**
A. ComDEAL
B. Dava-Pairav
C. SNEHA-ASHA
D. Tele-MANAS-child
**Answer: A.** Communication DEall: parent-training as the scalable delivery channel; WHO CST is the international sibling.

**8. A school-perfect 13-year-old girl screams and flaps only at home, with strict food texture rules and one intense friendship. Best hypothesis:**
A. Conduct disorder
B. Masked autism with the home-collapse pattern
C. Early psychosis
D. Oppositional defiant disorder
**Answer: B.** The Dr Jekyll/Mr Hyde report; ask the earlier-years history (pointing, showing, pretend play) and assess accordingly.
