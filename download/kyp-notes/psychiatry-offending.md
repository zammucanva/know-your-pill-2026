# Psychiatric Disorder & Offending — Causes, Associations and the Risk Architecture

## 1. In one line + overview

**Most offending grows from ordinary disadvantages (supervision, discipline, conflict, poverty, peers); some offending grows from disorder (schizophrenia's modest violence excess, the substance-misuse multiplier, frontal neurobiology); and the clinical task is a formulation, never a checklist, of perpetrator, victim and context.**

Three Oxford chapters combine here. **Psychosocial causes (11.2, Farrington's synthesis)** delivers the longitudinal-science backbone: parental supervision as the strongest replicable predictor; harsh, erratic and cold discipline; the warmth buffer (warm punitive mothers' sons convicted at 21% vs cold punitive 51%); teenage mothers, abuse and its intergenerational transmission; broken homes as vehicles of *conflict* rather than loss (the affectionate mother neutralises the broken home); criminal parents and the 6%-of-families-half-of-convictions concentration; large family size (4+ siblings doubling risk); socio-economic gradients and the 17–18 affluence paradox. **Disorder associations (11.3.1)**: schizophrenia's 2–4× (men) / 6–8× (women) violence excess after adjustment, 5% of population violence attributable to severe mental illness, homicide schizophrenia rates of 5–15%, premorbid conduct disorder as the engine, threat-control-override symptoms, and the neuropsychiatric model (prefrontal, amygdala-orbitofrontal connectivity, MAOA×maltreatment, serotonin-impulsivity). **Substance misuse (11.3.2)**: alcohol in half of interpersonal assaults, the ECA comorbidity ladder (2% → 7% → 20% → 22%), acquisitive offending concentrated in 10% of drug users, and the medico-legal rules (specific intent, diminished responsibility excluding simple intoxication). The integrating figure: offending emerges from the interaction of social deprivation, parenting, intelligence, peers, genetics, substance misuse, victimisation, personality and mental disorder, with every violent act a perpetrator-victim-context triad.

## 4. Epidemiology

**Offending and disorder.** Schizophrenia, personality disorder and substance-related disorders are significantly overrepresented among offenders; psychosis affects 3.7% of male and 4% of female prisoners (2–4× community rates, per the 23,000-prisoner systematic review); violence risk in schizophrenia 2–4× (men) and 6–8× (women) after adjustment; **~5% of violent crime in Northern European populations attributable to severe mental illness**; homicide schizophrenia rates 5–15% (family members more often victims than strangers); aggression in one-third of first-episode psychosis cases. **Substance misuse**: alcohol a key factor in at least half of interpersonal assaults; alcohol/drug misuse contributes to **two-fifths of homicides** (with 17% by patients with severe mental illness *plus* substance misuse); drug misusers overwhelmingly commit acquisitive offences, NTORS: 10% of clients committed 76% of the pre-treatment acquisitive crimes; onset sequence: truancy 13.8 → crime 14.5 → drugs 16.2 → hard drugs 19.9 years (crime *precedeses* drug use). **India**: NCRB-based Indian data show the majority of violent crime without established disorder, alcohol deeply implicated in interpersonal violence, and prison psychiatry underserved; the chapter's 5%-attributable figure is the stigma-countering number Indian clinicians most need.

## 5. Causes: the psychosocial architecture (ch 11.2)

**Individual risk factors.** Low IQ predicts offending: mediated through school failure (Lynam's Pittsburgh analysis: low verbal IQ → school failure → delinquency), poor abstract-concept manipulation (foreseeing consequences, appreciating victims' feelings), and executive-function deficits. **Child rearing**, the four dimensions: **supervision/monitoring** (the strongest and most replicable predictor: Cambridge-Somerville, best predictor of violent and property crime to age 45); **discipline** (harsh physical punishment: Nottingham, 40% of offenders smacked/beaten at 11 vs 14% of non-offenders; erratic-inconsistent discipline); **warmth/coldness** (the warmth buffer: 51% conviction for cold punishing mothers, 21% for warm punishing, 23% for warm non-punitive, warmth protects even within punishment); **involvement**. Explanations: attachment (Bowlby; unattached children become offenders) and social learning (inconsistent, non-contingent responses and antisocial models). **Teenage mothers**: children at risk for low attainment, antisocial behaviour, substance use, early sex, delinquency (the biological father's presence mitigates); Newcastle: teenage-married mothers twice as likely to have offender sons. **Child abuse**, Indianapolis: abused-under-11 children significantly more violent over 15 years; Cambridge-Somerville: half of abused/neglected boys had serious crime, alcoholism, mental illness or death before 35; Rochester: maltreatment under 12 predicted offending after controls. Theories: social learning (imitation), attachment/social bonding (low self-control), strain (negative emotions → revenge). **Parental conflict and broken homes**, Newcastle: disruption in the first 5 years doubles conviction risk; Dunedin: single-parent families overrepresented among violent offenders (28% vs 17% vs 9%); the National Survey: divorce/separation worse than death, under-5 worst, remarriage adds risk; **McCord's classic**: broken-without-affectionate-mother 62%, united-with-conflict 52%, united-no-conflict 26%, broken-with-affectionate-mother 22%; *conflict is criminogenic, structure is not; a loving mother compensates*. Cambridge: staying with the mother after separation equalled intact-low-conflict rates; the post-disruption trajectory (who cares, how stably) is decisive, favouring **life-course theories** (accumulating stressors and changing caretakers) over trauma (separation itself) or selection (pre-existing family differences) theories. **Criminal parents**, St Louis: arrested parents → arrested children (similar offence types); **less than 6% of Cambridge families produced half of all convictions; 63% of boys with a convicted parent were convicted by 40**; same-sex and older-sibling influences stronger; criminal parents did *not* teach crime (89% of convicted men at 32 opposed their children offending; joint parent-child convictions vanishingly rare): the mediating chain is **poor supervision**. Explanations: intergenerational risk-factor entrapment, environmental mediation, genetic mechanisms, official bias (all supported; none sufficient alone). **Large family size**: 4+ siblings doubles juvenile conviction risk; the most important independent predictor to age 32 in the Cambridge logistic regression; Wadsworth: 9% conviction with one child → 24% with four-plus; mechanisms: parental attention dilution, overcrowding (no prediction in the least-crowded homes), delinquent-sibling exposure (20% of boys with near-age brothers co-offended with them). **Socio-economic deprivation**: the literature is inconsistent, but British studies link low SES and offending (National Survey: 3% highest category to 19% lowest); low income and poor housing predict more consistently than occupational prestige; the paradox: convicted males were relatively well paid at the peak offending age of 17–18 (unskilled adult wages vs studying non-delinquents).

## 6. Causes: the neurobiological and disorder architecture (ch 11.3)

**Neurobiology of aggression**: orbitofrontal traumatic damage → impulsivity and aggression; prefrontal executive test abnormalities in antisocial subjects; EEG frontal slowing in more than half of repetitively violent prisoners; neuroimaging: reduced prefrontal size and activity in aggressive patients (predatory killers' blood flow resembles controls; the findings mark **impulsive/affective, not premeditated** aggression); two postulated groups (acquired frontal lesion impairing judgement and empathy; developmental executive deficits from foetal/birth injury, learning disorders, ADHD, substance misuse and antisocial PD with episodic dyscontrol); **reduced serotonin function relates to impulsivity rather than violence per se** (SSRI treatment implications); cortisol and dietary insufficiencies; adoption studies: biological parent–adoptee property-offence continuity; the Danish cohort's paternal-violence–adoptee-schizophrenia link; **MAOA×maltreatment** (low-activity variant → reduced amygdala regulatory-prefrontal reactivity → increased aggression, limbic volume reductions); twin studies: childhood antisocial behaviour with callous-unemotional traits is strongly genetic; adolescent-onset antisocial behaviour is environmentally driven. The chapter's caveat: these findings suggest management strategies; they neither cause nor predict individual violence deterministically.

**Schizophrenia and violence.** Two patterns: (1) conduct-disorder (in 20–40%) and antisocial PD *predating* psychosis, a premorbid-antisocial pathway; (2) violence arising later with the illness. Symptom evidence is method-conflicted but converges: positive symptoms raise minor and serious violence risk (negative symptoms reduce serious violence, perhaps by living alone); serious violence associated with psychotic *and depressive* symptoms, childhood conduct problems, and victimisation; **threat-control override (TCO)** (persecutory delusions, passivity, thought insertion: perceived threat plus loss of self-control) antecedes community violence after controlling for psychopathy and substance abuse; hallucinations, acute suicidality, acute conflict, separations, housing problems, and lack of insight all raise risk. **Comorbidity multipliers**: substance use 3× (men) / 16× (women); personality disorder 4× / 18×: with the comorbid group's common origin in conduct disorder. Neurobiology of schizophrenia-violence: better specific-executive and verbal test performance with greater impulsivity (the antisocial subgroup); worse orbitofrontal performance; reduced whole-brain and hippocampal volumes, impaired orbitofrontal-amygdala connectivity, amygdala structural abnormalities; emotion-intensity-perception deficits → conflict generation, failure to read resolution signals, escalation. **The formulation rule**: every violent act is perpetrator × victim × context (the drunk aggressive victim frightening the suspicious impulsive perpetrator with alcohol and a knife available); mental disorder may be the major determinant, entirely coincidental, or one interacting factor, even psychosis-explicable offences require the wider look (substances, networks, personality).

**Substance misuse and offending (ch 11.3.2).** Violence is not an inevitable pharmacological consequence, expectancy, consumption pattern, individual response, peers and interpersonal dynamics all shape it. **Alcohol is the single factor most associated with violence** (half of interpersonal assaults; stranger and domestic violence particularly). Drug misusers overwhelmingly commit **acquisitive offences**; the ECA ladder: violence in one year, no disorder 2%, major mental illness 7%, substance misuse disorder 20%, comorbid 22%. First-episode psychosis: under 10% seriously aggressive when psychotic, 23% lesser aggression; comorbid drug misuse 9× more aggressive after service contact. Intoxication mechanisms: disinhibition and impaired judgement from depressants; arousal and irritability from stimulants; toxic psychoses at severe intoxication; **withdrawal**: the overlooked state of irritability and aggression (delirium tremens with impaired perception, affect, judgement, impulse control); "pathological intoxication" of doubtful validity (usually hypoglycaemia, head injury or other organicity). Acquisition: NTORS concentration (half of clients crime-free, 10% committing 76% of crimes); the sequence argument (crime precedes drug use; causation runs both ways, heavy use amplifying impulsive acquisitive offending including robbery and burglary). Miscellany: drug-law classes, drug-driving, parenting impact.

## 7. Symptoms: presentation and assessment (the two chapters' practice core)

**Assessment of the substance-using offender** (the chapter's five-step guide): (1) detailed life history with corroboration (relationships, work, social situation); (2) substance history (onset, dose, route, weekly pattern, desired vs actual effects, effect on symptoms and behaviour, self-harm/aggression associations, treatment history); (3) detailed offence history with the effect of substances and illness on mental state **before, during and after each offence** (corroborated from witness statements); (4) full mental state with intelligence, personality and insight estimates; (5) practical implications for management and medico-legal reporting. **Structured risk context**: the **HCR-20** (Historical/Clinical/Risk-management 20-item) placing substance misuse among the significant risk factors; with the explicit warning that actuarial-tool reduction is "at best lazy and at worst negligent": the psychiatrist must articulate a formulation.

**Risk-management principles**: define the risk (self-harm, relapse, violence, toward whom: family, partners, carers); probability and severity; early-warning signs (behaviours, symptoms, non-compliance); change what can be changed, support, care, security; treat the substance misuse (therapeutic nihilism dispelled, NTORS: convictions for acquisitive, drug-selling and violent crimes reduced at 5 years); dual diagnosis provision as a *central* task of modern mental health care (staff training, joint working, clinical leadership, enhanced CPA).

**Medico-legal interface**: amnesia (common after violence, complicates but does not preclude fitness-to-plead); simple intoxication, no defence to basic-intent crimes, a narrow defence to specific-intent crimes (was the accused *so* intoxicated as to be unable to form the intent; purposiveness before/during/after the offence is the clinical clue); insanity rarely available (voluntary consumption bars automatism); diminished responsibility requires abnormality of mind from disease, injury or inherent causes; **intoxication is no defence; alcohol dependence could qualify as disease if the first drink was involuntary**; the court report's tasks: psychiatric contribution to *this* offence in the life context, whether treatment could prevent reoffending, and protecting society.

## 8. Diagnosis: using the association honestly

Three cautions discipline the diagnostic use of all this evidence. **Methodological**: offences are man-made and shifting concepts; psychiatrists see captive, selected prison populations (disadvantaged in the justice system by ethnicity, poverty, homelessness, mental illness); generalisability is context-bound; records are unreliable. **Attributional**: the population-level association (schizophrenia–violence 2–4×) does not reverse into individual causation; mental disorder is sometimes the major determinant, sometimes coincidental, usually one interacting factor. **Formulational**: the assessment must articulate which factors accounted for previous offending and which bear on future offending (perpetrator, victim, context) in the patient's own biography, not a checklist.

## 9. Management

- **Treat the disorder and the criminogenic factors together**, treating mental disorder without addressing substance misuse, networks, and structure will not reduce offending.
- **Dual diagnosis as core business**: staff training in substance misuse, joint working with drug/alcohol teams, local clinical leadership, enhanced care programme for severe mental illness with destabilising substance use.
- **Treatment reduces reoffending**: the NTORS 5-year conviction reductions; dispel therapeutic nihilism in both directions (the offender is treatable; the mentally disordered offender is manageable).
- **Relapse-prevention architecture**: early-warning signs, crisis plans, medication supervision where needed, the identified-victim principle (family members of patients with TCO symptoms deserve specific safety planning).
- **Court reports** in the chapter's three-task structure; risk management as a central mental-health task, not an orphan.

## 10. India lens

- **The 5% figure as an antidote**: Indian media narratives bind mental illness to violence; the Northern European attribution figure (~5% of violent crime) and the modesty of the 2–4× excess belong in every public-education answer.
- **Alcohol as the Indian multiplier**: consumed at home and in the street, alcohol sits in half of interpersonal assaults in the Western data: Indian alcohol-violence patterns (festival violence, domestic violence, road rage) mirror this; the practical screen at every forensic assessment is the drink-before-the-offence question.
- **The supervision finding generalises**: the strongest predictor (parental monitoring) maps onto Indian working-parent, migrant-labour and street-children realities; prevention programmes (child guidance, school engagement, Anganwadi-linked parenting support) are the policy translation.
- **Prison psychiatry gap**: Indian prisons hold large psychotic and substance-disordered populations with minimal mental health input; the 3.7%/4% psychosis figures with 2–4× elevation set the service case.
- **The MAOA×maltreatment and warmth findings** give Indian child-protection (POCSO enforcement, parenting programmes) a neurobiological as well as moral rationale.
- **Court work**: Indian forensic assessments under BNS unsoundness provisions need the chapter's assessment structure (the five steps) and its formulation discipline; the specific/basic-intent and diminished-responsibility doctrines have Indian analogues for the expert to master locally.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: The threatened neighbour.** A 34-year-old man with schizophrenia is referred from the community mental health team after telling staff his upstairs neighbour "is putting thoughts of killing me into my head through the fan". No violence has occurred; his family is frightened by the change. Formulation exercise: TCO symptoms (thought insertion + persecutory content), an antecedent marker; comorbid cannabis use discovered on the five-step assessment (the joint he smokes "for the voices"); past conduct problems in adolescence; a victim already specified (a named neighbour) with a shared building context; no weapon access established. Management: admission for assessment (the acute-symptom multiplier), antipsychotic review with cannabis-focused dual-diagnosis work, an explicit safety plan with the family (early-warning signs: the "fan" theme returning, missed appointments), housing mediation on discharge, and the named-neighbour consideration in risk planning. Teaching points: the TCO construct in clinical form; the comorbidity multipliers (3–16× substance, conduct-disorder pathway); the perpetrator-victim-context triad operationalised; formulation, not checklist.

**Case 2: The acquisitive decade.** A 27-year-old man with an opioid-use history appears for a court report after a burglary; his record shows theft from age 14, before any drug use (started at 17, heroin at 19). The defence wonders whether "addiction caused the offending". Assessment by the chapter's sequence: truancy 13, petty crime 14, first substance 16–17, heroin 19, **crime preceded drug use**; the family audit reveals the classic architecture, father convicted twice, poor supervision, six siblings in two rooms, parental conflict, mother warm and overwhelmed. The report states: substance dependence amplified an established antisocial trajectory (the NTORS concentration pattern, his offending intensified after heroin); treatment (ongoing buprenorphine maintenance plus occupational structuring) has documented crime-reduction evidence; family and supervision factors are the treatable criminogenic core. Teaching points: the sequence argument against single-cause defences; the psychosocial architecture in one biography; the treatability evidence delivered to a court.

## 13. FAQ

**"Are mentally ill people dangerous?"**: The measured answer: a small but real excess of violence risk in schizophrenia (2–4× in men after adjustments), around 5% of violent crime attributable to severe mental illness, far outweighed by alcohol, poverty and ordinary criminality; and people with mental illness are far more often victims than perpetrators.
**"Does schizophrenia cause his violence?"**: Sometimes primarily (especially with threat-and-control-override symptoms), sometimes coincidentally, usually as one factor among substances, personality, history and context. The formulation is the answer, not the diagnosis.
**"His parents split up, that explains it?"**: The longitudinal science says: conflict explains it more than breakage; a loving mother neutralises much of the break; and the post-separation arrangements (with whom, how stably) matter more than the separation itself.
**"Can addiction excuse the burglary?"**: No: crime typically *precedeses* heavy drug use; dependence amplifies but does not create the trajectory, and the law reflects this (simple intoxication is no defence to basic-intent crimes; diminished responsibility requires a qualifying abnormality beyond intoxication).
**"Is there a violence gene?"**: No: there is a gene-environment interaction (MAOA variant × maltreatment) detectable statistically, a research finding informing prevention, not a predictive test for any individual.
**"Can treating him actually prevent offending?"**: Yes: treatment of substance misuse reduces convictions at 5 years; treating psychosis with its comorbidities is risk management; the nihilism is the myth.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**Q1.** The single strongest and most replicable child-rearing predictor of later offending is:
- (a) Parental warmth
- (b) Poor parental supervision/monitoring
- (c) Bedtime routines
- (d) School fees

**Answer: (b).** Parents who do not know where their children are, and who let them roam unsupervised, raise the risk measured to age 45 (Cambridge–Somerville).

**Q2.** In McCord's analysis of broken homes, which group had the LOWEST later offending prevalence?
- (a) Broken homes without affectionate mothers (62%)
- (b) United homes with parental conflict (52%)
- (c) United homes without conflict (26%), statistically tied with broken homes with affectionate mothers (22%)
- (d) All groups equal

**Answer: (c).** With the affectionate-mother broken home at 22%; proving conflict, not breakage, is criminogenic, and maternal warmth compensates for paternal loss.

**Q3.** The ECA survey's one-year violence prevalences (no disorder / major mental illness / substance misuse / comorbid) were:
- (a) 2% / 7% / 20% / 22%
- (b) 20% / 20% / 20% / 20%
- (c) 0% / 25% / 50% / 75%
- (d) 5% / 5% / 10% / 10%

**Answer: (a).** The comorbidity staircase: substance misuse (alone or with mental illness) carries the risk, with comorbidity worst of all.

**Q4.** Threat-control-override (TCO) symptoms comprise:
- (a) Anxiety, tremor, sweating
- (b) Persecutory delusions, passivity phenomena and thought insertion, perceived threat plus perceived loss of self-control
- (c) Depression and guilt
- (d) Flashbacks and avoidance

**Answer: (b).** TCO antecedes community violence in schizophrenia-spectrum disorder even after controlling for psychopathy and substance abuse.

**Q5.** Reduced serotonin function in offenders is most closely associated with:
- (a) Violence directly
- (b) Impulsivity rather than violence per se
- (c) Intelligence
- (d) Psychosis

**Answer: (b).** Serotonin relates to impulsiveness, aggression, anxiety and depression, the impulsive pathway, with SSRI-treatment implications.

**Q6.** A defendant with alcohol dependence argues diminished responsibility for a killing committed while drunk. The correct legal analysis is:
- (a) Intoxation always supports the defence
- (b) Intoxication itself is no defence; dependence could qualify as a disease only if the first drink of the day was involuntary
- (c) Voluntary drinking always negates murder entirely
- (d) Alcohol is irrelevant to the defence

**Answer: (b).** The defence must establish an abnormality of mind from disease, injury or inherent causes; intoxication effects are set aside, and only genuine involuntary drinking within dependence could meet the disease limb.
