# Juvenile Delinquency & Serious Antisocial Behaviour — Young Offenders, Young Minds

## 1. In one line + overview

**Juvenile delinquency is antisocial behaviour that could result in conviction, and behind it lies a dense stack of treatable risk factors (supervision, discipline, school failure, mental disorder, substance misuse) that overlap so heavily with poor mental health that moving deeper into the youth justice system is itself a mental-health risk marker.**

The Oxford chapter (Bailey) reviews juvenile offending through the mental-health lens: delinquency, conduct problems and aggression as antisocial behaviours failing to conform to authority, norms, or others' rights; the juvenile age-band running from criminal responsibility to adult-court age (both varying by country); adolescence itself as the developmental stage of greatest health-need (mortality from accidents and self-harm; concerns of skin, weight, emotions, sexuality); and the machinery of the Youth Justice System. Its central clinical facts: **both mental disorder and offending peak in adolescence**; the risk factors for offending, poor mental health and substance misuse overlap substantially, and **the number of assessed risk factors increases as a young person moves deeper into the justice system**; many young offenders are disengaged from mainstream education and health services, and the critical task is maintaining service access through supervision and custody, since sentence-end detachment predicts deterioration and adult offending. *Source note: the uploaded text ends mid-chapter; the middle sections (epidemiological tables, screening, specific interventions) are unavailable, this note therefore builds its risk architecture from ch 11.2's fully-available juvenile data and cross-references the conduct-disorder note (L6), with the limitation flagged above and in the worklog.*

## 4. Epidemiology

**The Youth Justice System (England and Wales)**: the principal aim, preventing offending by under-18s; **157 Youth Offending Teams**; ~3,000 custodial places commissioned at any time across Young Offender Institutions, Local Authority Secure Children's Homes and private Secure Training Centres. 2005–2006: **301,860 recorded offences**, theft and handling 18.5%, violence against the person 18.1%, motoring offences 16.6%, criminal damage 12.9%. **16–17-year-olds committed 49.6%** of offences; males 80.6%, females 19.4%; white 85.2%, Black and minority ethnic 14.8%. Of 212,242 disposals: **80% pre-court or first-tier; 17% community sentences; 3% custodial**. **Adolescent health context**: 7.5 million 10–19-year-olds in the UK (half the child population); adolescent mortality (uniquely) did not fall in the late 20th century, accidents and self-harm leading; health needs greater than middle childhood or young adulthood, driven by chronic illness and **mental health problems**. **India**: no equivalent national youth-justice mental-health dataset; the JJ Act's structure (Juvenile Justice Boards, observation homes, the 16–18 assessment layer) parallels the YOT architecture; Indian young-offender mental health needs (substance misuse, conduct problems, abuse histories, school dropout) are documented in prison studies but systematic service links remain aspirational, making the chapter's service-access lesson the transferable core.

## 5. Causes: the risk architecture (ch 11.2's juvenile core)

The longitudinal science of juvenile risk factors (fully set out in the offending note, summarised here for the juvenile frame): **individual**, low IQ via school failure and executive deficits; impulsivity. **Family**: poor parental **supervision** (the strongest and most replicable juvenile predictor); **harsh, erratic or cold discipline** (with the warmth buffer: warm punitive mothers' sons convicted at 21% vs cold punitive 51%); **teenage motherhood**; **child abuse** (half of Cambridge-Somerville abused/neglected boys dead, ill or convicted before 35); **broken homes as conflict-vehicles** (not structure: united-with-conflict 52% vs broken-with-affectionate-mother 22%); **criminal parents** (6% of families producing half of convictions; 63% of sons with convicted parents convicted by 40; the mediating chain being supervision, not criminal teaching); **large family size** (4+ siblings doubling juvenile conviction risk, via attention dilution and delinquent-sibling exposure). **Social**: socio-economic deprivation (low income and poor housing predicting most consistently); **peer**: delinquent sibling and peer exposure (20% of boys with near-age brothers co-offending); **school**: failure and exclusion (the low-IQ bridge and the disengagement engine); **neighbourhood and situational** factors. **The clinical synthesis**: these are simultaneously the risk factors for mental disorder and substance misuse, which is the chapter's fundamental point.

## 6. What happens in the mind: two short stories

**The gradient story.** Picture the young person's journey through the system: first caution, first panel, first remand, first custody; at each step the assessed count of risk factors rises. This is not the system manufacturing risk; it is the sieve working: the young people who travel deepest are those carrying the most stacked disadvantages (unsupervised, harshly disciplined, school-failed, substance-involved, mentally unwell, previously abused). The clinical corollary: the custody population is the *highest-mental-health-need* paediatric population in the country; screening every entrant is not luxury but epidemiology.

**The detachment story.** The sentence ends; the statutory supervision ends with it; and the young person who had, through YOT and custody, been tenuously connected to some clinic, some counsellor, some prescription, detaches. The chapter's warning: once detached, circumstances deteriorate, offending continues, and the adult services inherit a harder case. The intervention window is the supervision period itself: register, treat, and hand over *before* the gate opens.

## 7. Symptoms: presentation and clinical needs

Young offenders present with: the conduct-disorder spectrum (see the L6 note for the full clinical programme: ODD vs CD, callous-unemotional traits, ADHD comorbidity, substance misuse); undisclosed depression, anxiety, PTSD (abuse histories, the child-abuse risk chain in reverse); self-harm and suicidality (the adolescent mortality pattern operating inside custody); neurodevelopmental disorders (learning difficulty, ADHD, communication disorder, the school-failure bridge); and substance misuse itself. The assessment needs are the standard CAMHS repertoire performed in justice settings: mental state, development, abuse enquiry, substance history, risk; plus the engagement problem: services reached through compulsion must convert to services accepted voluntarily before compulsion ends.

## 8. Diagnosis

- **Screen every entrant** to custody and every deep-end community case: mental state, self-harm risk, substance misuse, neurodevelopmental history, abuse history.
- **Diagnose the treatable** (depression, anxiety, PTSD, ADHD, psychosis) remembering the risk-factor-overlap logic: treating disorder is treating offending risk.
- **Read the conduct-spectrum accurately** (cross-reference L6): childhood-onset vs adolescent-onset CD (the genetic-vs-environmental distinction from the offending note); callous-unemotional traits; DMDD as the irritability-differential against misread "bipolar".
- **Assess the family system** (supervision, discipline, warmth, conflict, the treatable architecture).
- **Map service engagement** as an explicit clinical variable: who is this young person connected to, and what happens to that connection at sentence-end?

## 9. Management

- **Maintain mainstream and specialist access through supervision and custody**; the chapter's central instruction: support access while the YOT or custody holds the young person, because post-sentence detachment is the deterioration pathway.
- **Treat the psychiatric disorders** (per L6: multimodal conduct programmes, ADHD treatment, depression/anxiety interventions, trauma-focused work).
- **Work the family risk factors** (parenting programmes targeting supervision and discipline, the strongest-evidence prevention lever).
- **Engage education**: school reconnection as offending-prevention (the school-failure bridge).
- **Deliver the comprehensive CAMHS vision** (the Children's National Service Framework): the full tiered service for young offenders, from primary-tier consultation to YOT-embedded clinicians to secure forensic adolescent provision.
- **Plan the transition**: to adult services where needed, *before* the youth system releases the case (the transition-cliff lesson from the ID services note applies verbatim).

## 10. India lens

- **The JJ Act architecture**: Juvenile Justice Boards, observation and special homes, the age-assessment disputes, the Indian equivalent of the YOT layer; mental-health input into boards and homes is the service-development frontier.
- **The 3% custody figure reframes Indian fears**: the overwhelming majority of juvenile offending is handled pre-court, meaning the highest-yield mental-health interventions belong in the community (child guidance, school counselling, district early-intervention), not the institution.
- **Detachment Indian-style**: the observation-home release without follow-up is the exact detachment story; the JJ Act's aftercare provisions are the written answer; their implementation is the clinical task.
- **The stacked-risk Indian child**: migrant-labour families, street children, school dropout, child labour and substance misuse reproduce every element of the risk architecture, with Anganwadi, school and NGO workers as the screening workforce.
- **Costs (approx 2026)**: custody is the expensive end everywhere; the community risk-factor programmes (parenting groups, school engagement, brief interventions) are the cheap end, and theJJ system's own statutory machinery already exists to attach them to.

## 11. Clinical cases

**Case 1: The boy the board keeps seeing.** A 15-year-old appears before the Juvenile Justice Board for the third time in a year, petty theft, then fighting, now a scooter theft. The board's social enquiry report notes school dropout and "bad company"; nobody has assessed him. Screening in the observation home: marked ADHD symptoms since childhood, borderline literacy, evening self-harm scratches, and a history, disclosed only after the third interview, of physical abuse by a stepfather. Formulation: the stacked-risk case, abuse, school failure (the low-IQ-bridge with unrecognised ADHD), conduct deterioration, with depressive features beneath. Management: ADHD treatment and literacy support through the home's teacher; trauma-focused work beginning; the mother (warm but overwhelmed) engaged with a parenting programme; the board's disposition shaped by the assessment (a rehabilitative order rather than escalation); an aftercare plan with a named clinic appointment at release, not a hope, a booking. Teaching points: the repeated-attender is the screening trigger; three interviews before disclosure is normal; the risk-factor stack is the treatment plan; and the board is a clinical ally when handed readable assessment.

**Case 2: The release date.** A 17-year-old completes a custody order, stabilised on an SSRI for depression, engaged with the home's counsellor, and, for the first time, attending classes. The release date approaches; the statutory supervision ends with it; the district clinic has a four-month waiting list. The youth-justice clinician's moves, per the chapter's core instruction: transfer paperwork completed before release (with the young person's consent); the first clinic appointment booked *inside* the supervision window; the family's nearest adult identified as the appointment-keeper; a crisis card with Tele-MANAS; the school re-entry certificate processed; and, the honest addition, a named follow-up if the first appointment is missed, because detachment, not relapse, is the default. Teaching points: the sentence-end cliff is the predictable crisis; the intervention is administrative as much as clinical (bookings, consents, transfers); and engagement must convert from compulsory to voluntary while the compulsion still holds it.

## 13. FAQ

**"Is my child a criminal?"**: Delinquency means acts that *could* result in conviction; most never do; the great majority of juvenile offending is handled pre-court, and most young offenders age out of it. The clinical question is not the label but the risk stack underneath.
**"Does he have a mental disorder, or is he just bad?"**: Both questions are usually wrong separately and right together: the risk factors for offending and for poor mental health overlap heavily, and the deeper a young person goes into the justice system, the more of both he carries. Treating the disorder is treating the behaviour.
**"Why does the doctor want to assess him in the observation home?"**: Because that is where the highest-need, least-served population gathers; screening at entry is epidemiology, not bureaucracy, and what it finds (depression, trauma, ADHD) changes both the care and the disposal.
**"What happens when he's released?"**: The danger point: statutory supervision ends, service links snap, and deterioration follows. The plan is made before release, appointments booked inside the window, consents signed, a named appointment-keeper.
**"Can anything actually prevent this?"**: Yes, at the population level: supervision-focused parenting programmes, school engagement, and early treatment of ADHD and abuse sequelae are the evidence-backed levers, aimed at the risk architecture, not at the individual sentence.
**"He's almost 18, what then?"**: The transition cliff: plan the handover to adult services before the youth system releases the case, or the detachment story gets an adult sequel.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**Q1.** The defining characteristic of "delinquency" is:
- (a) Conviction for a crime
- (b) Acts that could result in conviction, although most do not
- (c) Any childhood misbehaviour
- (d) Adult criminality beginning late

**Answer: (b).** The legal concept spans the conviction-capable range; the juvenile band runs from criminal-responsibility age to adult-court age, both varying by jurisdiction.

**Q2.** What proportion of juvenile disposals in England and Wales (2005–2006) was custodial?
- (a) One-third
- (b) Half
- (c) 3%
- (d) 25%

**Answer: (c).** 80% pre-court or first-tier, 17% community, 3% custodial, the majority of juvenile offending never reaches an institution, locating prevention in the community.

**Q3.** The chapter's central epidemiological statement about risk factors is:
- (a) Offending and mental health risks are unrelated
- (b) Risk factors for offending, poor mental health and substance misuse overlap substantially, and the number of assessed risk factors increases with depth in the youth justice system
- (c) Risk factors decline with system involvement
- (d) Only genetics matters

**Answer: (b).** The overlap principle: the deep-end population is the highest-need population, and system progression is itself a risk marker.

**Q4.** Adolescent mortality in the second half of the twentieth century:
- (a) Fell like all other age groups
- (b) Did not fall, accidents and self-harm leading
- (c) Rose tenfold
- (d) Was eliminated

**Answer: (b).** The unique adolescent pattern: the clinical basis for custody self-harm screening and mental-state assessment of every entrant.

**Q5.** The single strongest and most replicable predictor of juvenile offending, per the longitudinal studies:
- (a) Birth order
- (b) Poor parental supervision
- (c) Television
- (d) Height

**Answer: (b).** Parents who do not know where their children are; the finding that makes supervision-focused parenting programmes the highest-yield prevention.

**Q6.** A young offender stabilised during custody approaches his release date. Per the chapter's core service instruction, the priority is:
- (a) Waiting to see if he self-refers
- (b) Supporting access to mainstream and specialist services during supervision so that engagement survives sentence-end
- (c) Terminating all services on release
- (d) Referring only if he reoffends

**Answer: (b).** Sentence-end detachment predicts deterioration and further offending; the bookings, consents and transfers are completed while the statutory window still holds the young person in the system.
