# Excessive Sleepiness & Hypersomnias

## 1. In one line + overview

**Excessive daytime sleepiness is the symptom of a person who cannot stay awake when life requires it, and behind it stand four very different engines: not-enough-sleep (the commonest by far), broken sleep (chiefly sleep apnoea), a primary brain sleepiness-disorder (narcolepsy and its cousins), or a medical/psychiatric condition (depression, hypothyroid, medicines), each demanding a completely different treatment.**

"Sleepiness" is routinely trivialised in India, the dozing commuter, the lorry driver, the teenager asleep at the desk are cultural jokes rather than clinical signals. Medicine knows better: sleepiness at the wheel kills on highways (driver sleepiness is implicated in a meaningful share of Indian road accidents, among the world's largest road-fatality burdens); sleepiness in class ends careers; and sleepiness that has lasted years is a symptom with a differential, not a personality. The clinical spine of this note is the four-engine structure: (1) **insufficient sleep syndrome**; the person simply sleeps 5–6 hours and needs 8 (the commonest "hypersomnia" in India and everywhere); (2) **obstructive sleep apnoea**: sleep repeatedly shattered by airway collapse, producing unrefreshing nights and deadly days (the most under-diagnosed sleep disorder in the country); (3) **central hypersomnias**: narcolepsy (with or without the emotion-triggered collapse called cataplexy) and idiopathic hypersomnia, genuine brain-sleepiness disorders of the orexin system; (4) **secondary hypersomnia**: depression's oversleeping, hypothyroidism, medicines, Kleine-Levin's rare sleeping marathons. The clinician's job is engine-identification (the sleep-history, the sleepiness ladder, and the right tests) because the treatments diverge completely: more sleep, an airway pressure machine, wake-promoting medication, or treatment of the underlying condition.

## 2. Learning objectives

After this note you can:
- Distinguish sleepiness from fatigue (the examiner's first fork) and use the Epworth concept
- Run the four-engine differential of excessive daytime sleepiness
- Diagnose insufficient sleep syndrome (the epidemiological giant hiding in plain sight)
- Screen and diagnose obstructive sleep apnoea (STOP-BANG-style questions; the CPAP conversation)
- Recognise narcolepsy's tetrad (cataplexy, sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations, fragmented night sleep) and its orexin biology
- Explain the MSLT's logic (sleep latency + REM-onset naps) and orexin-C measurement
- Prescribe the wake-promoting tier (modafinil, armodafinil, methylphenidate) and sodium oxybate's role; treat cataplexy correctly

## 4. Epidemiology

**Global.** Insufficient sleep syndrome: enormous, a quarter or more of working adults sleep under the 7-hour line (an occupational and digital-era epidemic). OSA: the middle-aged-plus prevalence is high and climbing with obesity, roughly 1 in 6–10 adults in modern surveys meets at least moderate apnoea criteria, with most undiagnosed (the treatment gap is the story). Narcolepsy: rare and real, roughly 25–50 per 100,000; type 1 (with cataplexy, orexin-deficient) peaks in a bimodal onset around 15 and 35; mean delay to diagnosis is years (the child with cataplexy is commonly misread as lazy, clumsy, or psychiatric). Idiopathic hypersomnia: a fraction of narcolepsy's prevalence. Kleine-Levin: an orphan, roughly 1 per million, adolescent-male-heavy, episodes of days-to-weeks sleeping 15+ hours with behavioural changes.

**India.** The sleepiness burden is massive and almost unmapped at the epidemiological level. What is known and quotable: road-traffic injury is among India's largest causes of premature death, with driver sleepiness implicated in a meaningful share of highway crashes (Indian studies of truck drivers document high rates of sleep-disordered breathing and sleep-deprivation patterns, the public-health framing this note owes); OSA in India presents at LOWER average BMI than Western cohorts (the craniofacial phenotype contributes; a thin Indian woman with apnoea is a real entity, and the Western BMI-gate misleads); narcolepsy clinics at major Indian centres report long diagnostic delays with school-failure histories; and the night-shift economy (IT/BPO/healthcare/gig) manufactures circadian sleepiness at industrial scale.

## 5. Causes & risk factors

**Engine 1, Insufficient sleep (the commonest):**
- Digital-evening displacement (reels, gaming, match nights), two-job economies, board-year study patterns, newborns, the "I sleep 5 and function" self-narrative
- The paradox: presenting as "I sleep whenever I sit", the person DOZES opportunistically but never asks for sleep at night's wrong times

**Engine 2, Broken sleep (OSA-centred):**
- Obesity, male sex, age 40+, post-menopausal women, craniofacial narrowing, enlarged tonsils/adenoids (children, the chubby snoring child with ADHD-look-alike behaviour), sedatives/alcohol at night, nasal obstruction, hypothyroidism, acromegaly
- Indian risk reality: lower-BMI phenotypes, dense body-fat patterning, and the under-recognised thin-patient apnoea

**Engine 3, Central hypersomnias (brain-sleepiness):**
- Narcolepsy type 1: autoimmune destruction of orexin neurons (in genetically susceptible people, HLA-DQB1*06:02 association; post-infectious triggers including influenza, post-H1N1-era data; the Pandemrix-vaccine signal as the famous epidemiological chapter)
- Narcolepsy type 2 and idiopathic hypersomnia: mechanisms incompletely understood
- Kleine-Levin: post-infectious/autoimmune hypotheses; adolescent males

**Engine 4, Secondary sleepiness:**
- Depression (atypical, bipolar-depression oversleeping), hypothyroidism, anaemia, poorly controlled diabetes, sedating medicines (antihistamines, benzodiazepines, opioids, gabapentinoids, some antidepressants), chronic liver/kidney disease, post-TBI sleepiness, epilepsy/anti-epileptics, Parkinson's disease

## 6. What happens in the mind/brain

**Story 1: the orexin switchboard.** Deep in the hypothalamus, ~70,000 orexin neurons act as the brain's wakefulness switchboard; the "ON and HOLD IT" circuit that keeps a person stably awake through a whole day. In narcolepsy type 1, an autoimmune attack destroys this switchboard. The wake-hold fails (sleep invades the day in irresistible naps), sleep's architecture loses its gating (REM leaks into wakefulness: the dream-imagery of sleep onset, hypnagogic hallucinations, and REM's muscle-paralysis arriving awake, sleep paralysis), and, in the disorder's signature, STRONG EMOTION trips the REM-atonia circuit: laughter or sudden surprise switches the body's muscles OFF for seconds (cataplexy, the knees buckle, the jaw drops, the person crumples fully CONSCIOUS). The treatments mirror the biology: wake-promoting agents for the day's hold, and REM-suppressing/sodium oxybate strategies for the leaks.

**Story 2: the airway and the night's demolition.** In deep sleep, the airway's muscle tone falls; in the apnoea-prone throat (narrowed, padded, or structurally crowded), it collapses. Blood oxygen dips, carbon dioxide rises, and the brain, a suffocating sentinel, rouses just enough to re-open the throat: a micro-arousal. This repeats tens-to-hundreds of times nightly: the sleeper "slept" 8 hours and got perhaps half of the architecture (see Sleep Basics note), which is why the morning report is "unrefreshed" and the day is sleepy, and why decades of this hammer the heart (hypertension, stroke, arrhythmia risk). The CPAP machine is an air-splint holding the airway open, ungainly, effective, and in India, needing a dedicated selling conversation.

**Story 3: the debt collector.** Insufficient-sleep syndrome is not a disorder at all in the disease sense: it is arithmetic. A person who needs 8 hours, sleeps 5.5, accumulates a weekly debt of 17 hours, and the debt collector calls at the seminar desk, the steering wheel, the afternoon chair. The tell-tale that separates it from the central hypersomnias: on holidays and weekends, the person sleeps long and wakes restored (the central-hypersomnia patient sleeps long and wakes UNrestored; the apnoea patient's long sleep never refreshes). The treatment, actually sleeping the required hours, is famously harder to prescribe than to write.

## 7. Symptoms

**The symptom itself, sleepiness, graded:**
- Mild: dozes in passive situations (bus, afternoon lecture, TV)
- Moderate: dozes in active-but-seated situations (meetings, cinema, as a passenger)
- Severe: dozes in active situations (mid-conversation, mid-meal, while driving), the seizure-level red flag
- Distinguish from FATIGUE: sleepiness = "I could fall asleep now"; fatigue = "I am exhausted but could not nap." The differential fork (sleepiness→sleep medicine; fatigue→medical/psychiatric workup)

**Engine-specific signatures:**
- **Insufficient sleep**: weekend-catch-up pattern, caffeine escalation, "fine once I sleep"
- **OSA**: loud snoring, witnessed pauses/gasps, unrefreshing sleep, nocturia, morning dry-mouth/throat, night sweats, morning headaches, weight gain, the hypertensive-obese (or deceptively thin) frame; in CHILDREN: snoring, enlarged tonsils, hyperactive-ADHD-lookalike behaviour, poor school performance, bedwetting
- **Narcolepsy**: daytime naps that REFRESH (a distinctive feature), cataplexy (knees/jaw/face with laughter, question asked specifically, "when you laugh hard or get angry suddenly, does anything happen to your muscles?"), sleep paralysis (the awake-but-paralysed terror at sleep boundaries), hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations (vivid dream-imagery at sleep onset/offset), fragmented night sleep (the paradox: sleepy by day, broken by night), automatic behaviours (doing things with no memory of the middle), weight gain at onset, late-onset presentation mimicking psychosis when hallucinations are misreported
- **Idiopathic hypersomnia**: long unrefreshing sleep, sleep drunkenness (slow, confused, irritable waking; "the alarm is a violence"), hard naps that don't refresh
- **Depression's hypersomnia**: morning-anchored oversleeping, anhedonia-flavoured energy absence, mood architecture
- **Kleine-Levin**: episodic, days-to-weeks of 15–20-hour sleep, dreamy derealisation, megaphagia, hypersexuality/disinhibition, then weeks-to-months of normalcy

## 8. Diagnosis

**The logic.** Excessive sleepiness + adequate documented opportunity = the central/medical engines. Excessive sleepiness + 5–6-hour nights = insufficient-sleep syndrome (diagnosed by the two-week extended diary and the catch-up question). OSA = the clinical cluster confirmed by polysomnography (the apnoea-hypopnea index; home sleep-apnoea testing where appropriate). Narcolepsy type 1 = EDS + cataplexy (or orexin-C deficiency) + MSLT confirmation; type 2 = EDS + MSLT without cataplexy/orexin deficiency; idiopathic hypersomnia = long unrefreshing sleep + MSLT without REM-intrusion criteria.

**The assessment craft:**
- The two-week EXTENDED sleep diary (all sleep, all naps, weekends, the fork between engines)
- The sleepiness grade and the driving question directly ("have you ever nodded off at the wheel, even for a second?")
- The snoring/witnessed-pauses question to the PARTNER (the patient never witnessed their own pauses; interview the spouse)
- The cataplexy question in the exact words above, patients hide cataplexy for years out of fear of appearing dramatic or "mad"; it is asked in EVERY sleepy person
- The hypothyroid/depression/medicine inventory
- Instruments: Epworth Sleepiness Scale (the eight-scenario self-rating, named, not reproduced); the STOP-BANG-style OSA screen (named); MSLT (the multiple sleep-latency test: mean latency ≤ 8 minutes with ≥ 2 REM-onset naps); polysomnography for apnoea/architecture; CSF orexin (type-1 confirmation in complex cases)

### Differential table

| Sleepy presentation | The engine and its tell |
|---|---|
| Dozing commuter, weekend catch-up restores | Insufficient sleep syndrome (diary proves) |
| Snorer, unrefreshed, hypertension, witnessed pauses | OSA (partner interview + sleep study) |
| Refreshing naps + laughter-collapse | Narcolepsy type 1 (cataplexy is pathognomonic) |
| Long sleep + drunken waking, no REM-signs | Idiopathic hypersomnia |
| Oversleeping + mood, anhedonia | Depression (screen alongside) |
| Sleepy + fatigue + cold intolerance, weight gain | Hypothyroidism (TSH) |
| Sleepy on antihistamines/benzos/opioids/gabapentinoids | Medication effect (the inventory) |
| Episodic marathons in a teenage boy | Kleine-Levin (episodic pattern) |
| Sleepy + recent head injury | Post-TBI hypersomnia |
| Child snoring, hyperactive, wetting bed | Paediatric OSA (tonsils; NOT ADHD-lookalike only) |

## 9. Management

**By engine, the treatments diverge completely:**

**Insufficient sleep syndrome:** prescribe the sleep, a structured schedule expansion (30 minutes earlier per fortnight), the digital-evening curfew, the bedroom-environment fixes (see the Insomnia note's India tier), and caffeine timing. Occupational advocacy where the job itself forbids the hours (night-shift protocols below).

**Obstructive sleep apnoea:**
- Weight management where weight contributes (5–10% loss moves indices meaningfully; see the Obesity/DM-note logic; sleep itself improves with weight)
- **CPAP** (continuous positive airway pressure): the splint-machine, the gold standard; the Indian conversation is about the first month (mask-fitting, humidity, the claustrophobic week, the partner's role, cost and power realities); adherence support determines everything (first-month contact, mask alternatives, the heated-humidifier tier)
- Alternatives: mandibular-advancement devices (dental) for mild-moderate/CPAP-refusers; positional therapy for position-dependent apnoea; surgery (adenotonsillectomy in children; selected adult procedures)
- The comorbidity management: hypertension, glucose, lipids; apnoea treatment IS cardiovascular medicine

**Narcolepsy:**
- Scheduled naps (two 15–20-minute planned naps anchor many patients' days, free, underused)
- **Wake-promoting tier**: modafinil/armodafinil 100–400 mg (the first-line, favourable safety profile; the rash warning and the interaction counsel); methylphenidate and amphetamine-class as second tier (tachycardia, BP, appetite, dependence monitoring)
- **Sodium oxybate**: the night-architecture medicine; consolidates night sleep AND treats cataplexy; highly regulated (misuse potential), expensive, specialist-only: the referral-tier answer
- **Cataplexy treatment**: venlafaxine, clomipramine, or fluoxetine (the REM-suppressing antidepressants, effective for cataplexy specifically at low doses); sodium oxybate for severe cases
- Safety counselling: driving rules (treated-and-stable = the goal; untreated cataplexy+sleepiness = the hazard), occupation choices, school accommodations (the scheduled nap in the nurse's room changes school trajectories)

**Idiopathic hypersomnia:** modafinil/armodafinil; the scheduling adaptations; honest counsel that the waking is the hard part (the alarm-protocol planning)

**Kleine-Levin:** watchful waiting with episode care (safety, hydration, school/work coverage); the specialist-tier options (lithium case-reports/series for frequent relapsers)

**Secondary engines:** treat the driver, thyroid replacement, depression's treatment, the medicine-substitution audit (morning-ify the sedatives, swap the antihistamine, review the opioid/gabapentinoid load)

*India note (approx 2026): modafinil 200 mg ≈ ₹8–20/tablet (₹400–1,000/month depending on schedule; armodafinil similar tier, both under varied regulatory enforcement; the methylphenidate route goes through the Ritalin-scarcity/Narcotics pipeline and is harder); venlafaxine 37.5–75 mg ≈ ₹100–200/month for cataplexy; CPAP machines ₹25,000–60,000 one-time (₹45,000-70,000 with humidifier/auto-titrating) with mask replacements ₹2,000–5,000/year; PM-JAY's coverage for OSA hardware is limited; home sleep-apnoea testing is emerging at ₹3,000–8,000, full polysomnography ₹5,000–15,000 in private metro labs (medical colleges lower). The Indian narcolepsy reality: diagnostic delay averages years; the school desk-sleeping child collects punishments before diagnosis; the first-cataplexy-question often happens at an Indian clinic after a decade of "laziness" labels. The public-health lever: driver-sleepiness screening for commercial licences (the snoring-BMI-BP triad at transport-office medicals) is a policy worth having and mostly absent.*

## 10. Indian practice

- **The lorry-driver consultation is a life-or-death clinic.** The sleepy commercial driver (long-haul routes, night driving, amphetamine-and-chai self-medication culture in some corridors) needs: the sleep history (apnoea risk, hours actually slept), the honest discussion of sleep-inertia crash risk, the referral for apnoea testing where indicated, and the de-escalation of the "chewing-tobacco-to-stay-awake" tier. Transport-company medical reviews are the systematic entry-point; write sleepiness into the form even if the form does not ask.
- **The desk-sleeping adolescent.** The teenage sleeper is read as lazy (school), anaemic (parents), or depressed (clinics), while the real engines are: chronic 5–6-hour nights (device displacement + board-year fear), delayed phase (see the Sleep Basics note's adolescent shift), or narcolepsy with cataplexy hidden in the "clumsy" child who buckles when laughing. The cataplexy question belongs in every paediatric sleep consult; the school-nurse nap-room prescription changes report cards.
- **Thin-patient apnoea.** Indian OSA includes the low-BMI craniofacial phenotype, the slim woman with refractory hypertension, morning headaches, and unrefreshing sleep; the Western obesity-gate misleads; when the snore-and-pause cluster exists, test regardless of the mirror.
- **The BPO/night-shift tier.** Circadian sleepiness (not apnoea, not narcolepsy), the sunglasses-commute, black-out curtain, anchor-sleep protocol from the Insomnia note applies; the occupational-health conversation (shift-rotation direction, forward rotations) beats individual medicine.
- **"Nervous weakness" and sleepiness mislabels.** Idiopathic hypersomnia and narcolepsy patients accumulate anaemia/thyroid/tonic treatments for years before the MSLT; the two-week diary and the Epworth concept are the GP-level tools that route correctly.
- **Sodium oxybate and the regulation reality.** Where cataplexy is severe, the Indian pathway usually manages with venlafaxine/clomipramine + modafinil; oxybate access is via specialist programmes, the honest answer to families asking for "the best treatment abroad".
- **The CPAP adherence programme.** The machine's failure mode is the first month, the Indian adaptation: the technician-call in week 1, the mask-trial menu, the humidity setting for Indian summers, and the family's role in the morning report ("did he snore with it on?"). A CPAP on the shelf is a cardiovascular risk factor of its own kind, schedule the adherence review like an insulin review.

## 11. Clinical cases (de-identified)

**Case 1: the laughter that dropped him.** A 22-year-old M.Com student in Indore was brought by his brother for "weakness attacks": at family jokes, his head would nod and knees would dip for a few seconds, fully awake, and in one instance he had slid against a wall laughing at a wedding video. For six years he had been the family's "sleepy lazy boy": sleeping in classes, napping after school "for hours", collecting school punishments; nights were broken with vivid dream-onset images he never reported (fear of being thought mad). The laughter-collapse was hidden for years, the family first suspected a "nerve disease" when he fell at a function. Assessment: EDS with refreshing naps + video-documented laughter-triggered atonia (cataplexy); sleep paralysis reported on direct question; MSLT showed mean latency 4 minutes with REM-onset in 3/4 naps, narcolepsy type 1 confirmed; CSF orexin at the reference centre measured in the deficient range. Management: the diagnosis-delivery session ("this has a name, a biology, and a treatment; you were never lazy"); scheduled two naps built into his day (the college's first-ever nurse-room nap permission, arranged by letter); modafinil 200 mg each morning; venlafaxine 37.5 mg for cataplexy (attacks reduced from daily to rare); driving counsel deferred until stability (the six-month treated mark). At 1 year: completing his course, one cataplexy relapse-month after a fever (infectious-trigger flare counsel), the brother reporting "he is a different human". Teaching points: (1) cataplexy is pathognomonic and almost never volunteered; ask the laughter-muscle question in every sleepy person; (2) the "lazy child" history is narcolepsy's camouflage until the day it isn't; (3) refreshing naps + broken nights is the narcolepsy signature inside the diary; (4) school/college accommodations (nap permission) are as therapeutic as modafinil.

**Case 2: the "well-slept" woman who never woke refreshed.** A 46-year-old Ludhiana schoolteacher, BMI 26 (deceptive for the apnoea stereotype), presented via a hypertension clinic for "tiredness and morning headaches"; she had snored "for years" per her husband, with witnessed pauses and a nocturia pattern; her sleepiness was moderate (dozing at staff meetings). Depression screens negative; TSH normal. Home sleep-apnoea testing: AHI in the moderate range, OSA confirmed (the thin-phenotype point made explicit in the file). Management: the CPAP conversation with the first-month programme (technician week-1 home visit, three mask options trialled, humidity set for the summer, husband assigned the "does she snore with it?" morning report); weight-counselling modest (5 kg target); the hypertension co-management flagged to the physician. At 3 months: nightly 6.5-hour CPAP use (the adherence-review numbers), morning headaches gone, sleepiness scores halved, antihypertensive load under review. Teaching points: (1) the partner's interview is the diagnostic instrument in apnoea; the patient has never seen herself pause; (2) Indian apnoea lives at lower BMIs, the phenotype gate misleads; (3) CPAP is a programme, not a purchase, the first month's engineering decides the decade.

## 13. FAQ: asked the way patients and families ask it

**"He sleeps anywhere: bus, chair, function. Lucky fellow, no?"** Sleeping the moment you sit is not luck; it is a symptom. If the nights are short, the fix is hours; if the nights are long and he still dozes, we look for the broken-sleep and brain-sleepiness causes, and if it is happening while driving, it is an emergency, not a joke.

**"I feel tired all the time but I cannot nap. Is that the same thing?"** No: that is fatigue rather than sleepiness, and it points us toward different doors: thyroid, anaemia, depression, medicines. The nap test ("if you lay down right now, could you sleep?") separates the two in ten seconds.

**"My son is 16 and sleeps at every opportunity. Teenagers are like this, no?"** Partly biology (the teenage clock shifts late), but check the arithmetic: how many hours on school nights versus weekends? If school nights run 5–6 hours, the treatment is sleep, not scolding. And if he sleeps enough and STILL dozes (especially if his muscles dip when he laughs hard) bring him in: that specific sign has a name and a treatment.

**"The doctor says my husband stops breathing at night. Is that dangerous?"** Yes: that is sleep apnoea, and it matters beyond the snoring: hundreds of nightly dips in oxygen strain the heart and brain over years, feeding blood pressure and stroke risk. The treatment (a small machine that holds his airway open with air pressure) is ungainly and works. Our job is to make the first month of it succeed.

**"She is thin. How can she have sleep apnoea?"** The Western picture of the overweight snorer misleads here: Indian apnoea often lives in the throat's structure, not just in weight. Thin patients snore, pause, and carry the same heart risks. The test follows the snore-and-pause cluster, not the mirror.

**"Is narcolepsy a mental illness?"** It is a brain-sleepiness disorder with an identified chemistry (the loss of a wake-keeping signal), not a mental illness, not laziness, and not dangerous to others once treated. It is lifelong, manageable with medicine and nap-scheduling, and famous people have carried it through full careers.

**"When he laughs hard, he goes weak and slides down. That is a 'nervous' problem, they said."** That weakness-with-laughter has a specific name, cataplexy, and it is the signature of narcolepsy. It is fully conscious, seconds-long, and treatable; it is not epilepsy, not fainting, and not drama. It is also the answer most patients have been hiding for years, so thank him for telling you.

**"Will the wake-promoting medicines make him dependent or jittery?"** The first-line (modafinil/armodafinil) is comparatively gentle, headache and nausea in the early days, blood-pressure checks, a rare rash warning. The stronger stimulants exist as backup with more monitoring. Dependence is far less the story than it is with older stimulants; the goal is a person awake, not a person wound.

**"Can he drive with this condition?"** The honest answer: not while untreated and unstable; sleepy-and-cataplectic driving is a genuine hazard; yes, once treated and stable, with the doctor's documented review. This is a safety rule, not a punishment, and we will take it seriously with you.

**"The CPAP machine is ugly, costly, and the power fails. What then?"** All three are real Indian objections. The machine's benefits justify the ugliness (marriages survive masks better than snoring); inverter/UPS power backup is a one-time cost many households already own; and the auto-titrating travel-tier exists. What does not survive is untreated decades of nightly suffocation; the machine is the cheaper of the two roads.

## 14. Exam & student lens

**The four-engine differential is the answer skeleton for any EDS question:** insufficient sleep / broken sleep (OSA) / central hypersomnias / secondary. Say the engines before the names: examiners hear structure.

**Mnemonic, narcolepsy tetrad "CHSF" (or the classic "tetrad"): Cataplexy, Hypnagogic hallucinations, Sleep paralysis, Fragmented night sleep**, plus EDS as the floor. Cataplexy = pathognomonic; orexin deficiency = the biology; MSLT latency ≤ 8 min with ≥ 2 SOREMPs = the test.

**High-yield facts:**
- Sleepiness vs fatigue: the fork question, first line of every answer
- Insufficient-sleep syndrome is the commonest EDS cause (weekend catch-up restores = the tell)
- OSA in India: lower-BMI phenotype, the craniofacial contribution
- STOP-BANG-style screening; partner interview for witnessed apnoea
- Epworth Sleepiness Scale: named only
- Cataplexy treated by REM-suppressing antidepressants (venlafaxine/clomipramine) + sodium oxybate
- Modafinil = first-line wake-promoter; sodium oxybate = specialist-tier (night architecture + cataplexy)
- HLA-DQB1*06:02 association; autoimmune aetiology; post-H1N1/Pandemrix chapter
- Kleine-Levin: episodic, adolescent males, sleep + derealisation + megaphagia/hypersexuality
- Paediatric OSA mimics ADHD (tonsils, snoring, hyperactivity), the child-trap
- EDS at the wheel = the public-health answer for Indian-context marks

**Classic traps:**
1. Labelling the desk-sleeping teenager (or the narcoleptic adult) "lazy" for years
2. Missing cataplexy because the question was never asked in laughter-terms
3. Gate-keeping apnoea testing behind the BMI mirror
4. Confusing depression's fatigue with EDS, or missing depression's oversleeping
5. Missing the sedating-medicine layer (the antihistamine/benzo/gabapentinoid inventory)
6. Treating shift-workers' circadian sleepiness with hypnotics-at-8-a.m. logic

**Indian exam corner:** "narcolepsy, clinical features and management" (the evergreen), "obstructive sleep apnoea, screening and treatment", "causes of excessive daytime sleepiness", "MSLT, what and when". Indian-context marks: driver sleepiness and road safety, the thin-phenotype apnoea, the school-nap accommodation as therapy, CPAP adherence programmes.

## 16. Self-test MCQs

**1. The commonest cause of excessive daytime sleepiness is:**
A. Narcolepsy
B. Behaviourally induced insufficient sleep
C. Kleine-Levin syndrome
D. Idiopathic hypersomnia
**Answer: B.** The arithmetic giant: the two-week diary with weekend catch-up is the proof.

**2. The symptom pathognomonic of narcolepsy type 1:**
A. Morning headache
B. Cataplexy (emotion-triggered muscle atonia with preserved consciousness)
C. Snoring
D. Bedwetting
**Answer: B.** Ask the laughter-muscle question in every sleepy person; cataplexy is volunteered almost never.

**3. MSLT findings defining narcolepsy:**
A. Latency > 20 minutes with no REM
B. Mean latency ≤ 8 minutes with ≥ 2 REM-onset naps
C. Only leg movements counted
D. Night-time apnoea count
**Answer: B.** Short latency + REM-intrusion into naps = the objective signature.

**4. A thin 44-year-old woman with snoring, witnessed pauses and refractory hypertension:**
A. Cannot have OSA (low BMI)
B. Should be tested: Indian apnoea includes low-BMI craniofacial phenotypes
C. Needs an SSRI
D. Needs modafinil first
**Answer: B.** The BMI-gate misleads in India: the snore-pause cluster orders the study.

**5. Cataplexy is specifically treated with:**
A. Modafinil
B. REM-suppressing antidepressants (e.g., venlafaxine, clomipramine) and sodium oxybate
C. CPAP
D. Melatonin
**Answer: B.** The wake-promoters treat sleepiness; the cataplexy-lexicon is different.

**6. Narcolepsy type 1's underlying biology:**
A. Dopamine excess
B. Loss of hypothalamic orexin/hypocretin neurons (autoimmune, HLA-associated)
C. Serotonin deficiency
D. Thyroid dysfunction
**Answer: B.** The orexin switchboard destruction: CSF orexin measurement confirms.

**7. In narcolepsy management, scheduled daytime naps:**
A. Worsen the disorder
B. Are a legitimate treatment component (refreshing brief naps)
C. Are forbidden
D. Replace medicines entirely
**Answer: B.** Contrast with insomnia, where naps spend the pressure, in narcolepsy the brief planned nap anchors the day.

**8. The sleepy child with snoring, big tonsils, hyperactivity and bedwetting most likely has:**
A. ADHD
B. Paediatric obstructive sleep apnoea masquerading as ADHD
C. Narcolepsy
D. Depression
**Answer: B.** The classic paediatric trap: treat the airway, reconsider the label.
